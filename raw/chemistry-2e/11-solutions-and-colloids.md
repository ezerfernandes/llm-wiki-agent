## 11.1 The Dissolution Process

> Source: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/11-1-the-dissolution-process

### Learning Objectives
- Describe basic properties of solutions and their formation
- Predict whether a given mixture will yield a solution based on molecular properties
- Explain why some solutions produce or absorb heat during formation

### Solutions: Definitions and Components
A **solution** is a "homogeneous mixture of two or more substances." In most solutions, one component is present in significantly greater concentration and is called the **solvent**, while components at lesser concentrations are called **solutes**. The solutes are individually dispersed throughout the solution when dissolved.

**Sugar in water:** Sucrose (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁), a covalent solid, dissolves as:

C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁(s) → C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁(aq)

The "aq" subscript signifies an aqueous solution where sucrose molecules are solutes dispersed throughout water. Despite being heavier than water molecules, dissolved sugar remains uniformly distributed and does not settle over time.

**Potassium dichromate in water:** K₂Cr₂O₇ is an ionic compound of colorless K⁺ ions and orange Cr₂O₇²⁻ ions. It dissolves and dissociates:

K₂Cr₂O₇(s) → 2K⁺(aq) + Cr₂O₇²⁻(aq)

The ions remain individually dispersed among the solvent (water) molecules.

### Characteristics of Solutions
- **Homogeneity** — uniform composition throughout at all points after mixing.
- **Physical state** — typically matches that of the solvent.
- **Molecular dispersion** — components dispersed on a molecular scale as separated solute particles (molecules, atoms, and/or ions), each closely surrounded by solvent species.
- **Non-settling** — dissolved solute will not settle out or separate from the solvent.
- **Variable composition** — concentrations can be varied continuously within limits set by solubility.

### Types of Solutions
Solutions are not limited to aqueous systems. "Almost any gas, liquid, or solid can act as a solvent."

| Solution | Solute | Solvent |
|----------|--------|---------|
| Air | O₂(g) | N₂(g) |
| Soft drinks | CO₂(g) | H₂O(l) |
| Hydrogen in palladium | H₂(g) | Pd(s) |
| Rubbing alcohol | H₂O(l) | C₃H₈O(l) (2-propanol) |
| Saltwater | NaCl(s) | H₂O(l) |
| Brass | Zn(s) | Cu(s) |

Many alloys are solid solutions of one metal dissolved in another (US five-cent coins contain nickel dissolved in copper); air is a gaseous solution, a homogeneous mixture of nitrogen, oxygen, and several other gases.

### Formation of Solutions: Spontaneity and Thermodynamics
Solution formation is a **spontaneous process** — "a process that occurs under specified conditions without the requirement of energy from some external source." Stirring may speed dissolution but is not necessary.

Two criteria **favor** (but do not guarantee) spontaneous solution formation:
1. **Decrease in internal energy** — an exothermic change, releasing heat.
2. **Increased dispersal of matter** — an increase in entropy of the system.

During dissolution, an internal energy change often (but not always) occurs as heat is absorbed or evolved. However, an increase in matter dispersal **always** results when a solution forms.

### Ideal Solutions
When "the strengths of the intermolecular forces of attraction between solute and solvent species in a solution are no different than those present in the separated components, the solution is formed with no accompanying energy change." Such a solution is an **ideal solution**.

- A mixture of ideal gases (e.g., helium and argon) exemplifies ideal solutions: the gas entities experience no significant intermolecular attractions and mix spontaneously by diffusion. Each gas now occupies a volume twice as large as before, demonstrating increased matter dispersal.
- Ideal solutions also form when structurally similar liquids mix: methanol (CH₃OH) + ethanol (C₂H₅OH); pentane (C₅H₁₂) + hexane (C₆H₁₄). These form with no significant change in energy because the attractive forces between like and unlike molecules are essentially the same.
- This shows that increased matter dispersal **alone** can drive spontaneous solution formation.

### Intermolecular Forces and the Dissolution Process
Three types of intermolecular attractive force are relevant:
1. **Solute-solute attractions** — between solute particles.
2. **Solvent-solvent attractions** — between solvent molecules.
3. **Solute-solvent attractions** — solvation.

**Stepwise process model** (a hypothetical decomposition of dissolution):
- **Step 1 (Endothermic):** energy consumed to overcome solute-solute attractions, separating solute particles.
- **Step 2 (Endothermic):** energy consumed to overcome solvent-solvent attractions, expanding the solvent to accommodate solute.
- **Step 3 (Exothermic):** solute-solvent attractions established via solvation, releasing energy.

"The relative magnitudes of the energy changes associated with these stepwise processes determine whether the dissolution process overall will release or absorb energy."

**Ionic compounds in water:** "Formation of the solution requires the electrostatic forces between the cations and anions of the compound (solute–solute) be overcome completely as attractive forces are established between these ions and water molecules (solute–solvent)." Hydrogen bonding between a fraction of the water molecules must also be overcome.
- If solute electrostatic forces ≫ solvation forces: dissolution is significantly **endothermic** and the compound may not dissolve appreciably (e.g., calcium carbonate, a major coral-reef component).
- If solvation forces ≫ electrostatic forces: dissolution is significantly **exothermic** and the compound may be highly soluble (e.g., sodium hydroxide).

### Exothermic vs. Endothermic Dissolution
"Spontaneous solution formation is favored, but not guaranteed, by exothermic dissolution processes. While many soluble compounds do, indeed, dissolve with the release of heat, some dissolve endothermically."

**Endothermic example — ammonium nitrate:** NH₄NO₃ dissolves endothermically and is used in instant cold packs. When the inner water bag breaks, NH₄NO₃ dissolves, absorbing heat from the surroundings (the injured area) and providing a cold compress. Such endothermic dissolutions "require a greater energy input to separate the solute species than is recovered when the solutes are solvated, but they are spontaneous nonetheless due to the increase in disorder that accompanies formation of the solution."

### Solutions That Do Not Form
"In some cases, however, the relative magnitudes of intermolecular forces of attraction between solute and solvent species may prevent dissolution." When the energy to separate solute and solvent species is much greater than the energy released by solvation, forming a homogeneous solution is thermodynamically unfavorable.

## 11.2 Electrolytes

> Source: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/11-2-electrolytes

### Learning Objectives
- Define and give examples of electrolytes
- Distinguish between the physical and chemical changes that accompany dissolution of ionic and covalent electrolytes
- Relate electrolyte strength to solute-solvent attractive forces

### Electrolytes and Nonelectrolytes
When certain substances dissolve in water they undergo physical or chemical changes that produce ions in solution — these are **electrolytes**. Substances that do not yield ions when dissolved are **nonelectrolytes**.

Classification by completeness of the ion-producing process:
- **Strong electrolyte:** the process that generates ions is essentially 100% efficient (all dissolved compound yields ions).
- **Weak electrolyte:** only a relatively small fraction of the dissolved substance undergoes the ion-producing process.

### Electrical Conductance
Substances may be identified as strong, weak, or nonelectrolytes by measuring electrical conductance of an aqueous solution. "To conduct electricity, a substance must contain freely mobile, charged species." In metallic wires, electrons are the mobile charges. In solutions, dissolved ions carry the current, and conductivity increases as ion concentration increases. Conductivity can be assessed quantitatively (current flow) or qualitatively (brightness of a light bulb in the circuit). Nonelectrolyte solutions (e.g., ethanol) contain no ions and do not conduct.

### Ionic Electrolytes
Water and other polar molecules are attracted to ions. "The electrostatic attraction between an ion and a molecule with a dipole is called an ion-dipole attraction."

**Dissociation and hydration:** When ionic compounds dissolve, the ions in the solid separate and disperse uniformly because water molecules surround and solvate the ions, reducing the strong electrostatic forces between them. This physical change is **dissociation**. Under most conditions ionic compounds dissociate nearly completely and so are **strong electrolytes** — even sparingly soluble ionic compounds are strong electrolytes, because the small amount that does dissolve dissociates completely.

**Ion-dipole interactions in KCl dissolution:** Ion-dipole forces attract the positive (hydrogen) end of polar water molecules to the negative Cl⁻ ions and the negative (oxygen) ends to the positive K⁺ ions. Water molecules surround individual K⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, reducing the interionic forces and letting them move off into solution as solvated (hydrated) ions, transitioning from fixed positions in the solid to widely dispersed, solvated ions in solution.

### Covalent Electrolytes
Pure water is an extremely poor conductor because it is only very slightly ionized — only about two of every 1 billion molecules ionize at 25 °C. Water self-ionizes when one molecule gives a proton (H⁺) to another, yielding hydronium and hydroxide ions:

H₂O(l) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

**Chemical ionization:** Some covalent compounds conduct because solute molecules react chemically with the solvent to produce ions. Pure hydrogen chloride is a gas of covalent HCl molecules containing no ions, yet aqueous HCl is a very good conductor. Because HCl is an acid, its molecules react with water, transferring H⁺ to form hydronium (H₃O⁺) and chloride (Cl⁻):

H₂O + HCl → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻

This reaction is essentially 100% complete for HCl (a strong acid and therefore a strong electrolyte).

**Weak acids and bases** react only partially and generate relatively low concentrations of ions when dissolved — they are **weak electrolytes**.

## 11.3 Solubility

> Source: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/11-3-solubility

### Definition and Equilibrium
**Solubility** is "the maximum concentration that may be achieved under given conditions when the dissolution process is at equilibrium."

Classification by solute concentration:
- **Saturated solution:** solute concentration equals its solubility.
- **Unsaturated solution:** solute concentration is less than its solubility.
- **Dilute solution:** relatively low concentration of solute.
- **Concentrated solution:** relatively high concentration of solute.
- **Supersaturated solution:** solute concentration exceeds its solubility; a nonequilibrium state that is relatively stable but can precipitate when disturbed.

### Solutions of Gases in Liquids
**Intermolecular forces:** Gaseous solutes have no solute-solute interactions, so only solute-solvent interactions determine solubility. Oxygen is ~3× more soluble in water than helium (greater dispersion forces with larger O₂), but ~100× less soluble than chloroform (which has dipole-dipole attraction with polar water).

**Temperature effect:** Gas solubility typically **decreases** as temperature increases. This is responsible for thermal-pollution impacts: decreased dissolved oxygen from elevated water temperature can cause large-scale fish kills.

**Pressure effect:** Gas solubility increases as the partial pressure of the gas increases. Carbonated beverages are saturated with CO₂ at high pressure; opening the container reduces pressure, the beverage becomes supersaturated, and CO₂ escapes as bubbles.

**Henry's law.** For many gaseous solutes, solubility is proportional to partial pressure:

**Cg = k·Pg**

- **Cg** = concentration of dissolved gas (mol/L)
- **k** = Henry's law constant (depends on solute identity, solvent identity, and temperature)
- **Pg** = partial pressure of the gas (atm or kPa)

Statement: "The quantity of an ideal gas that dissolves in a definite volume of liquid is directly proportional to the pressure of the gas."

*Worked example:* At 20 °C, O₂ concentration in water exposed to O₂ at 101.3 kPa is 1.38 × 10⁻³ mol/L.
- Find k: k = Cg/Pg = (1.38 × 10⁻³ mol/L)/(101.3 kPa) = 1.36 × 10⁻⁵ mol/(L·kPa).
- Solubility at 20.7 kPa: Cg = k·Pg = (1.36 × 10⁻⁵ mol/(L·kPa))(20.7 kPa) = 2.82 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L.

**Deviations from Henry's law:** Chemical reactions between solute and solvent cause deviations. Ammonia solubility rises faster with pressure than predicted because NH₃ reacts with water: NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻.

**Supersaturated gas solutions:** A gas solution prepared cold or at high pressure may become supersaturated when warmed or depressurized. Historical example: the 1986 Lake Nyos, Cameroon disaster — CO₂ became supersaturated from volcanic activity at depth; warming caused the water to rise and release tremendous CO₂ that flowed down the valley and suffocated over 1,700 people and animals.

**Decompression sickness ("the bends"):** Ambient pressure rises ~1 atm per 10 m of depth, so per Henry's law gases dissolve in blood at higher concentrations at depth. Rapid ascent makes dissolved gases less soluble, forming bubbles in blood that cause rashes, joint pain, paralysis, or death. Prevention: ascend slowly (10–20 m/min) or make decompression stops. Treatment: hyperbaric oxygen therapy in pressurized recompression chambers.

### Solutions of Liquids in Liquids
- **Miscible liquids:** mix in any proportion (infinite mutual solubility), e.g., ethanol, sulfuric acid, ethylene glycol with water; two-cycle motor oil with gasoline. Typically similar polarities.
- **Immiscible liquids:** do not mix appreciably, forming separate layers, e.g., gasoline, oil, benzene, carbon tetrachloride with water. Weak attractions between polar water and nonpolar liquids cannot overcome water's strong hydrogen bonding.
- **Partially miscible liquids:** moderate mutual solubility, forming two layers, e.g., bromine and water (upper = water saturated with bromine; lower = bromine saturated with water). Immiscibility vs miscibility is a matter of degree, not absolute.

**"Like dissolves like":** polar molecules dissolve better in polar solvents, nonpolar in nonpolar. For miscible liquids, solute-solvent attractions are at least as strong as solute-solute or solvent-solvent attractions.

### Solutions of Solids in Liquids
**Temperature dependence:** Solubility curves generally show increasing solid solubility with temperature, with exceptions (e.g., cerium sulfate decreases).

| Compound | 0 °C | 100 °C |
|----------|------|--------|
| Sugar (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) | ~180 g/100 g H₂O | >240 g/100 g H₂O |
| KNO₃ | ~115 g/100 g H₂O | ~240 g/100 g H₂O |
| NaBr | ~115 g/100 g H₂O | ~123 g/100 g H₂O |
| Na₂SO₄ | ~7 g/100 g H₂O | ~52 g/100 g H₂O |
| Ce₂(SO₄)₃ | ~20 g/100 g H₂O | ~0 g/100 g H₂O (decreases with T) |

**Supersaturated solutions and crystallization:** Exploit temperature dependence — (1) dissolve solute at elevated temperature, (2) cool without disturbing, (3) solute concentration then exceeds equilibrium solubility, (4) solution is relatively stable until triggered. Triggers: adding a seed crystal or mechanical agitation. Application: hand warmers contain supersaturated sodium acetate; clicking an internal metal disk creates a mechanical shockwave that initiates crystallization, releasing heat.

## 11.4 Colligative Properties

> Source: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/11-4-colligative-properties

### Overview
"Colligative properties include vapor pressure lowering, boiling point elevation, freezing point depression, and osmotic pressure." They depend only on the **total number of solute particles**, regardless of chemical identity.

### Mole Fraction and Molality
**Mole fraction (X):** ratio of a component's molar amount to total moles of all components.

X_A = (mol A) / (total mol of all components)

The sum of mole fractions equals one.

**Molality (m):** moles of solute per kilogram of solvent.

m = (mol solute) / (kg solvent)

Both are temperature-independent and preferred for colligative calculations.

### Vapor Pressure Lowering
Dissolving a nonvolatile solute in a volatile liquid lowers the solution's vapor pressure. Solute particles occupy surface area, reducing solvent vaporization rate while condensation rate is unchanged.

**Raoult's law:** "the partial pressure exerted by any component of an ideal solution is equal to the vapor pressure of the pure component multiplied by its mole fraction in the solution."

P_A = X_A · P°_A

where P_A is the partial pressure of component A, P°_A is the vapor pressure of pure A, and X_A its mole fraction.

For solutions with only nonvolatile solutes:

P_solution = X_solvent · P°_solvent

Total vapor pressure for multicomponent ideal solutions:

P_solution = Σ P_i = Σ X_i · P°_i

### Distillation
Distillation separates components by differing vapor pressures; the more volatile component is enriched in the vapor phase. Fractional distillation in oil refineries uses tall fractionating columns where vaporized components condense at appropriate temperature zones (diesel, kerosene, gasoline).

### Boiling Point Elevation
Solutions boil higher than pure solvents because their lower vapor pressure requires a higher temperature to reach atmospheric pressure.

ΔT_b = K_b · m

where K_b is the **ebullioscopic constant** (boiling point elevation constant), in °C·m⁻¹, and m is molality of all solute species. Examples: water K_b = 0.512 °C·m⁻¹; benzene 2.53; chloroform 3.63. A 1 m sucrose solution and a 1 m ethylene glycol solution boil identically because each has one mole of solute particles per kg solvent.

### Freezing Point Depression
Solutions freeze lower than pure solvents.

ΔT_f = K_f · m

where K_f is the **cryoscopic constant** (freezing point depression constant), in °C·m⁻¹. Examples: water K_f = 1.86 °C·m⁻¹; benzene 5.12; chloroform 4.68. Applications: road de-icing salts, automobile antifreeze; seawater freezes below 0 °C, keeping polar oceans unfrozen.

### Phase Diagram for Solutions
The solution's liquid-vapor curve lies beneath the pure solvent's (vapor pressure lowering, ΔP). At any pressure the solution's boiling point is higher (ΔT_b). The solid-liquid curve shifts left (freezing point depression, ΔT_f). The solid-gas curves are identical for solvent and solution, since only solvent molecules undergo the liquid-to-solid transition.

### Osmosis and Osmotic Pressure
**Semipermeable membranes** allow solvent molecules to pass while blocking solute (e.g., cell membranes, dialysis tubing). **Osmosis** is the "diffusion-driven transfer of solvent molecules through a semipermeable membrane" — solvent diffuses into the solution faster than it returns, because solvent concentration is higher on the pure-solvent side.

**Osmotic pressure (Π)** is the pressure required to stop osmosis:

Π = M·R·T

- Π = osmotic pressure (atm)
- M = molar concentration (mol/L)
- R = universal gas constant = 0.08206 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹
- T = absolute temperature (K)

**Tonicity classifications:**
- **Isotonic:** same osmotic pressure as blood serum (~7.7 atm); injected solutions must be isotonic to prevent cell damage.
- **Hypotonic:** less concentrated than blood serum; water enters cells, swelling and rupturing them (**hemolysis**).
- **Hypertonic:** more concentrated than blood serum; cells lose water, shrivel, and may die (**crenation**).

### Reverse Osmosis
Applying pressure exceeding the solution's osmotic pressure reverses osmosis, pushing solvent out of the solution. Used to desalt seawater and produce high-purity drinking water.

### Determination of Molar Masses
Osmotic pressure and changes in freezing point, boiling point, and vapor pressure are directly proportional to solute particle numbers; measuring them for a known solute mass permits molar mass determination.

### Colligative Properties of Electrolytes
Electrolytes dissociate/ionize on dissolution:
- NaCl(s) → Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)
- HCl(aq) + H₂O(l) → Cl⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq)

A 1.0 m NaCl solution theoretically contains 2.0 mol ions per kg water. Expected ΔT_f = 2.0 mol ions/kg × 1.86 °C·kg/mol = 3.7 °C. Measured value is 3.4 °C — suggesting incomplete (effective) dissociation.

**van 't Hoff factor (i):**

i = (moles of particles in solution) / (moles of formula units dissolved)

| Solute | Classification | Products | i (predicted) | i (measured, 0.050 m) |
|--------|----------------|----------|---------------|------------------------|
| C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ (sucrose) | Nonelectrolyte | C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ | 1 | 1.0 |
| NaCl | Strong electrolyte | Na⁺, Cl⁻ | 2 | 1.9 |
| HCl | Strong electrolyte | H₃O⁺, Cl⁻ | 2 | 1.9 |
| MgSO₄ | Strong electrolyte | Mg²⁺, SO₄²⁻ | 2 | 1.3 |
| MgCl₂ | Strong electrolyte | Mg²⁺, 2Cl⁻ | 3 | 2.7 |
| FeCl₃ | Strong electrolyte | Fe³⁺, 3Cl⁻ | 4 | 3.4 |

**Ion pairing (Debye-Hückel theory, 1923):** Lower measured i values arise from residual interionic attraction. "Although interionic attraction in aqueous solution is very greatly reduced by solvation of the ions and the insulating action of the polar solvent, it is not completely nullified." Cations and anions may touch, forming solvated **ion pairs**, reducing the effective concentration (activity) of free ions. In dilute solutions ions separate widely, attractions weaken, and measured i approaches the predicted value.

**Modified colligative equations** (incorporating i):

ΔT_b = i·K_b·m
ΔT_f = i·K_f·m
Π = i·M·R·T

## 11.5 Colloids

> Source: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/11-5-colloids

### Definition and Classification
A **colloid** (colloidal dispersion) is a mixture with properties intermediate between suspensions and solutions. Unlike solutions (homogeneous; dissolved species are molecules or ions that do not settle), colloids contain particles larger than simple molecules but small enough that they do not settle on standing. **Suspensions**, by contrast, are heterogeneous mixtures with relatively large, visible particles; they are cloudy and the suspended particles settle out after mixing.

### The Tyndall Effect
Colloidal particles are "large enough to scatter light, a phenomenon called the Tyndall effect." This scattering makes colloidal mixtures appear cloudy or opaque (e.g., a visible searchlight beam in fog). "Clouds are colloidal mixtures" — water droplets much larger than molecules but small enough not to settle.

### Dispersed Phase and Dispersion Medium
"The particulate component typically present in a relatively minor amount is called the dispersed phase and the substance or solution throughout which the particulate is dispersed is called the dispersion medium."

### Classification of Colloidal Systems
| Dispersed Phase | Dispersion Medium | Common Examples | Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solid | Gas | Smoke, dust | — |
| Solid | Liquid | Starch in water, inks, paints, milk of magnesia | Sol |
| Solid | Solid | Colored gems, alloys | — |
| Liquid | Gas | Clouds, fogs, mists, sprays | Aerosol |
| Liquid | Liquid | Milk, mayonnaise, butter | Emulsion |
| Liquid | Solid | Jellies, gels, pearl, opal | Gel |
| Gas | Liquid | Foams, whipped cream, beaten egg whites | Foam |
| Gas | Solid | Pumice, floating soaps | — |

### Preparation of Colloidal Systems
Particles of colloidal size are formed by two methods:
1. **Dispersion methods** — breaking down larger particles (e.g., paint pigments produced by grinding large particles in special mills).
2. **Condensation methods** — growth from smaller units such as molecules or ions (e.g., clouds form when water molecules condense into small droplets).

Some substances disperse spontaneously: gelatin, glue, starch, and dehydrated milk powder are already colloidal-sized; water simply disperses them.

**Emulsion preparation:** An emulsion may be prepared by shaking or blending two immiscible liquids, breaking one into colloidal droplets dispersed in the other. The dispersed phase tends to coalesce and separate, so emulsions are usually stabilized by an **emulsifying agent**, a substance that inhibits coalescence. Examples: soap stabilizes kerosene in water; milk is an emulsion of butterfat in water with the protein casein as emulsifier; mayonnaise is an emulsion of oil in vinegar with egg-yolk components as emulsifiers.

**Condensation example:** A red colloidal suspension of iron(III) hydroxide is prepared by mixing concentrated iron(III) chloride with hot water:

Fe³⁺(aq) + 3Cl⁻(aq) + 6H₂O(l) → Fe(OH)₃(s) + H₃O⁺(aq) + 3Cl⁻(aq)

Gold colloids show long-term stability — some gold sols prepared in 1857 are still intact (particles have not coalesced and settled).

### Soaps and Detergents
"The salts of fatty acids are called soaps," produced by treating animal fats with bases. Sodium stearate contains an uncharged nonpolar hydrocarbon chain (the C₁₇H₃₅— unit) and an ionic carboxylate group. **Detergents** (soap substitutes) also contain nonpolar hydrocarbon chains (e.g., C₁₂H₂₅—) and an ionic group such as a sulfate (—OSO₃⁻) or sulfonate (—SO₃⁻). Soaps form insoluble calcium and magnesium compounds in hard water; detergents form water-soluble products.

**Cleaning action:** The nonpolar (hydrocarbon) end dissolves in or is attracted to nonpolar oil, grease, or dirt; the ionic end is attracted by polar water. Such molecules are **amphiphilic** — having a hydrophobic ("water-fearing") part and a hydrophilic ("water-loving") part — so dirt particles become suspended as colloidal particles and are washed away.

### Electrical Properties of Colloidal Particles
"Dispersed colloidal particles are often electrically charged." A colloidal Fe(OH)₃ particle does not contain enough hydroxide ions to compensate exactly for the positive iron(III) charges, so each particle bears a positive charge. All colloidal particles in any one system have charges of the same sign, which helps keep them dispersed because like charges repel.

**Removal via electrostatic precipitation:** Charged particles are attracted to highly charged electrodes, where they are neutralized and deposited as dust. Frederick Cottrell developed the **electrostatic precipitator** — an important method for cleaning up smoke from industrial processes.

### Gels
"A gel is a colloidal dispersion of a liquid phase throughout a solid phase." The fibers of the dispersing medium form a complex three-dimensional network, the interstices filled with the liquid medium or a dilute solution. Examples: gelatin desserts; pectin (a carbohydrate from fruit juices, important in jelly making); silica gel (a colloidal dispersion of hydrated silicon dioxide, formed when dilute HCl is added to dilute sodium silicate).
