## 18.1 Periodicity

### Classification of Elements
**Representative elements** are those in which the *s* and *p* orbitals are filling. **Transition elements** have *d* orbitals filling (groups 3–11); **inner transition metals** have *f* orbitals filling. Group 12 elements qualify as representative metals because the last electron added enters an *s* orbital.

**Metallic character** arises from an element's ability to lose its outer valence electrons; it gives high thermal and electrical conductivity. There are twenty nonradioactive representative metals (groups 1, 2, 3, 12, 13, 14, 15). **Metalloids** have properties between those of metals and nonmetals and are typically semiconductors. **Nonmetals** tend to form anions or molecular compounds. A **salt** is an ionic compound consisting of cations and anions.

### Passivation and Reactivity
Many representative metals do not occur uncombined in nature because they readily react with water and oxygen in the air. **Passivation** occurs when a metal reacts with air to form a protective, nonreactive film of oxide or some other compound.

### Group 1: Alkali Metals
The alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr) have the largest atomic radii and the lowest first ionization energy in their periods, making it very easy to remove the single valence electron. They form stable 1+ cations; reactivity increases with increasing atomic number. The solid metals are very soft; lithium has the lowest density of any metal (0.5 g/cm³).

Reactions with water (vigorous, forming H₂ and a basic metal hydroxide; alkali metals are easier to oxidize than hydrogen):
- 2Li(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2LiOH(aq) + H₂(g)

Alkali metals react directly with all nonmetals except the noble gases to form binary ionic compounds with 1+ ions. Because of high reactivity they are stored under mineral oil. They never occur free in nature; preparation requires electrolysis of alkali metal compounds. Sodium reduces compounds of Ti and Zr in their production; Li/Na are reducing agents in organic synthesis. Flame colors: sodium = bright yellow; lithium = crimson; others = pale violet.

### Group 2: Alkaline Earth Metals
The alkaline earth metals (Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra) are named because the oxides of the heavier members react with water to form alkaline solutions. They are smaller and have higher first ionization energies than the alkali metals in the same period; still very reactive, losing both valence electrons to form 2+ ions. Be and Mg (highest ionization energies) form some compounds with covalent character. Flame colors: Ca and Sr = red shades; Ba = green.

Magnesium is a silver-white, malleable/ductile (at high T) metal; passivation by a magnesium oxycarbonate layer lowers its reactivity. It is the lightest widely used structural metal. Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba react with water and air (Ba most vigorous), giving H₂ + metal hydroxide — so heavier alkaline earth metals are better reducing agents than hydrogen. They react with acids and nonmetals to form ionic compounds; many salts are water-insoluble due to high lattice energies.

Hot magnesium's reducing power:
- 2Mg(s) + CO₂(g) → 2MgO(s) + C(s)  (a CO₂ extinguisher cannot put out a magnesium fire)

Burning magnesium emits brilliant white light (flares, fireworks).

### Group 12: Zn, Cd, Hg
Group 12 elements are representative metals because the last electron added is an *s* electron (ns²). Forming 2+ cations gives pseudo-noble-gas configurations. Mercury can also show 1+ via the diatomic Hg₂²⁺ ion.

Reactivity trend (reverse of groups 1–2): zinc is most reactive, mercury least. Zn and Cd have lower reduction potentials than hydrogen and release H₂ with acids:
- Zn(s) + 2H₃O⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) → H₂(g) + Zn²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) + 2H₂O(l)

Zinc tarnishes to a blue-gray basic carbonate Zn₂(OH)₂CO₃ that passivates it. Uses: zinc anode in dry/alkaline cells; brass (Cu+Zn) and bronze (Cu, Sn, sometimes Zn); about half of zinc protects iron from corrosion as a **sacrificial (galvanic) anode** or as a coating (galvanized steel).

Mercury is the only metal liquid at 25 °C; it forms **amalgams** (alloys of mercury with other metals). It is harder to oxidize than hydrogen, so it does not displace H₂ from acids, but reacts with strong oxidizing acids:
- Hg(l) + HCl(aq) → no reaction
- 3Hg(l) + 8HNO₃(aq) → 3Hg(NO₃)₂(aq) + 4H₂O(l) + 2NO(g)  (NO oxidizes further to reddish-brown NO₂)

Most mercury compounds decompose on heating and are toxic; most contain Hg in the 2+ state. Amalgams (most metals except Fe, Pt, W, Ta) were historically used in electrolysis cells, gold extraction, and dental fillings (~50% Hg by weight); use has declined due to toxicity.

### Group 13: B, Al, Ga, In, Tl
Group 13 contains the metalloid boron and the metals Al, Ga, In, Tl. Boron is semiconducting with covalent binary compounds. Oxides/hydroxides of Al and Ga are **amphoteric** (react with both acids and bases); In and Tl oxides/hydroxides are only basic. Gallium melts at ~30 °C (in the hand).

Aluminum amphoterism:
- 2Al(s) + 6HCl(aq) → 2AlCl₃(aq) + 3H₂(g)
- 2Al(s) + 2NaOH(aq) + 6H₂O(l) → 2Na[Al(OH)₄](aq) + 3H₂(g)

Valence configuration ns²np¹; Al usually uses all valence electrons (oxidation state 3+). Some compounds covalent; AlF₃ and Al₂(SO₄)₃ are ionic. Aqueous Al salts contain [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ (abbreviated Al³⁺(aq)). Ga, In, Tl also form M³⁺ ions.

**Inert pair effect:** these elements also show an oxidation state two below expected (here 1+), formation of a stable ion with oxidation state two lower than the group value; the inert pair is the valence *s* orbital. Important for lower p-block elements; Tl⁺(aq) is more stable than Tl³⁺(aq). Group 13 metals react with sulfur, phosphorus, and halogens to form binary compounds. They are reactive but passivate with a tough oxide film; exposure to mercury (amalgam formation) disrupts the film. Uses of aluminum: construction, transportation, cans, foil (light, tough, strong, corrosion-resistant, good heat conductor); good reducing agent for isolating Mo, W, Cr from oxides.

### Group 14: C, Si, Ge, Sn, Pb
Metallic members are tin, lead, flerovium; carbon is a typical nonmetal; silicon and germanium are metalloids/semimetals. Tin and lead form stable divalent cations Sn²⁺ and Pb²⁺ (oxidation states two below the group 4+ value) — inert pair effect. They also form covalent 4+ compounds (SnCl₄, PbCl₄ are low-boiling covalent liquids).

Tin reacts readily with nonmetals and acids (more easily oxidized than hydrogen), giving Sn(II) or Sn(IV) depending on conditions. Lead is less reactive; oxidation usually needs hot concentrated acid.

**Allotropes** are two or more forms of the same element in the same physical state with different properties. Tin has two: white tin (stable above 13.2 °C, malleable) and gray tin (stable at low T, brittle, powders). The gray-to-white conversion ("tin disease"/"tin pest") can make tin articles disintegrate in cold weather. Uses: tin coating on steel (tin cans), bronze (Cu+Sn), solder (Sn+Pb); lead in automobile storage batteries.

### Group 15: Bismuth
Bismuth, heaviest member of group 15, is a less reactive metal; it gives up three of five valence electrons to form Bi³⁺ and reaches the 5+ group state only with strong oxidizers — another inert pair effect example.

## 18.2 Occurrence and Preparation of the Representative Metals

Representative metals are highly reactive and rarely free in nature; their compounds are abundant in minerals.

Occurrence:
- Alkali metals: Li in silicate/phosphate minerals; Na, K in salt deposits (evaporated ancient seas) and silicates.
- Alkaline earth metals: silicates, carbonates, sulfates. Be as beryl, Be₃Al₂Si₆O₁₈ (emerald, aquamarine).
- Magnesium: seawater, silicates, carbonates, sulfates.
- Aluminum: clay and **bauxite** (impure aluminum oxide hydroxide).
- Tin: cassiterite, SnO₂.
- Lead and thallium: sulfides or their weathering products.

### Electrolysis (very difficult-to-reduce ions: groups 1, 2, Al)

**Sodium — Downs cell:** electrolysis of molten NaCl at 600 °C (CaCl₂ added to lower the 801 °C melting point):
- 2NaCl(l) → 2Na(l) + Cl₂(g)   (electrolysis, 600 °C)
- Cathode: 2Na⁺ + 2e⁻ → 2Na(l)
- Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂(g) + 2e⁻

Liquid sodium floats off; chlorine is collected. Aqueous electrolysis cannot make Na (H⁺ reduces more easily, giving H₂).

**Aluminum — Hall–Héroult cell** (Charles M. Hall and Paul L. T. Héroult, independently 1886):
- AlO(OH)(s) + NaOH(aq) + H₂O(l) → Na[Al(OH)₄](aq)  (hot NaOH dissolves bauxite; impurities remain)
- Na[Al(OH)₄](aq) + H₃O⁺(aq) → Al(OH)₃(s) + Na⁺(aq) + 2H₂O(l)
- Al(OH)₃ heated → Al₂O₃, dissolved in molten cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) + CaF₂
- Electrolysis: cathode reduces Al³⁺ to Al; anode gives O₂, CO, CO₂.

**Magnesium (electrolytic):** from seawater (~0.5% MgCl₂):
- MgCl₂(aq) + Ca(OH)₂(aq) → Mg(OH)₂(s) + CaCl₂(aq)
- Mg(OH)₂(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l)
- MgCl₂(l) → Mg(l) + Cl₂(g)  (electrolysis)

### Chemical Reduction (cheaper, preferred where possible)

**Magnesium — Pidgeon process** (>75% of world production, mostly China):
- Si(s) + 2MgO(s) → SiO₂(s) + 2Mg(g)  (Δ; Mg vapor removal shifts equilibrium per Le Châtelier)

**Zinc:**
- 2ZnS(s) + 3O₂(g) → 2ZnO(s) + 2SO₂(g)  (Δ; roasting)
- ZnCO₃(s) → ZnO(s) + CO₂(g)  (Δ)
- ZnO(s) + C(s) → Zn(g) + CO(g)  (carbon reduction)
- Purified by distillation (bp Zn 907 °C; impurities As 613 °C, Cd 767 °C, Pb 1750 °C, Fe 2862 °C).

**Tin** (Sn(IV) oxide reduces with hot coals — known since antiquity): roast to remove volatile contaminants, treat with HCl, then:
- SnO₂(s) + 2C(s) → Sn(s) + 2CO(g)  (>1000 °C)

## 18.3 Structure and General Properties of the Metalloids

Six metalloids — B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te — lie at the metal/nonmetal boundary; they are semiconductors with electrons more tightly bound than in metallic conductors. They form covalent crystals like nonmetals but generally do not form monatomic anions.

### Boron
Outer configuration 2s²2p¹ gives one fewer valence electron than valence orbitals (electron deficiency); usual oxidation state +3, but boron hydrides show fractional states. Pure crystalline boron is transparent, built from B₁₂ icosahedra (B–B ≈ 176 pm). Boron is <0.001% of Earth's crust, only in oxygen compounds: boric acid B(OH)₃, borates, **borax** Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O.

Elemental boron is inert at room temperature, reacting only with F₂ and O₂ (giving BF₃ and B₂O₃). At high temperature it reacts with all nonmetals except Te and the noble gases, and most metals. Production:
- B₂O₃(s) + 3Mg(s) → 2B(s) + 3MgO(s)  (then HCl removes MgO)
- 2BCl₃(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2B(s) + 6HCl(g)  (1500 °C, ΔH° = 253.7 kJ; purification)

**Boron halides** (BF₃, BCl₃, BBr₃, BI₃) — nonpolar, sp² (trigonal planar) boron; made by direct reaction. All except BF₃ hydrolyze:
- BCl₃(g) + 3H₂O(l) → B(OH)₃(aq) + 3HCl(aq)
- BF₃(aq) + HF(aq) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + BF₄⁻(aq)  (BF₃ acts as a Lewis acid)

**Boric acid and boric oxide:** boron burns at 700 °C in O₂ to boric oxide B₂O₃ (used in borosilicate glass):
- B₂O₃(s) + 3H₂O(l) → 2B(OH)₃(aq)

In B(OH)₃ boron is sp² (trigonal); boric acid is a Lewis acid, not a proton donor:
- B(OH)₃(aq) + 2H₂O(l) ⇌ B(OH)₄⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq)  (Ka = 5.8 × 10⁻¹⁰)

Heating: B(OH)₃ → metaboric acid HBO₂ (100 °C) → tetraboric acid H₂B₄O₇ (150 °C) → boric oxide. **Borates** are salts of boron oxyacids ranging from trigonal BO₃³⁻ to chains/rings; borax Na₂[B₄O₅(OH)₄]·8H₂O is the commercially most important (laundry detergent), from dry lakes (Searles Lake, CA) or kernite Na₂B₄O₇·4H₂O.

### Silicon
Silicon is ~one-fourth of Earth's crust by mass (second to oxygen); it forms Si–O–Si linkages (chains, sheets, 3-D frameworks) that dominate the mineral world. Valence config 3s²3p², usually sp³ tetrahedral, +4. Si and Ge crystallize in the diamond structure (giant 3-D molecules).

Preparation (high-T reduction of SiO₂):
- SiO₂(s) + 2C(s) → Si(s) + 2CO(g)  (Δ)
- SiO₂(s) + 2Mg(s) → Si(s) + 2MgO(s)  (Δ)

**Semiconductor purification:** convert impure Si to a silicon tetrahalide or silane (SiH₄), decompose at high T, then **zone refining** (a molten zone is moved along a rod, sweeping impurities to one end, which is cut off). Result: ≤1 impurity part per million — the key element of the computer industry (transistors, chips, solar cells). [Transistors/chips here are descriptive applications; the wiki's hardware Transistor page is a different, computer-architecture concept.]

Passivation by a thin SiO₂ film; SiO₂ is soluble in hot base, so strong base destroys passivation:
- Si(s) + 4OH⁻(aq) → SiO₄⁴⁻(aq) + 2H₂(g)

Silicon does not readily form double/triple bonds. SiX₄ (X electronegative) act as Lewis acids forming six-coordinate Si:
- 2NaF(s) + SiF₄(g) → Na₂SiF₆(s)  (octahedral [SiF₆]²⁻)

**Silicon halides:** SiCl₄ by direct chlorination or:
- SiO₂(s) + 2C(s) + 2Cl₂(g) → SiCl₄(g) + 2CO(g)  (Δ; SiCl₄ is nonpolar, bp 57 °C)
- SiO₂(s) + 4HF(g) → SiF₄(g) + 2H₂O(l)  (ΔH° = −191.2 kJ)

HF is the only common acid that attacks SiO₂/silicates (Si–F bond is the only one stronger than Si–O), so all common acids except HF can be stored in glass. Except SiF₄, silicon halides are very water-sensitive; SiCl₄ hydrolyzes to orthosilicic acid Si(OH)₄ (H₄SiO₄), which decomposes to SiO₂.

**Silicon dioxide (silica):** crystalline (quartz; Fe³⁺ traces → amethyst) and amorphous (opal, glass). Contrast with CO₂: dry ice is discrete CO₂ molecules with C=O double bonds and weak intermolecular forces (volatile); SiO₂ is a covalent network solid — each Si bonded to four O by single bonds in corner-sharing SiO₄ tetrahedra, a 3-D network (a quartz crystal is one macromolecule). This reflects group 14's weak π bonding for elements below period 2; SiO₂ has only σ bonds. Quartz melts at 1600 °C to a viscous liquid that supercools to glass; silica glass is UV-transparent with a very low expansion coefficient.

**Silicates** are salts of Si–O anions; sp³ Si at tetrahedral centers, O at corners, varying Si:O ratios from discrete units to corner-shared networks. Many ceramics are silicates.

### Germanium
Very similar in chemical behavior to silicon.

### Arsenic and Antimony
Generally +3 or +5; arsenic can form arsenides (−3). Tarnish slightly in dry air, oxidize when warmed. Antimony reacts with stoichiometric halogens to trihalides, with excess F₂/Cl₂ to pentahalides SbF₅, SbCl₅; with sulfur to Sb₂S₃ or Sb₂S₅. Antimony is more metallic than arsenic. Arsenic's most stable allotrope is a puckered-layer sheet structure (three covalent bonds per atom); antimony is similar (both graphite-like but puckered).

### Tellurium
Combines directly with most elements; most stable compounds are tellurides (Te²⁻ with active metals/lanthanides) and compounds with O, F, Cl (usually +2 or +4). Te(VI) compounds exist (e.g., TeF₆) but Te resists oxidation to the maximum group state. Te crystals contain infinite spiral chains, each atom bonded to two others.

## 18.4 Structure and General Properties of the Nonmetals

Nonmetals occupy the upper-right of the periodic table. More than half are gases, one is a liquid, the rest are solids (some softest, some hardest). They include the most and least reactive elements and form many ionic and covalent compounds.

### Bonding and physical states
Nonmetals have higher electronegativities than metals. Metal–nonmetal compounds are generally ionic (large Δ electronegativity); nonmetal–nonmetal compounds are covalent and tend to be molecular (gases, liquids, volatile solids). Nonmetals do not form monatomic positive ions (ionization energies too high); all monatomic nonmetal ions are anions (Cl⁻, N³⁻, Se²⁻).

Common oxidation states: H (+1, −1); C (+4, −4); N, P, As (+5, +3, −3); O, S, Se (+6, +4, −2); F, Cl, Br, I (+7, +5, +3, +1, −1); Xe (+8, +6, +4, +2).

**First-member effects:** the first member of each group differs (smaller size, greater ionization energy, only four valence orbitals — one 2s, three 2p — no d orbitals). Example: N forms only NF₃, while P forms both PF₃ and PF₅. First members form π bonds more readily.

### Redox chemistry of nonmetals (five aspects)
1. Nonmetals oxidize metals: 4Fe(s) + 3O₂(g) → 2Fe₂O₃(s)
2. More electronegative nonmetals oxidize less electronegative ones (N and C are poor oxidizers): S(s) + O₂(g) → SO₂; Cl₂(g) + 2I⁻(aq) → I₂(s) + 2Cl⁻(aq)
3. F and O are strongest oxidizers in their groups; group 17 holds the strongest in any period: 2As(s) + 3Br₂(l) → 2AsBr₃(s)
4. The stronger the oxidizer, the harder its anion is to oxidize (most stable anions from tops of groups / group 17).
5. F never shows a positive state; O is positive only with F: 2F₂(g) + 2OH⁻(aq) → OF₂(g) + 2F⁻(aq) + H₂O(l)

### Oxides of nonmetals
Nonmetals (except most noble gases) form covalent oxides, mostly acidic (exceptions CO, N₂O, NO). Three traits of acidic oxides:
1. **Acid anhydrides** (no oxidation-state change): SO₂(g) + H₂O(l) → H₂SO₃(aq); N₂O₅(s) + H₂O(l) → 2HNO₃(aq)
2. **Disproportionation** (oxide not in a common state): 3NO₂(g) + H₂O(l) → 2HNO₃(aq) + NO(g)
3. Acid strength increases with the electronegativity of the central atom.

**Binary hydrogen compounds:** acid strength increases left→right across a period and down a group. Only HCl, HBr, HI are strong acids. NH₃ < H₂O < HF; H₂O < H₂S < H₂Se. Weaker acid → stronger base.

### Structures of the nonmetals
Nonmetal solids have covalent bonds (often discrete molecules) with localized electrons, unlike close-packed metals with delocalized electrons. Noble gases are monatomic (no bonding). H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂ are diatomic. Halogens: F₂, Cl₂ gases; Br₂ liquid; I₂ solid (rising London dispersion forces with mass/polarizability).

Allotropes: oxygen (O₂, ozone O₃); phosphorus (white, red, black); sulfur (several); carbon (diamond, graphite, charcoal, fullerenes, nanotubes, graphene).

### Carbon
- **Diamond:** very hard, colorless; each C forms four single bonds (sp³, tetrahedral) in a 3-D network (giant molecule), mp ~4400 °C.
- **Graphite:** soft, slippery, conducts electricity; layers of sp²-hybridized C (three σ bonds, trigonal planar) with delocalized π bonds (resonance) within layers; weak London forces between layers (pencil "lead", lubricant).
- Carbon black (incomplete CH₄ combustion); charcoal (heating wood without air); coke (heating coal without air).
- **Buckminsterfullerene C₆₀:** soccer-ball icosahedral molecules.
- **Graphene:** single graphite layer (2003); six-membered sp² rings, strong, light, excellent heat/electrical conductor.
- **Carbon nanotubes:** rings of sp² carbon wrapped into tubes; extremely strong (harder than diamond); conductor or semiconductor depending on shape; used in composites (bicycle parts, skis, bats, rods, surfboards).

### Phosphorus
Name = "light bringing"; only group-15 member not free in nature; many allotropes.
- **White phosphorus:** white waxy solid, mp 44.2 °C, bp 280 °C, insoluble in water (stored under water), soluble in CS₂, bursts into flame in air; P₄ tetrahedral molecules (each P single-bonded to three others); most reactive, very toxic.
- **Red phosphorus:** from heating white P to 270–300 °C without air; denser, mp ~600 °C, much less reactive, essentially nontoxic; polymeric 3-D network of P₄ tetrahedra joined by P–P bonds.

### Sulfur
Greatest, most complex allotropy of any element (biblical "brimstone"; in atmospheres of Venus and Io).
- **Rhombic sulfur (yellow):** most common/stable; mp 113 °C; S₈ molecules.
- **Monoclinic sulfur:** needles from cooling liquid; stable 96–119 °C; S₈; reverts to rhombic at room temperature.
- **S₈ structure:** eight-membered puckered crown rings, single S–S bonds.
- **Liquid sulfur:** melts to a mobile straw-colored liquid (spherical S₈); on heating, rings break to polymeric chains, liquid darkens and becomes very viscous (~230 °C barely pours); rapid cooling gives plastic (amorphous) sulfur.
- **Vapor:** bp 445 °C; S₂, S₆, S₈; near 1000 °C mostly S₂ (paramagnetic, like O₂, weak S=S double bond).

### Octet rule and structural patterns
Nonmetals usually attain eight valence electrons by forming enough covalent bonds. Group 15 (5 e⁻): three bonds (N≡N or three single bonds in As, P). Group 16 (need 2): O=O double bond; S, Se, Te two single bonds (rings/chains). Group 17 (need 1): diatomic, one bond. Noble gases: filled shell, no covalent bonds.

## 18.5 Occurrence, Preparation, and Compounds of Hydrogen

Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe (~90% of atoms). On Earth water is the most abundant hydrogen compound; H also appears in petroleum, minerals, cellulose, starch, sugars, fats, oils, alcohols, and acids. It is a colorless, odorless, tasteless, nonpoisonous diatomic gas (H₂).

### Isotopes
- **Protium (¹H)** — ordinary hydrogen.
- **Deuterium (²H or D)** — ~1 per 7,000 H atoms.
- **Tritium (³H or T)** — radioactive, ~1 per 10¹⁸ H atoms.
Same chemistry (identical electron structure), differing physical properties due to mass; D and T have lower vapor pressure.

### Preparation
- Steam over coke at 1000 °C (**water gas**): C(s) + H₂O(g) → CO(g) + H₂(g) (water-gas shift then converts CO + steam to CO₂ + H₂)
- Steam reforming of hydrocarbons over Ni at 900 °C: C₃H₈(g) + 3H₂O(g) → 3CO(g) + 7H₂(g)
- Electrolysis: 2H₂O(l) + electrical energy → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) (H₂ at cathode, twice the O₂ volume)
- Metal + dilute acid (most convenient lab method): Fe(s) + 2H₃O⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) → Fe²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) + H₂(g) + 2H₂O(l)
- Ionic metal hydride + water (convenient where weight matters — life jackets, rafts, balloons): CaH₂(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) + 2H₂(g)

### Reactions
Unreactive at normal conditions; reacts when heated. Combustion with O₂ is very exothermic (286 kJ per mole H₂O); oxyhydrogen torch reaches 2800 °C; liquid H₂ is rocket fuel.
- With group 1 and Ca/Sr/Ba: ionic hydrides (H⁻; strong reducing agent and base)
- 3H₂ + N₂ → 2NH₃ (high P/T, low yield)
- 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O (explosive if ignited)
- H₂ + S → H₂S (heated, low yield)
- H₂ + X₂ → 2HX (explosive with F₂; low yield with I₂)
- Reduces hot metal oxides: H₂(g) + MnO₂(s) → MnO(s) + H₂O(g) (Δ)

### Hydrogen compounds
**Ammonia (NH₃):** lab — NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O; commercial **Haber process** — N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) (catalyst, ΔH° = −92 kJ); ionic nitride hydrolysis — Mg₃N₂(s) + 6H₂O(l) → 3Mg(OH)₂(s) + 2NH₃(g). Colorless, sharp odor, liquefies at −33 °C; H-bonding gives the highest enthalpy of vaporization of any liquid except water; very soluble (658 L at STP per L H₂O). Brønsted base; weak acid (forms amides NH₂⁻, imides, nitrides N³⁻ with metals at high T); resists reduction but is readily oxidized (burns to NO + H₂O). Derivatives:
- NH₃(aq) + OCl⁻(aq) → NH₂Cl(aq) + OH⁻(aq) (chloramine)
- NH₂Cl(aq) + NH₃(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → N₂H₄(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) + H₂O(l) (hydrazine)

**Hydrazine (N₂H₄):** N₂(g) + 2H₂(g) → N₂H₄(l), ΔG°f = 149.2 kJ/mol; fuming colorless liquid (mp 2 °C, bp 113.5 °C, density 1.00 g/mL at 25 °C). N₂H₄(l) + O₂(g) → N₂(g) + 2H₂O(l), ΔH° = −621.5 kJ/mol; Brønsted/Lewis base (N₂H₅⁺, N₂H₆²⁺); rocket fuel.

**Phosphine (PH₃):** not by direct union. AlP(s) + 3H₃O⁺(aq) → PH₃(g) + Al³⁺(aq) + 3H₂O(l); P₄(s) + 4OH⁻(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 2HPO₃²⁻(aq) + 2PH₃(g). Colorless, very poisonous (decaying-fish odor); 4PH₃ → P₄ + 6H₂; forms PH₄Cl, PH₄I (decompose in water). Uses: grain fumigation, semiconductor processing.

**Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S):** rotten-egg odor; as toxic as HCN and deceptive (paralyzes olfactory nerves). FeS(s) + 2H₃O⁺(aq) → Fe²⁺(aq) + H₂S(g) + 2H₂O(l). Good reducing agent (reduces Fe³⁺→Fe²⁺, MnO₄⁻→Mn²⁺, Cr₂O₇²⁻→Cr³⁺, HNO₃→NO₂): 2H₂S(g) + O₂(g) → 2S(s) + 2H₂O(l). Weak diprotic acid (hydrosulfuric acid); soluble sulfides/hydrosulfides are basic.

**Hydrogen halides (HF, HCl, HBr, HI):** gases. X₂ + H₂ → 2HX (commercial for HCl, HBr); CaF₂(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → CaSO₄(s) + 2HF(g); for HBr/HI use H₃PO₄ (weaker oxidizer): H₃PO₄(l) + Br⁻(aq) → HBr(g) + H₂PO₄⁻(aq). All soluble (hydrohalic acids); all strong except HF. Heavier halide ions are reducing agents:
- Cl₂(aq) + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻(aq), E° = 1.36 V; Br₂(aq) + 2e⁻ → 2Br⁻(aq), E° = 1.09 V; I₂(aq) + 2e⁻ → 2I⁻(aq), E° = 0.54 V
- Br₂(aq) + 2HI(aq) → 2HBr(aq) + I₂(aq), E° = 0.55 V
HF attacks glass: SiO₂(s) + 4HF(aq) → SiF₄(g) + 2H₂O(l); CaSiO₃(s) + 6HF(aq) → CaF₂(s) + SiF₄(g) + 3H₂O(l). HF uses: hydrochlorofluorocarbons, cryolite for aluminum, BF₃ catalyst. HCl: inexpensive, versatile (metal chlorides, dyes, glue, glucose, oil-well activation, pickle liquor).

Industrially, two-thirds of world hydrogen goes to ammonia (fertilizer, nitric acid); large amounts also for hydrogenation. Periodic position: H has one 1s electron; can form H⁺ (group 1 character) or H⁻ and single covalent bonds (group 17 character) — a unique element almost deserving its own table location.

## 18.6 Occurrence, Preparation, and Properties of Carbonates

Groups 1 and 2 plus Zn, Cd, Hg, Pb(II) form ionic carbonates (CO₃²⁻). Group 1, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba also form hydrogen carbonates (bicarbonates, HCO₃⁻).

### Preparation
Carbonates of groups 1–2 (except MgCO₃) from CO₂ + oxide/hydroxide:
- Na₂O(s) + CO₂(g) → Na₂CO₃(s)
- Ca(OH)₂(s) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l)
Insoluble carbonates (alkaline earth, group 12, Pb(II)) by precipitation:
- Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → CaCO₃(s)
- Pb²⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → PbCO₃(s)
Pearls/shells are CaCO₃. Sn(II) and tri-/tetravalent ions (Al³⁺, Sn⁴⁺) give CO₂ + oxide instead of carbonates.

Hydrogen carbonates by saturating hydroxide with CO₂:
- OH⁻(aq) + CO₂(aq) → HCO₃⁻(aq)

### Solubility and caves
Alkaline earth carbonates dissolve in CO₂-rich water (caves, sinkholes in limestone):
- CaCO₃(s) + CO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2HCO₃⁻(aq)
Hydrogen carbonates are stable only in solution; evaporation reverses this (stalactites, stalagmites).

### Commercial production
Sodium carbonate from the mineral **trona**, Na₃(CO₃)(HCO₃)(H₂O)₂:
- 2Na₃(CO₃)(HCO₃)(H₂O)₂(s) → 3Na₂CO₃(s) + 5H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)

### Properties
Carbonates are moderately strong bases (CO₃²⁻ accepts H⁺):
- CO₃²⁻(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ HCO₃⁻(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
React with acids → salt + CO₂ + water:
- CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) (CaCO₃ is the antacid in Tums)
Used in glassmaking (CO₃²⁻ as oxide-ion source) and oxide synthesis.

Hydrogen carbonates are amphoteric:
- KHCO₃(aq) + KOH(aq) → K₂CO₃(aq) + H₂O(l)
Baking soda (NaHCO₃) in baking powder with a solid acid such as cream of tartar (KHC₄H₄O₆):
- HC₄H₄O₆⁻(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq) → C₄H₄O₆²⁻(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) (CO₂ leavens dough)

## 18.7 Occurrence, Preparation, and Properties of Nitrogen

Pure nitrogen comes mainly from fractional distillation of liquid air; the atmosphere is 78% N₂ by volume. Nitrogen is essential in proteins and DNA/RNA. Colorless, odorless, tasteless gas; bp 77 K, fp 63 K (liquid N₂ is an inexpensive coolant).

**Reactivity:** very low (strong N≡N triple bond). At room temperature reacts only with Li (Li₃N), some transition-metal complexes, and via nitrogen-fixing bacteria.

**Nitrogen fixation** — converting atmospheric N₂ to usable chemicals: (1) biological, via nitrogenase enzymes (Fe and Mo) in rhizobia of legume root nodules; (2) lightning, forming nitrogen oxides carried to soil. Industrial uses: making ammonia (the principal feedstock for other N compounds); inert blanketing of canned foods/meats.

**Oxidation states:** compounds exist for every state from 3− to 5+. Active metals reduce nitrogen to metal nitrides.

### Nitrogen oxides
- **N₂O (dinitrogen oxide):** from careful heating of ammonium nitrate; colorless, mild sweet odor ("laughing gas", dental anesthetic); resonance N≡N⁺–O⁻ ↔ N⁻=N⁺=O.
- **NO (nitric oxide):** low yields from heating N₂ + O₂; from lightning; commercially by burning ammonia; lab by 3Cu(s) + 8HNO₃(aq) → 2NO(g) + 3Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) + 4H₂O(l). Most thermally stable nitrogen oxide; simplest stable molecule with an unpaired electron; colorless (absorption not in visible); dimerizes to N₂O₂ in liquid/solid.
- **N₂O₃ (dinitrogen trioxide):** equal NO + NO₂ cooled to −21 °C → blue liquid; exists only as liquid/solid; reverts to NO + NO₂ on heating; anhydride of nitrous acid (→ HNO₂ with water).
- **NO₂ (nitrogen dioxide):** from heating a heavy-metal nitrate or reducing concentrated HNO₃ with Cu; commercially by oxidizing NO with air; unpaired electron (colored, paramagnetic, dimerizes). 2NO₂(g) ⇌ N₂O₄(g), Kₚ = 6.86. In cold water disproportionates to HNO₂ + HNO₃; hot, to HNO₃ + NO.
- **N₂O₅ (dinitrogen pentaoxide):** white solid, anhydride of nitric acid; by dehydration of HNO₃ with P₄O₁₀: P₄O₁₀(s) + 4HNO₃(l) → 4HPO₃(s) + 2N₂O₅(s); unstable above room T (→ N₂O₄ + O₂); N₂O₅(s) + H₂O(l) → 2HNO₃(aq).

### Redox behavior of nitrogen oxides
- N₂O is a strong oxidizer (decomposes to N₂ + O₂; supports combustion better than air).
- NO as oxidizer: P₄(s) + 6NO(g) → P₄O₆(s) + 3N₂(g); as reducer: Cl₂(g) + 2NO(g) → 2ClNO(g)
- NO₂ as oxidizer: NO₂(g) + CO(g) → NO(g) + CO₂(g); NO₂(g) + 2HCl(aq) → NO(g) + Cl₂(g) + H₂O(l)

## 18.8 Occurrence, Preparation, and Properties of Phosphorus

Industrial preparation — heat calcium phosphate (phosphate rock) with sand and coke:
- 2Ca₃(PO₄)₂(s) + 6SiO₂(s) + 10C(s) → 6CaSiO₃(l) + 10CO(g) + P₄(g)
Phosphorus distills out, condensing as solid or burned to P₄O₁₀.

**Oxidation states:** 3−, 3+, 5+. Unusual states with P–P bonds: H₂P–PH₂ (diphosphorus tetrahydride) and P₄S₃ (tetraphosphorus trisulfide, head of strike-anywhere matches).

### Oxides
- **P₄O₆ (phosphorus(III) oxide):** white crystalline solid, garlic odor, toxic vapor; oxidizes slowly in air, inflames at 70 °C to P₄O₁₀; dissolves slowly in cold water to phosphorous acid H₃PO₃.
- **P₄O₁₀ (phosphorus(V) oxide):** white powder from burning P in excess O₂; ΔHf = −2984 kJ; stable, poor oxidizer; P₄O₁₀(s) + 6H₂O(l) → 4H₃PO₄(aq); excellent drying agent.

### Halogen compounds
P reacts directly with halogens to PX₃ and PX₅ (trihalides more stable than nitrogen trihalides).
- **PCl₃:** colorless liquid, Cl₂ over molten P; hydrolyzes (excess water) to H₃PO₃.
- **PCl₅:** off-white solid, from oxidizing PCl₃ with excess Cl₂; sublimes, equilibrium with PCl₃ + Cl₂ when heated; hydrolyzes to H₃PO₄.
Pentahalides are Lewis acids (empty d orbitals) forming PX₆⁻. Solid PCl₅ is ionic [PCl₄⁺][PCl₆⁻]; PBr₅ is [PBr₄⁺][Br⁻]; PI₅ is [PI₄⁺][I⁻]; PF₅ is molecular in all states.

**Applications:** fertilizers and chemical manufacturing; ferrophosphorus and phosphor-bronze alloys, pesticides, matches, some plastics; many phosphorus compounds start from P₄O₁₀.

## 18.9 Occurrence, Preparation, and Compounds of Oxygen

Oxygen is the most abundant element in Earth's crust (~50% of crust mass, mainly combined with silicon); ~20% of air mass; water is ~89% O by mass. Colorless, odorless, tasteless gas, slightly denser than air; 49 mL dissolves per L water at STP.

### Preparation
- Commercially from air (cool/compress to liquefy, then warm; O₂ bp 90 K separates from N₂ bp 77 K) and from electrolysis of water.
- **Ozone:** dry O₂ between charged plates: 3O₂(g) → 2O₃(g) (electric discharge, ΔH° = 287 kJ).

### Allotropes
- **Dioxygen (O₂)** — standard form.
- **Ozone (O₃)** — distinctive odor; forms in stratosphere by UV on O₂; absorbs harmful UV: O₃(g) → O(g) + O₂(g) (UV); bleaching and disinfectant.

### Reactions
Strong oxidizing agent; reacts with most elements (not noble gases, halogens, few noble-ish transition metals) and many compounds:
- 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s); P₄(s) + 5O₂(g) → P₄O₁₀(s)
- 2H₂S(g) + 3O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l) + 2SO₂(g)
Only fluorine can oxidize oxides to O₂.

### Oxide classification
**1. Oxides (O²⁻):** by heating hydroxides/carbonates:
- 2Al(OH)₃(s) → Al₂O₃(s) + 3H₂O(g) (Δ); CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g) (Δ)
- 2KNO₃(s) + 10K(s) → 6K₂O(s) + N₂(g) (Δ); 2LiOH(s) + 2Li(s) → 2Li₂O(s) + H₂(g) (Δ)
Groups 2–15 metals form oxides by burning in air (except HgO); heavy members show inert-pair states (Tl₂O, PbO, Bi₂O₃) vs lighter (SnO₂). O²⁻ is a powerful H⁺ acceptor; except insoluble Al₂O₃, SnO₂, PbO₂, oxides react with acids:
- Na₂O + 2HNO₃(aq) → 2NaNO₃(aq) + H₂O(l); CaO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l); SnO(s) + 2HClO₄(aq) → Sn(ClO₄)₂(aq) + H₂O(l)
Groups 1–2 (and Tl₂O) oxides react with water → hydroxides:
- Na₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → NaOH(aq); CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq); Tl₂O(s) + H₂O → 2TlOH(aq)
Important oxides: CaO (from CaCO₃; "limelight"); Al₂O₃ (corundum; ruby/sapphire; mp 2050 °C); ZnO (pigment, tires, sunscreens); PbO₂ (lead storage battery, oxidizer).

**2. Peroxides (O₂²⁻; O–O single bond):** groups 1–2 in pure O₂:
- 2Na(s) + O₂(g) → Na₂O₂(s) (Δ); 2Na₂O(s) + O₂(g) → 2Na₂O₂(s) (Δ); 2SrO(s) + O₂(g) → 2SrO₂(s) (Δ)
- 2K(s) + O₂(g) → K₂O₂(s) (2 mol K per mol O₂)
Strong oxidizers. **Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂)** from metal peroxides; bleach and disinfectant.

**3. Superoxides (O₂⁻; bond order 1½):** excess O₂:
- K(s) + O₂(g) → KO₂(s) (1 mol K per mol O₂)
Stability of peroxides/superoxides increases with cation size.

### Hydroxides (OH⁻)
Soluble: groups 1–2 (except Be, Mg) with water → hydroxide + H₂; safer via oxide + water:
- 2Li(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2LiOH(aq) + H₂(g); Ca(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g)
- Li₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → 2LiOH(aq); CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq)
Most metal oxides are base anhydrides. Insoluble hydroxides by adding NaOH to salt solutions:
- Mg²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Mg(OH)₂(s); Al³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Al(OH)₃(s); Zn²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Zn(OH)₂(s)
Avoid excess OH⁻ for amphoteric Al, Ga, Zn, Sn(II) hydroxides (dissolve to Al(OH)₄⁻, Ga(OH)₄⁻, Zn(OH)₄²⁻, Sn(OH)₃⁻).
Industrial: **NaOH** (chlor-alkali process, top-10 US chemical; 40 g dissolves in 60 mL water at 25 °C); **Ca(OH)₂** (cheap base from limestone); **Mg(OH)₂** (milk of magnesia).

## 18.10 Occurrence, Preparation, and Properties of Sulfur

(Note: in Chemistry 2e the body of §18.10 covers occurrence, preparation, and properties of elemental sulfur; detailed sulfur compounds such as SO₂/SO₃ and sulfuric acid manufacture are not part of this section's text.)

**Occurrence:** elemental deposits underground; sulfide minerals (Fe, Zn, Pb, Cu); sulfate minerals (Na, Ca, Ba, Mg); hydrogen sulfide in natural gas and volcanic gases; constituent of many proteins (essential for life).

**Preparation — Frasch process** (Texas, Louisiana): three concentric pipes —
1. Outer: superheated water (170 °C, 10 atm) forced down to melt the sulfur.
2. Inner: compressed air injected into the liquid sulfur.
3. Outlet: air forces the liquid sulfur mixture up to the surface.
Sulfur settles out in vats on cooling, 99.5–99.9% pure. Alternative source: H₂S recovered during natural-gas purification.

**Allotropic forms:** the stable form at room temperature contains eight-membered rings, true formula S₈ (equations conventionally use S for simpler coefficients).

**Chemical properties:** a group 16 nonmetal, behaving like oxygen. Forms binary sulfides (oxidation state 2−) with metals and exhibits multiple positive states. Oxidizes less electronegative nonmetals; oxidized by O₂, halogens, and strong oxidizers. Representative reaction with concentrated nitric acid:
- S(s) + 6HNO₃(aq) → 2H₃O⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) + 6NO₂(g)

## 18.11 Occurrence, Preparation, and Properties of Halogens

The halogens (group 17): F, Cl, Br, I, At. Too reactive to occur free; their compounds are widespread (chlorides most abundant). In seawater: Cl⁻ ~0.54 M, others <10⁻⁴ M. Sources: fluoride (CaF₂, Ca(PO₄)₃F, Na₃AlF₆); chloride (Great Salt Lake, Dead Sea, salt beds NaCl/KCl/MgCl₂); bromine (Dead Sea, brines); iodine (Chile saltpeter, brines, sea kelp).

### Preparation (oxidize halide ions; difficulty F > … > I)
- **Fluorine:** electrolysis of molten KHF₂ in anhydrous HF (F₂ at anode, H₂ at cathode; keep separated).
- **Chlorine:** commercially by electrolysis of aqueous NaCl (chlor-alkali process); byproduct of Na/Ca/Mg electrolysis. Chemically: MnO₂(s) + 2Cl⁻(aq) + 4H₃O⁺(aq) → Mn²⁺(aq) + Cl₂(g) + 6H₂O(l)
- **Bromine:** 2Br⁻(aq) + Cl₂(g) → Br₂(l) + 2Cl⁻(aq) (Cl₂ stronger oxidizer; essentially all domestic Br)
- **Iodine:** reduce NaIO₃ impurity in Chile saltpeter with hydrogen sulfite: 2IO₃⁻(aq) + 5HSO₃⁻(aq) → 3HSO₄⁻(aq) + 2SO₄²⁻(aq) + H₂O(l) + I₂(s)

### Physical states and solubility
F₂ pale yellow gas; Cl₂ greenish-yellow gas; Br₂ deep reddish-brown liquid; I₂ grayish-black solid (sublimes to violet vapor). Br₂ slightly soluble in water, miscible with nonpolar solvents. I₂ soluble in CCl₄/CHCl₃/CS₂/hydrocarbons (violet) and aqueous iodide (brown); I₂ acts as a weak Lewis acid toward I⁻:
- I₂(s) + I⁻(aq) → I₃⁻(aq) (triiodide)

### Reactivity / oxidizing strength
The easier the halide is to oxidize, the weaker the halogen as an oxidizer. With excess F₂ sulfur → SF₆; Cl₂ → SCl₂; Br₂ → S₂Br₂; I₂ does not react with sulfur.
- **Fluorine:** most powerful elemental oxidizer; oxidizes nearly all elements (water inflames → O₂, OF₂, H₂O₂, O₃, HF); wood/asbestos ignite; handled in Cu/Fe/Ni (passivating fluoride film); only element that reacts directly with xenon.
- **Chlorine:** strong but less active than F. Dark H₂+Cl₂ reaction is slow; light → explosive. Attacks most nonmetals (not C, N₂, O₂). Disproportionation in cold water: Cl₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → HOCl(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq); photochemical: 2HOCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 2H₃O⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) + O₂(g) (sunlight). P₄(s) + 10Cl₂(g) → 4PCl₅(l).
- **Bromine:** like chlorine but weaker oxidizer.
- **Iodine:** least reactive/weakest oxidizer; reacts with metals on heating; barely reacts with water; starch–iodide test gives deep blue (sensitive test for I₂).

### Halides of representative metals
Most binary halides are ionic; Hg, group-13 (3+), Sn(IV), Pb(IV) form covalent halides.
- Cd(s) + Cl₂(g) → CdCl₂(s); 2Ga(l) + 3Br₂(l) → 2GaBr₃(s)
- Stoichiometry controls state: Sn(s) + Cl₂(g) → SnCl₂(s); Sn(s) + 2Cl₂(g) → SnCl₄(l)
- With HX: Zn(s) + 2HF(g) → ZnF₂(s) + H₂(g); Cd(s) + 2HBr(aq) → CdBr₂(aq) + H₂(g); Sn(s) + 2HI(aq) → SnI₂(aq) + H₂(g)
- With carbonates/hydroxides: CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l); TlOH(aq) + HF(aq) → TlF(aq) + H₂O(l)
- Metathesis for insoluble salts (e.g., HgI₂ from KI + Hg(NO₃)₂).

### Interhalogens
Compounds of two or more different halogens: one heavier halogen bonded to an odd number of lighter halogen atoms. Geometries: IF₃ T-shaped; IF₅ square pyramidal; IF₇ pentagonal bipyramidal. Examples: ClF, ClF₃, ClF₅; BrF, BrF₃, BrF₅, BrCl; IF, ICl, ICl₃, IF₅, IF₇, IBr. F oxidizes I to 7+; Br/Cl reach only 5+ (7+ is the halogen limit). Many are unstable/reactive (halogen fluorides are stronger oxidizers than chlorides). **Polyhalide anions** (KI₃, KICl₂, KICl₄, CsIBr₂, CsBrCl₂) relate to interhalogens; I₃⁻ explains iodine's solubility in iodide solution.

### Applications
- **Fluorine:** C–H–F compounds replacing Freons; Teflon (–CF₂CF₂–); fluoride (SnF₂, NaF) in water/toothpaste (converts Ca₅(PO₄)₃(OH) to Ca₅(PO₄)₃F).
- **Chlorine:** bleaching pulp/cotton (via HOCl); CCl₄, CHCl₃, C₂H₅Cl; PVC; water disinfection.
- **Bromine:** dyes; NaBr/KBr sedatives; light-sensitive AgBr (photography).
- **Iodine:** tincture of iodine antiseptic; iodized salt (0.023% KI) for thyroid (deficiency → goiter); AgI cloud seeding; iodoform CHI₃ antiseptic.

## 18.12 Occurrence, Preparation, and Properties of the Noble Gases

Group 18: He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn. Once thought inert (filled valence shells), disproved in 1962 by Neil Bartlett (University of British Columbia).

### Occurrence and preparation
Present in small amounts in the atmosphere. Natural gas contains 1–2% He by mass (most commercial He from US gas fields). He is isolated from natural gas by liquefying the condensable components; Ar, Ne, Kr, Xe by fractional distillation of liquid air. Radon comes from radioactive elements in soils/minerals, accumulating in well-insulated buildings (lung-cancer hazard).

### Physical properties
Very low boiling/melting points due to weak London dispersion forces. Helium: the only substance that does not solidify on cooling at normal pressure (liquid near absolute zero, 0.001 K; solidifies only under high pressure); bp 4.2 K. (Liquid N₂ bp 77 K is a cheaper coolant.)

### Applications by element
- **Helium:** balloons/airships (nonflammable); He–O₂ diving mixtures (avoids nitrogen narcosis); inert welding atmosphere; cryogenic coolant; producing superconductivity in MRI/magnet superconductors.
- **Neon:** neon lamps/signs (red glow; modified by Ar/Hg vapor or colored glass).
- **Argon:** gas-filled light bulbs (inhibits tungsten vaporization); Ar–Hg fluorescent tubes; third most abundant gas in dry air.
- **Krypton:** Kr–Xe flash tubes for high-speed photography (≈1/150,000 s); forms KrF₂ (thermally unstable at room T).
- **Xenon:** reacts with fluorine to stable fluorides —
  - XeF₂ (excess Xe + F₂, heated then cooled; colorless crystals stable in dry air)
  - XeF₄ (stoichiometric F₂, excess Xe)
  - XeF₆ (excess F₂)
  Xenon oxides by replacing F with O: XeF₆(s) + 3H₂O(l) → XeO₃(aq) + 6HF(aq) (dry solid XeO₃ is explosive). XeF₆ and XeO₃ disproportionate in base to Xe, O₂, and perxenate XeO₆⁴⁻ (Xe at maximum 8+).
- **Radon:** forms RnF₂ (radiochemical tracer evidence).

Reactivity summary: stable Ar compounds form only at low temperatures; no stable He or Ne compounds are known; xenon readily forms stable compounds with F and O.
