## Chapter Introduction

The daily routines and products we use routinely involve chemistry. Making coffee, preparing eggs, and toasting bread all employ chemical processes. Commonly used items — including soap, shampoo, clothing fabrics, electronic devices, and automotive fuel — all rely on chemical substances and reactions. Whether you are aware or not, chemistry is part of your everyday world. Chemical substances and processes are essential to human existence: they provide nutrition, maintain hygiene and health, enable technology manufacturing, and support transportation systems, among numerous other functions.

## 1.1 Chemistry in Context

### Learning Objectives
- Outline the historical development of chemistry
- Provide examples of the importance of chemistry in everyday life
- Describe the scientific method
- Differentiate among hypotheses, theories, and laws
- Provide examples illustrating macroscopic, microscopic, and symbolic domains

### Historical Development of Chemistry

**Early chemical practice.** Humans began practicing chemistry when they learned to control fire and use it to cook, make pottery, and smelt metals. Subsequent developments included:
- Isolation of drugs from plants (aloe, myrrh, opium)
- Extraction of dyes (indigo, Tyrian purple)
- Creation of alloys (copper and tin combined to make bronze)
- Smelting techniques for iron production
- Extraction of alkalis from ashes
- Preparation of soaps by combining alkalis with fats
- Production and purification of alcohol through fermentation and distillation

**Philosophical and alchemical foundations.** Greek philosophers dating back to the sixth century BC proposed that water was the basis of all matter. The classical Greek postulate identified four elements: earth, air, fire, and water. Alchemists from Egypt, China, and the eastern Mediterranean combined chemical technologies with philosophical speculation, attempting to transform base metals into noble metals and create elixirs for disease treatment and life extension.

**Modern drug development.** The progression from alchemy to modern chemistry included isolating drugs from natural sources. Animal-derived compounds like progesterone (available as medicine in 1935) were extremely scarce and expensive. Chemist Percy Lavon Julian developed processes using soybean sources — isolating soy protein and focusing on soy sterols to rapidly produce progesterone, testosterone, and other hormones. He later developed cortisone synthesis, which previously required a 36-step process. Since soybeans were plentiful, these drugs became widely available, saving many lives.

### Chemistry: The Central Science

Chemistry is called "the central science" due to its interconnectedness with numerous STEM disciplines, intersecting with: biology and medicine; materials science and nanotechnology; forensics and environmental science; physics (chemical physics, nuclear chemistry); mathematics and computer science; biochemistry and molecular biology; chemical engineering; agriculture and food science; pharmacology and biotechnology; geology, oceanography, and atmospheric science; astronomy and cosmology.

Chemistry is defined as **the study of the composition, properties, and interactions of matter.**

### The Scientific Method

Chemistry is a science based on observation and experimentation. The scientific approach attempts to answer questions and explain observations using accepted procedures, building on a common element: knowledge based on experiments that can be reproduced to verify the results.

- **Hypothesis:** A tentative explanation of observations that guides information gathering and checking. Hypotheses are tested through experimentation, calculation, and comparison with others' experiments, then refined as needed.
- **Law:** A summary of a vast number of experimental observations that describes or predicts some aspect of the natural world.
- **Theory:** A well-substantiated, comprehensive, testable explanation of a particular aspect of nature. Theories are accepted when they provide satisfactory explanations but can be modified with new data.

The path from question and observation to law or hypothesis to theory — combined with experimental verification and necessary theory modification — constitutes the scientific method. Scientific progress is seldom neat and clean: it requires open inquiry and the reworking of questions and ideas in response to findings.

### The Domains of Chemistry

Chemists study matter and energy behavior across three domains:

- **Macroscopic domain:** The realm of everyday things large enough to be sensed directly by human sight or touch. Chemists observe and measure physical and chemical properties such as density, solubility, and flammability.
- **Microscopic domain:** Entities too small for direct observation but essential to understanding chemistry — molecules and atoms (visible with advanced microscopes), ions and electrons, protons and neutrons, chemical bonds. Most are too small to be seen even with the most advanced microscopes and may only be pictured in the mind.
- **Symbolic domain:** Specialized language representing macroscopic and microscopic components — chemical symbols (periodic table), chemical formulas, chemical equations, graphs, drawings, and calculations. A challenge for students is recognizing that the same symbols can represent different things in the macroscopic and microscopic domains.

**Water as an example of the three domains.** Macroscopically, water is liquid at moderate temperatures, freezes to a solid at lower temperatures, and boils to a gas at higher temperatures. Microscopically, water comprises two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom; freezing and boiling are explained through attractions between molecules. Symbolically, H₂O describes water at macroscopic or microscopic levels, and the abbreviations (g) for gas, (s) for solid, and (l) for liquid represent phases.

## 1.2 Phases and Classification of Matter

### Learning Objectives
- Describe the basic properties of each physical state of matter: solid, liquid, and gas
- Distinguish between mass and weight
- Apply the law of conservation of matter
- Classify matter as an element, compound, homogeneous mixture, or heterogeneous mixture
- Define and give examples of atoms and molecules

### Definition of Matter

**Matter is defined as anything that occupies space and has mass.** Solids and liquids are obviously matter; gases are also matter because they occupy space (as evidenced by balloons inflating).

### Three States of Matter
- **Solid:** Rigid, with a definite shape and volume nearly independent of pressure.
- **Liquid:** Flows and takes the shape of its container, forming a flat or slightly curved upper surface under gravity; volume nearly independent of pressure. In zero gravity, liquids assume a spherical shape.
- **Gas:** Takes both the shape and volume of its container.

**Plasma (fourth state of matter):** A gaseous state of matter that contains appreciable numbers of electrically charged particles. These charged particles impart unique properties justifying classification as a distinct state. Plasmas occur in star interiors and high-temperature environments such as lightning strikes, certain television screens, and analytical instruments for detecting trace metals.

### Mass versus Weight
- **Mass:** A measure of the amount of matter in an object. Determined with a balance comparing the object's mass with a standard mass, or by measuring the force required to accelerate it.
- **Weight:** The force that gravity exerts on an object, directly proportional to mass. Weight changes with gravitational force, but mass does not. An astronaut's mass is constant on the moon, but her weight is only one-sixth her Earth weight because the moon's gravity is one-sixth that of Earth.

### Law of Conservation of Matter

There is no detectable change in the total quantity of matter present when matter converts from one type to another (a chemical change) or changes among solid, liquid, or gaseous states (a physical change).

- *Beer brewing:* Original ingredients (water, yeast, grains, malt, hops, sugar) convert to beer (water, alcohol, carbonation, flavoring) with no loss of substance. When glucose transforms to ethanol and carbon dioxide during bottling, total mass is unchanged.
- *Lead-acid battery:* Lead, lead oxide, and sulfuric acid convert to lead sulfate and water with no change in total matter.

### Classification of Matter

**Pure substances** have constant composition; all specimens have the same makeup and properties regardless of source. Example: any sample of sucrose contains 42.1% carbon, 6.5% hydrogen, and 51.4% oxygen by mass, with identical physical properties.
- **Elements:** Pure substances that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical changes. Over 100 known elements exist; about 90 occur naturally on Earth (iron, silver, gold, aluminum, sulfur, oxygen, copper).
- **Compounds:** Pure substances of two or more elements that may be broken down by chemical changes. Examples: mercury(II) oxide → mercury + oxygen (by heat); sucrose → carbon + water (caramelization); silver(I) chloride → silver + chlorine (by light; basis of photographic film and photochromic eyeglasses). Properties of combined elements differ from their uncombined states (e.g., soft shiny sodium + yellow-green chlorine gas → white crystalline sodium chloride).

**Mixtures** are composed of two or more types of matter in varying amounts, separable by physical changes such as evaporation.
- **Heterogeneous mixtures:** Composition varies from point to point (Italian dressing, chocolate chip cookies, granite).
- **Homogeneous mixtures (solutions):** Uniform composition throughout (sports drinks, air, maple syrup, gasoline, salt dissolved in water). Composition can still vary while remaining the same class.

**Classification flowchart:** Does the matter have constant properties and composition? No → mixture (uniform throughout? No → heterogeneous; Yes → homogeneous). Yes → pure substance (can it be simplified chemically? No → element; Yes → compound).

### Atoms

An atom is the smallest particle of an element that has the properties of that element and can enter into a chemical combination.

- **Origin of the concept:** Greek philosophers Leucippus and Democritus (5th century BCE) first suggested matter is composed of atoms. John Dalton (1766–1844) supported this hypothesis with quantitative measurements in the early nineteenth century.
- **Scale:** A spider web thread is about 0.0001 cm in diameter; a single carbon atom is about 0.000000015 cm in diameter (~7,000 carbon atoms span one web strand). If a carbon atom were the size of a dime, ~150 million such "dimes" would cover a football field.
- **Mass:** One billion lead atoms weigh about 3 × 10⁻¹³ g; over 300 trillion (3 × 10¹⁴) lead atoms weigh only 0.0000001 g.

### Molecules

A molecule consists of two or more atoms joined by strong forces called chemical bonds; the atoms move around as a unit.
- **Elemental molecules:** Helium, neon, argon exist as individual atoms. Others form units: H₂, N₂, O₂, Cl₂ (pairs); P₄ (four atoms); S₈ (eight atoms).
- **Compound molecules:** Water (H₂O); carbon dioxide (CO₂); glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆).
- **Scale:** If a glass of water were enlarged to Earth's size, the water molecules would be about golf-ball sized.

### Chemistry in Everyday Life: Decomposition of Water and Hydrogen Production

Water can be broken down into hydrogen and oxygen by adding energy: **2H₂O(l) → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g)**. Oxygen is not flammable but supports combustion; hydrogen is highly flammable and a potent energy source. Fuel-cell vehicles (FCVs) run on hydrogen — more efficient than internal combustion, nonpolluting, with reduced greenhouse emissions and less fossil-fuel dependence — but are not yet economically viable, and current hydrogen production depends on natural gas.

### Chemistry in Everyday Life: Chemistry of Cell Phones

About 30% of naturally occurring elements appear in a typical smartphone: case/frame polymers (ABS, polycarbonate) of C, H, O, N plus structural metals (Al, Mg, Fe); toughened silica glass display strengthened with Al, Na, K and an indium tin oxide conductive coating; circuit board of silicon semiconductor plus Cu, Sn, Ag, Au and rare elements (yttrium, praseodymium, gadolinium); lithium-ion battery with Fe, Co, Cu, polyethylene oxide, polyacrylonitrile.

## 1.3 Physical and Chemical Properties

### Learning Objectives
- Identify properties of and changes in matter as physical or chemical
- Identify properties of matter as extensive or intensive

### Physical Properties and Physical Changes

A **physical property** is a characteristic of matter not associated with a change in its chemical composition (density, color, hardness, melting and boiling points, electrical conductivity). Some physical properties can be observed without altering state (density, color); others are only observable during a physical change (melting temperature of iron, freezing temperature of water).

A **physical change** is a change in the state or properties of matter without any accompanying change in the chemical identities of the substances. Examples: wax melting; sugar dissolving in coffee; steam condensing; magnetizing/demagnetizing metals; grinding solids into powder.

### Chemical Properties and Chemical Changes

A **chemical property** is the ability (or inability) to change from one type of matter into another (flammability, toxicity, acidity, reactivity). Examples: iron rusts in the presence of oxygen and water while chromium does not; nitroglycerin explodes easily while neon is unreactive.

A **chemical change** always produces one or more types of matter that differ from the matter present before the change. Examples: rust formation; explosion of nitroglycerin; copper reacting with nitric acid; combustion; food cooking, digesting, or rotting; oxidation of myoglobin iron during cooking (red → brown meat); banana ripening.

### Extensive vs. Intensive Properties

- **Extensive property:** Depends on the amount of matter present; value is directly proportional to amount (mass, volume, heat content). A gallon of milk has more mass than a cup.
- **Intensive property:** Does not depend on the amount of matter present (temperature, density, color). A gallon and a cup of milk both at 20 °C remain at 20 °C when combined. A drop and a pot of hot oil are at the same temperature (intensive) but the pot contains more heat (extensive).

### Chemical Hazard Diamond (NFPA 704 System)

The National Fire Protection Agency (NFPA) 704 Hazard Identification System uses a diamond with four sections, each rated 0–4 (0 = no hazard, 4 = extreme):
- **Top (Red):** Fire/flammability hazard (flash-point ranges)
- **Left (Blue):** Health hazard
- **Right (Yellow):** Reactivity/instability hazard (incl. detonation risk)
- **Bottom (White):** Special hazards (oxidizer, acid, alkali, corrosive, no water use, radioactive, etc.)

### Classification of Elements

Elements differ in their ability to conduct heat and electricity:
- **Metals:** conduct heat and electricity well
- **Nonmetals:** conduct poorly
- **Metalloids:** intermediate conductivities

The periodic table organizes elements with similar properties close together.

### Components of Measurement

Every measurement provides three kinds of information: a **magnitude** (the size, a number), a **unit** (a standard of comparison), and an implicit **uncertainty**. Numbers may be in decimal or scientific notation (e.g., 298,000 kg = 2.98 × 10⁵ kg; 0.0000025 kg = 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ kg).

**Units matter.** Without units, measurements are meaningless or dangerous: a phenobarbital dosage of "100" could mean 100 mg (therapeutic) or 100 g (lethal).

### Length, Mass, Temperature, Time
- **Length:** SI unit meter (m), defined as the distance light travels in a vacuum in 1/299,792,458 of a second. 1 m = 1.094 yd = 39.37 in; 1 km = 10³ m; 1 cm = 10⁻² m; 1 mm = 10⁻³ m.
- **Mass:** SI unit kilogram (kg); since May 2019 defined by fundamental physical constants (formerly a Pt–Ir cylinder). 1 kg ≈ 2.2 lb; 1 g = 10⁻³ kg.
- **Temperature:** SI unit kelvin (K), used without a degree symbol. Water freezes at 273.15 K (0 °C), boils at 373.15 K (100 °C); body temperature ≈ 310 K (37 °C). The degree Celsius (°C) is permitted; a Celsius degree equals a kelvin in magnitude but with a different zero.
- **Time:** SI base unit second (s). E.g., 3 µs = 3 × 10⁻⁶ s; 5 Ms = 5 × 10⁶ s. Hours, days, years remain acceptable.

### Volume (Derived Unit)

Volume is the amount of space occupied by an object. Standard SI unit: cubic meter (m³). Common derived units: 1 L = 1 dm³ = 1 × 10⁻³ m³ ≈ 1.06 qt; 1 cm³ = 1 mL = 1/1000 L (cc used in medicine).

### Density (Derived Unit)

**Density = mass / volume** — the ratio of the mass of a sample to its volume. SI unit kg/m³; commonly g/cm³ for solids/liquids and g/L for gases. Most liquids/solids: 0.7–19 g/cm³; air ≈ 1.2 g/L.

Selected densities: ice (0 °C) 0.92, iron 7.9, copper 9.0, silver 10.5, lead 11.3, gold 19.3 g/cm³; water 1.0, ethanol 0.79, glycerin 1.26, olive oil 0.92, mercury 13.6 g/cm³; (25 °C, 1 atm) dry air 1.20, O₂ 1.31, N₂ 1.14, CO₂ 1.80, He 0.16, Rn 9.1 g/L.

**Example 1.1 (density of a cube):** Lead cube, edge 2.00 cm, mass 90.7 g. V = 2.00³ = 8.00 cm³; density = 90.7 / 8.00 = 11.3 g/cm³. (Check: copper edge 0.843 cm, V = 0.599 cm³, mass 5.34 g → 8.91 g/cm³.)

**Example 1.2 (water displacement):** Iron: water rises 25.5 → 29.5 mL, V = 4.0 mL, mass 31.48 g → 7.9 g/mL. Wood (must be fully submerged): V = 3.0 mL, mass 1.95 g → 0.65 g/mL. (Check: foam → 0.230 g/mL.)

## 1.5 Measurement Uncertainty, Accuracy, and Precision

### Learning Objectives
- Define accuracy and precision
- Distinguish exact and uncertain numbers
- Correctly represent uncertainty in quantities using significant figures
- Apply proper rounding rules to computed quantities

### Exact vs. Uncertain Numbers

Counting is the only measurement free from uncertainty (provided the number of objects does not change during counting); results are **exact numbers**. Defined quantities are also exact: 1 ft = exactly 12 in; 1 in = exactly 2.54 cm; 1 g = exactly 0.001 kg. All other measured quantities contain uncertainty due to practical limitations.

### Significant Figures

Measured quantities are not exact (read a graduated cylinder at the bottom of the meniscus). Rules:
- **Nonzero digits:** always significant.
- **Leading zeros** (before the first nonzero digit): never significant — 0.00832407 has 6 sig figs.
- **Captive zeros** (between nonzero digits): always significant — 70.607 mL has 5 sig figs.
- **Trailing zeros:** significant only if to the right of a decimal point — 55.0 g has 3 sig figs.
- Ambiguous trailing zeros to the left of a decimal are resolved with exponential notation: 1.3 × 10³ (2), 1.30 × 10³ (3), 1.300 × 10³ (4 sig figs).

### Significant Figures in Calculations
1. **Addition/subtraction:** round to the fewest **decimal places** among the inputs.
2. **Multiplication/division:** round to the fewest **significant figures** among the inputs.
3. **Rounding rule:** dropped digit < 5 → round down; > 5 → round up; exactly 5 (last digit or followed only by zeros) → round to make the retained digit even; 5 followed by nonzero digits → round up.

Rounding examples: 0.028675 → 0.0287; 18.3384 → 18.3; 6.8752 → 6.88; 92.85 → 92.8.

- **Example 1.3:** 31.57 → 32; 8.1649 → 8.16; 0.051065 → 0.05106; 0.90275 → 0.9028.
- **Example 1.4:** 1.0023 g + 4.383 g = 5.385 g; 486 g − 421.23 g = 65 g.
- **Example 1.5:** 0.6238 cm × 6.6 cm = 4.1 cm²; 421.23 g ÷ 486 mL = 0.867 g/mL.
- **Example 1.6:** V = 13.44 dm × 5.920 dm × 2.54 dm = 202 dm³ = 202 L (3 sig figs).
- **Example 1.7:** Rebar 69.658 g; water 13.5 → 22.4 mL → V = 8.9 cm³; density = 69.658 / 8.9 = 7.8 g/cm³ (~iron's 7.9, so mostly iron).

### Accuracy and Precision

- **Precision:** how closely repeated measurements agree with each other.
- **Accuracy:** how closely a measurement agrees with the true or accepted value.

Archery analogy: clustered at the bull's eye = accurate and precise; clustered but off-center = precise, not accurate; scattered = neither.

Pharmaceutical dispenser example (target 296 mL): #1 precise (283.3–284.1) but not accurate; #2 accurate (293.9–298.3) but less precise; #3 both accurate and precise (295.9–296.1).

## 1.6 Mathematical Treatment of Measurement Results

### Learning Objectives
- Explain dimensional analysis (the factor-label method)
- Use dimensional analysis for unit conversions and multi-property computations

### Dimensional Analysis

Dimensional analysis rests on the principle that **the units of quantities must be subjected to the same mathematical operations as their associated numbers.** Identical units that divide cancel to 1.

- **Speed:** speed = distance/time → 100 m ÷ 10 s = 10 m/s.
- **Time:** time = distance/speed → 25 m ÷ 10 m/s = 2.5 s (m/m cancels, leaving s).

### Conversion Factors

A **unit conversion factor** expresses two equivalent quantities in different units. From 2.54 cm = 1 in: factors are (2.54 cm / 1 in) or (1 in / 2.54 cm). Choose the orientation that cancels the unwanted unit.

| Length | Volume | Mass |
|---|---|---|
| 1 m = 1.0936 yd | 1 L = 1.0567 qt | 1 kg = 2.2046 lb |
| 1 in = 2.54 cm (exact) | 1 qt = 0.94635 L | 1 lb = 453.59 g |
| 1 km = 0.62137 mi | 1 ft³ = 28.317 L | 1 oz = 28.349 g |
| 1 mi = 1609.3 m | 1 tbsp = 14.787 mL | 1 troy oz = 31.103 g |

Vertical jump: 34 in × (2.54 cm / 1 in) = 86 cm.

- **Example 1.8:** 125 g × (1 oz / 28.349 g) = 4.41 oz. (Check: 9.345 qt = 8.844 L.)
- **Example 1.9:** Antifreeze density. 9.26 lb × (453.59 g/lb) = 4.20 × 10³ g; 4.00 qt × (1 L/1.0567 qt) = 3.78 L = 3.78 × 10³ mL; density = 4.20 × 10³ / 3.78 × 10³ = 1.11 g/mL. (Check: 1.000 oz = 2.957 × 10⁻² L.)
- **Example 1.10:** 1250 km, 213 L gasoline. 1250 km × 0.62137 = 777 mi; 213 L × (1.0567 qt/L) × (1 gal/4 qt) = 56.3 gal; 777/56.3 = 13.8 mpg. Cost: 56.3 gal × $3.80 = $214. (Check: 51 mpg; $62.)

### Temperature Unit Conversion

Reference points are water's freezing and boiling temperatures at a specified pressure.
- **Celsius:** 0 °C (freeze) to 100 °C (boil); 100 intervals.
- **Fahrenheit:** 32 °F (freeze) to 212 °F (boil); 180 intervals.

The scales share no common zero; the relationship is linear (y = mx + b), not proportional. Slope m = (212 − 32)/(100 − 0) = 180/100 = 9/5; intercept b = 32.
- **°C → °F:** T_°F = (9/5 × T_°C) + 32
- **°F → °C:** T_°C = (5/9)(T_°F − 32)

**Kelvin** is an absolute scale where 0 K is the theoretical lowest temperature (no degree symbol). Water freezes at 273.15 K, boils at 373.15 K; the 100-unit span matches Celsius (slope 1). The 273.15 offset is experimentally determined, not exact.
- **°C → K:** T_K = T_°C + 273.15
- **K → °C:** T_°C = T_K − 273.15

- **Example 1.11:** 37.0 °C → K = 37.0 + 273.15 = 310.2 K; → °F = (9/5 × 37.0) + 32.0 = 98.6 °F. (Check: 80.92 °C = 354.07 K = 177.66 °F.)
- **Example 1.12:** 450 °F → °C = (5/9)(450 − 32) = 232 → 230 °C (2 sig figs); → K = 232 + 273.15 = 505.4 K → 5.1 × 10² K. (Check: 50 °F = 10 °C = 280 K.)
