## Introduction

The chemical reactions considered in previous chapters involve changes in the *electronic* structure of the species involved — the arrangement of electrons around atoms, ions, or molecules. Nuclear chemistry instead concerns changes to the nucleus itself: changes in atomic numbers, mass numbers, or nuclear energy states. Nuclear chemistry began with the discovery of radioactivity in 1896 (Henri Becquerel) and has become increasingly important for energy, medicine, geology, and many other fields.

## 21.1 Nuclear Structure and Stability

### Nuclear Structure Definitions
- **Nucleus**: the central part of an atom containing protons and neutrons (except hydrogen-1, which contains only one proton).
- **Nucleons**: protons and neutrons collectively, packed tightly together in the nucleus.
- **Atomic Number (Z)**: the number of protons in a nucleus.
- **Mass Number (A)**: the sum of the number of protons and neutrons.
- **Isotopes**: atoms with the same atomic number but different mass numbers.
- **Nuclide**: a single type of nucleus, identified by notation $^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$, where X is the element symbol, A is the mass number, and Z is the atomic number (example: $^{14}_{6}\text{C}$, "carbon-14").

### Nuclear Density
The nucleus has a radius of approximately 10⁻¹⁵ m, compared to the atom's radius of about 10⁻¹⁰ m. Nuclear density averages 1.8 × 10¹⁴ g/cm³ — vastly denser than ordinary matter (water = 1 g/cm³; iridium = 22.6 g/cm³).

### The Strong Nuclear Force
Protons repel each other electrostatically. The **strong nuclear force** holds the nucleus together, acting between protons, neutrons, and proton-neutron pairs. It operates only over distances less than ~10⁻¹⁵ m and is much stronger than electrostatic repulsion within the nucleus; outside the nucleus it is essentially nonexistent.

### Mass Defect and Nuclear Binding Energy
The **mass defect** is the difference between the calculated combined mass of a nucleus's constituent particles and its experimentally measured atomic mass; this mass difference converts to energy.

**Example (Helium-4):**
- Calculated mass: (2 × 1.0073 amu) + (2 × 1.0087 amu) + (2 × 0.00055 amu) = 4.0331 amu
- Measured mass: 4.0026 amu
- Mass defect: 4.0331 − 4.0026 = 0.0305 amu

The **nuclear binding energy** is the energy produced when nucleons bind together; it is also the energy needed to break a nucleus into its constituent protons and neutrons.

### Einstein's Mass-Energy Equivalence
$$E = mc^2$$
where E = energy, m = mass, c = speed of light in vacuum (2.998 × 10⁸ m/s).

### Calculating Nuclear Binding Energy
Conversion factors:
- 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
- 1 amu = 1.66605 × 10⁻²⁷ kg
- 1 MeV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹³ J

The numerical equivalence of atomic mass (amu) and molar mass (g/mol) lets mass defect in amu be expressed as g/mol.

**Example (Helium-4):**
- (a) Binding energy per mole: mass defect 0.0305 g/mol = 3.05 × 10⁻⁵ kg/mol; E = mc² = (3.05 × 10⁻⁵ kg/mol)(2.998 × 10⁸ m/s)² = 2.74 × 10¹² J/mol = 2.74 TJ/mol
- (b) Per nucleus: (2.74 × 10¹² J/mol) ÷ (6.022 × 10²³ nuclei/mol) = 4.55 × 10⁻¹² J/nucleus = 4.55 pJ
- (c) In MeV per nucleus: (4.55 × 10⁻¹² J)(1 eV / 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J) = 2.84 × 10⁷ eV = 28.4 MeV

### Binding Energy per Nucleon
$$\text{Binding energy per nucleon} = \frac{\text{Total binding energy}}{\text{Number of nucleons}}$$

**Example (Iron-56, among the most stable nuclides):** atomic mass 55.9349 amu; mass defect 0.5302 amu; binding energy 7.913 × 10⁻¹¹ J = 493.9 MeV; **binding energy per nucleon = 493.9 MeV ÷ 56 = 8.820 MeV/nucleon**.

The **binding energy per nucleon** measures relative nuclear stability. Nuclides with mass numbers around 56 (iron-56, nickel-58) show the highest binding energy per nucleon and are most stable.

### Chemical vs. Nuclear Energy Changes
The most energetic chemical reactions have enthalpies on the order of thousands of kJ/mol (mass differences in the nanogram range, 10⁻⁹ g). Nuclear binding energies are typically billions of kJ/mol (mass differences in the milligram range, 10⁻³ g). Energy changes in nuclear reactions vastly exceed those in chemical reactions, making mass changes during ordinary chemical reactions virtually undetectable.

### Band of Stability
A plot of neutron number versus proton number for stable nuclei shows ~250 stable nuclides falling within a narrow region called the **band of stability** (also belt, zone, or valley of stability).

**Neutron-to-Proton (n:p) ratio:**
- Lighter stable nuclei (low mass number) have ~equal numbers of protons and neutrons (n:p ≈ 1:1); e.g. nitrogen-14 (7p, 7n).
- Heavier stable nuclei have increasingly more neutrons than protons; e.g. iron-56 (26p, 30n; n:p = 1.15), lead-207 (82p, 125n; n:p = 1.52). The excess neutrons compensate (via the strong force) for increased proton-proton electrostatic repulsion.

### Nuclear Stability Patterns
Even/odd counts (number of stable isotopes):

| Protons | Neutrons | Stable Isotopes |
|---|---|---|
| Even | Even | 157 |
| Even | Odd | 53 |
| Odd | Even | 50 |
| Odd | Odd | 5 |

Nuclei with even numbers of protons, neutrons, or both are more likely to be stable.

**Magic Numbers**: nuclei with 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, or 126 nucleons are particularly stable (complete nuclear shells, analogous to noble-gas electron shells). **Doubly magic nuclei** (magic numbers of both protons and neutrons) — e.g. $^{4}_{2}\text{He}$, $^{16}_{8}\text{O}$, $^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}$, $^{208}_{82}\text{Pb}$ — are especially stable.

### Radioactive Nuclei
Nuclei to the left or right of the band of stability are unstable and radioactive; they spontaneously decay into nuclei within or closer to the band. All isotopes of elements with atomic number > 83 are unstable. Radioactive decay converts one unstable isotope (radioisotope) into a more stable one.

## 21.2 Nuclear Equations

A **nuclear reaction** is a change of nuclei that results in changes in their atomic numbers, mass numbers, or energy states. Nuclear reaction equations identify the nuclides, their mass numbers and atomic numbers, and other particles involved.

### Types of Particles in Nuclear Reactions

| Particle | Symbols | A | Z | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha particle | $^{4}_{2}\text{He}$ or $^{4}_{2}\alpha$ | 4 | 2 | High-energy helium nucleus (2p, 2n) |
| Beta particle | $^{0}_{-1}\beta$ or $^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$ | 0 | −1 | High-energy electron |
| Positron | $^{0}_{+1}\text{e}$ or $^{0}_{+1}\beta$ | 0 | +1 | Positively charged electron (antimatter) |
| Proton | $^{1}_{1}\text{H}$ or $^{1}_{1}\text{p}$ | 1 | 1 | Nucleus of hydrogen atom |
| Neutron | $^{1}_{0}\text{n}$ | 1 | 0 | Mass ≈ proton, uncharged |
| Gamma ray | $\gamma$ | 0 | 0 | Very high-energy electromagnetic radiation |

### Antimatter and Gamma Ray Production
When antimatter encounters ordinary matter, both are annihilated and their mass is converted into energy as gamma rays, per E = mc²: $^{0}_{-1}\text{e} + ^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \rightarrow \gamma + \gamma$. Gamma rays are high-energy electromagnetic radiation produced when a nucleus transitions from a higher to a lower energy state.

### Conservation Laws for Nuclear Equations
A balanced nuclear equation reflects a rearrangement of nucleons (not atoms). Two conservation laws hold:
1. **Conservation of mass number (A)**: the sum of mass numbers of reactants equals the sum of mass numbers of products.
2. **Conservation of atomic number / charge (Z)**: the sum of charges of reactants equals the sum of charges of products.

If all but one particle's A and Z are known, the unknown particle is identified by applying these conservation laws.

### Worked Example (21.4)
The reaction of an α particle with magnesium-25 produces a proton and a nuclide of another element. Identify the new nuclide.
$$^{25}_{12}\text{Mg} + ^{4}_{2}\text{He} \rightarrow ^{1}_{1}\text{H} + ^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$$
Mass number: 25 + 4 = A + 1 → A = 28. Atomic number: 12 + 2 = Z + 1 → Z = 13 (aluminum). Answer: $^{28}_{13}\text{Al}$.

Check-your-learning: $^{125}_{53}\text{I} + ^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \rightarrow ^{125}_{52}\text{Te}$ (electron capture).

### Historical Nuclear Reactions
- Polonium decay (Curies, 1898): $^{212}_{84}\text{Po} \rightarrow ^{208}_{82}\text{Pb} + ^{4}_{2}\text{He}$
- Artificial $^{17}$O synthesis (Rutherford, 1919): $^{14}_{7}\text{N} + ^{4}_{2}\text{He} \rightarrow ^{17}_{8}\text{O} + ^{1}_{1}\text{H}$
- Neutron discovery (Chadwick, 1932): $^{9}_{4}\text{Be} + ^{4}_{2}\text{He} \rightarrow ^{12}_{6}\text{C} + ^{1}_{0}\text{n}$
- Technetium synthesis (Segrè & Perrier, 1937): $^{2}_{1}\text{H} + ^{97}_{42}\text{Mo} \rightarrow 2\,^{1}_{0}\text{n} + ^{97}_{43}\text{Tc}$
- Controlled chain reaction (Chicago, 1942): $^{235}_{92}\text{U} + ^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow ^{87}_{35}\text{Br} + ^{146}_{57}\text{La} + 3\,^{1}_{0}\text{n}$

## 21.3 Radioactive Decay

**Radioactive decay** is the spontaneous change of an unstable nuclide into another. The unstable nuclide is the **parent**; the result is the **daughter** (which may be stable or decay further). **Radioactivity** (term coined by Marie Curie) is the emission of radiation from unstable nuclei.

### Types of Radioactive Decay

**Alpha (α) decay** — emission of an α particle (He-4 nucleus).
$$^{210}_{84}\text{Po} \rightarrow ^{4}_{2}\text{He} + ^{206}_{82}\text{Pb}$$
A decreases by 4, Z decreases by 2. Occurs in heavy nuclei (A > 200, Z > 83); increases the daughter's n:p ratio, moving toward the band.

**Beta (β) decay** — emission of an electron; a neutron converts to a proton plus an electron.
$$^{131}_{53}\text{I} \rightarrow ^{0}_{-1}\text{e} + ^{131}_{54}\text{Xe}$$
A unchanged, Z increases by 1 (neutrons decrease). Occurs in nuclides with high n:p ratio (above the band); decreases n:p.

**Gamma (γ) emission** — an excited nucleus decays to its ground state by emitting high-energy EM radiation.
$$^{60}_{27}\text{Co}^{*} \rightarrow ^{0}_{0}\gamma + ^{60}_{27}\text{Co}$$
A and Z unchanged; usually accompanies another decay mode.

**Positron emission (β⁺ decay)** — emission of a positron; a proton converts to a neutron plus a positron.
$$^{15}_{8}\text{O} \rightarrow ^{0}_{+1}\text{e} + ^{15}_{7}\text{N}$$
A unchanged, Z decreases by 1. Occurs in nuclides with low n:p ratio (below the band); increases n:p.

**Electron capture** — an inner-shell electron is captured by the nucleus, converting a proton to a neutron.
$$^{40}_{19}\text{K} + ^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \rightarrow ^{40}_{18}\text{Ar}$$
A unchanged, Z decreases by 1. Creates a vacancy filled by outer electrons, emitting X-rays. Occurs in proton-rich nuclei below the band; same nuclear effect as positron emission.

### Predicting Decay Mode from n:p Ratio
- Below the band (low n:p): positron emission or electron capture
- Above the band (high n:p): β decay
- Heavy nuclei with excess nucleons: α decay

### Radioactive Decay Series
Naturally occurring radioactive isotopes of the heaviest elements fall into chains of successive decays; all species in one chain form a **radioactive family / radioactive decay series**. Three major series: uranium, actinide, and thorium — each begins with a long-half-life parent and ends with a stable lead isotope. A fourth (neptunium) series is no longer significant due to short half-lives.

### Kinetics of Radioactive Decay
Radioactive decay is **first-order**, identical to first-order chemical reactions.

**Half-life** (t₁/₂): the time required for half of the atoms in a sample to decay.
$$t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} = \frac{0.693}{\lambda} \qquad \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}} = \frac{0.693}{t_{1/2}}$$

**Decay rate / activity:**
$$\text{decay rate} = \lambda N$$
where λ is the decay constant and N the number of nuclei. **Activity (A)** is the rate in nuclear decays per second, A = λN.

**Integrated rate law:**
$$N_t = N_0 e^{-\lambda t} \qquad t = -\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\!\left(\frac{N_t}{N_0}\right)$$
Using half-lives: fraction remaining = (1/2)ⁿ, n = number of half-lives elapsed.

### Radiometric Dating
Uses radioactive decay to determine the age of objects by measuring the ratio of parent isotope to daughter/decay products.

**Carbon-14 (radiocarbon) dating:** C-14 forms in the upper atmosphere from N-14 struck by cosmic-ray neutrons: $^{14}_{7}\text{N} + ^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow ^{14}_{6}\text{C} + ^{1}_{1}\text{H}$. Living organisms maintain a constant ¹⁴C:¹²C ratio; upon death ¹²C stays constant while ¹⁴C decays: $^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow ^{14}_{7}\text{N} + ^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$.
- Half-life of C-14: **5,730 years**
- λ = 0.693 / 5730 y = 1.21 × 10⁻⁴ y⁻¹
- $t = -\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln(\text{Rate}_t/\text{Rate}_0)$
- Accurate to ~30,000 y (reasonable to ~50,000 y max)
- Example (Dead Sea Scrolls): initial 13.6 disintegrations/min/g C, current 10.8 → age ~1,910 years
- Corrections: atmospheric ¹⁴C:¹²C shifted from fossil-fuel ¹²CO₂; tree-ring (dendrochronology) calibration corrects.

**Other methods:**
- U-238 → Pb-206: half-life 4.5 billion y; dates rocks/geological formations.
- K-40 → Ar-40 (positron emission + electron capture): half-life 1.25 billion y; measures Ar-40:K-40.
- Rb-87 → Sr-87: half-life 48.8 billion y.
- Oldest known rocks: Jack Hills zircons (Australia), U-Pb dated to ~4.4 billion years.

## 21.4 Transmutation and Nuclear Energy

**Nuclear transmutation** is the conversion of one nuclide into another, via radioactive decay or by reacting a nucleus with another particle. The first manmade nucleus (Rutherford, 1919): $^{14}_{7}\text{N} + ^{4}_{2}\text{He} \rightarrow ^{17}_{8}\text{O} + ^{1}_{1}\text{H}$.

### Particle Accelerators
Reaching the necessary kinetic energies requires **particle accelerators**, which use magnetic and electric fields to speed up nuclear particles in a vacuum (e.g. cyclotrons, linear accelerators). Neutrons for transmutation come from radioactive decay or nuclear reactors.

### Transuranium Elements
Elements beyond uranium (Z > 92); 22 officially recognized by IUPAC. Example sequence:
$$^{238}_{92}\text{U} + ^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow ^{239}_{92}\text{U} \xrightarrow{\beta^-} ^{239}_{93}\text{Np} \xrightarrow{\beta^-} ^{239}_{94}\text{Pu}$$
Half-lives: U-239 = 23.5 min; Np-239 = 2.36 days. Heavier Pu isotopes (Pu-240/241/242) form via neutron capture; long half-lives present storage challenges.

### Nuclear Fission
**Fission** is the breaking of a large nucleus into smaller pieces. Heavier elements with lower binding energy per nucleon decompose into more stable intermediate-mass elements with larger binding energy per nucleon, producing neutrons and enormous energy. First observed in 1939 (Meitner, Hahn, Strassmann) with U-235 bombarded by slow neutrons.
$$^{235}_{92}\text{U} + ^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow ^{236}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow ^{141}_{56}\text{Ba} + ^{92}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3\,^{1}_{0}\text{n}$$
Other product channels: $\rightarrow ^{90}_{38}\text{Sr} + ^{144}_{54}\text{Xe} + 2\,^{1}_{0}\text{n}$; $\rightarrow ^{87}_{35}\text{Br} + ^{146}_{57}\text{La} + 3\,^{1}_{0}\text{n}$. Fission products cluster around mass numbers 85–105 and 130–150.

**Energy release:** one mole of U-235 fissioning loses ~0.2 g of mass, releasing ~**1.8 × 10¹⁰ kJ per mole of U-235**. Fissioning 1 kg of U-235 yields ~2.5 million times the energy of burning 1 kg of coal.

**Chain reaction:** fission of U-235 yields two medium nuclei and 2–3 neutrons; these may induce further fission. A chain reaction occurs when the number of neutrons produced equals or exceeds the number absorbed plus those that escape.

**Critical mass:** the amount of fissionable (fissile) material that sustains a self-sustaining chain reaction. Below = subcritical; above = supercritical. Depends on material type, purity, temperature, sample shape, and neutron control.

**Atomic bombs:** contain several pounds of fissionable material (U-235 or Pu-239), a neutron source, and conventional explosives. U-235 design fires two subcritical masses together; Pu-239 design rapidly compresses a hollow plutonium sphere to exceed critical mass.

### Nuclear Reactors
Control and sustain fission chain reactions without explosion; fission heat boils water → steam → turbines → generators → electricity. Five required components:
1. **Nuclear fuel** — fissionable material in sufficient quantity for a self-sustaining chain reaction.
2. **Nuclear moderator** — slows neutrons to fission-enabling speeds.
3. **Reactor coolant** — carries heat to an external boiler/turbine.
4. **Control rods** — adjust neutron absorption to maintain a safe rate.
5. **Shield and containment system** — protects personnel and contains radioactive materials.

**Nuclear fuel:** natural uranium ore is 0.05–0.3% U₃O₈; natural uranium is ~99.3% U-238 (nonfissionable) and 0.7% U-235 (fissionable). Reactors require enrichment to ~**5% U-235 by mass** (insufficient for an explosion). Enrichment: **gaseous diffusion** (lighter ²³⁵UF₆ diffuses faster than ²³⁸UF₆ through hundreds of barriers), gas centrifuge, laser separation. Enriched UF₆ is solidified and fabricated into fuel assemblies of ceramic UO₂ pellets (modern reactors hold up to 10 million pellets; each pellet ≈ 1 ton coal or 150 gal oil).

**Moderators:** early reactors used high-purity graphite; modern US reactors use heavy water (²H₂O) or light water (H₂O); others use CO₂, beryllium, graphite. **Coolants:** all US reactors use water (often two loops to isolate radioactivity); others use molten sodium, lead, lead-bismuth, molten salts. **Control rods:** made of boron, cadmium, hafnium (neutron absorbers); e.g. $^{10}_{5}\text{B} + ^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow ^{7}_{3}\text{Li} + ^{4}_{2}\text{He}$. Inserting rods slows fission; full insertion shuts down the chain reaction. **Containment:** reactor vessel (steel, 3–20 cm), main shield (1–3 m high-density concrete), personnel shield, often a steel/concrete dome.

**Spent fuel:** only ~0.1% of fuel mass converts to energy; fission products accumulate and absorb neutrons, requiring fuel replacement after months to years. Spent fuel (unstable nuclei Z = 25–60, transuranium elements like Pu and Am, unreacted U) is highly radioactive and needs thousands of years to decay to safe levels.

### Nuclear Fusion
**Fusion** converts very light nuclei into heavier nuclei, releasing large energy.

Solar (net) reaction: $4\,^{1}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow ^{4}_{2}\text{He} + 2\,^{0}_{+1}\text{e}$. The He nucleus has 0.7% less mass than four H nuclei; the lost mass → ~**3.6 × 10¹¹ kJ per mole of $^{4}_{2}\text{He}$**.

Deuterium-tritium fusion: $^{2}_{1}\text{H} + ^{3}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow ^{4}_{2}\text{He} + ^{1}_{0}\text{n}$; mass loss 0.0188 amu → **1.69 × 10⁹ kJ per mole of $^{4}_{2}\text{He}$**.

Fusion requires temperatures ~**15,000,000 K or higher** (thermonuclear); molecules dissociate and ionize into plasma. Stars are powered by fusion.

**Fusion reactors** control fusion of light nuclei. No solid withstands such temperatures; two plasma-containment approaches are researched: **magnetic field containment** and **focused laser beam containment**. Goal: net energy gain. As of publication no self-sustaining fusion reactor operates; ITER (France) targets ~2027. The National Ignition Facility briefly produced >500 TW peak power and delivered 1.85 MJ, attaining ignition conditions for billionths of a second.

### Energy Comparison

| Process | Energy |
|---|---|
| U-235 fission | 1.8 × 10¹⁰ kJ/mol |
| ²H–³H fusion | 1.69 × 10⁹ kJ/mol of ⁴He |
| Solar fusion | 3.6 × 10¹¹ kJ/mol of ⁴He |
| Octane combustion | 5,471 kJ/mol |

Fusion releases significantly more energy than fission per particle mass, and both vastly exceed chemical energy release.

## 21.5 Uses of Radioisotopes

**Radioactive isotope (radioisotope):** an isotope with the same chemical properties as stable isotopes of the same element, but which emits detectable radiation.

**Radioactive tracer (radioactive label):** a compound in which one or more atoms are replaced with radioisotopes, allowing tracking by monitoring radioactive emissions — used to trace biochemical pathways or determine a substance's distribution within an organism.

### Medical Applications
**Radioimmunoassay (RIA)** detects the presence and concentration of antigens with extreme sensitivity (developed by Rosalyn Yalow and Solomon Berson, 1950s) — used for narcotics detection, blood-bank screening, early cancer screening, hormone and allergy diagnosis. The US performs >10 million nuclear medicine procedures and >100 million nuclear medicine tests annually.

Common medical radioisotopes:
- **Technetium-99m (Tc-99m)** — half-life 6.01 h; damaged heart, liver, and lung tissue preferentially absorb Tc compounds, located by detecting γ rays. Generated on-site from **Mo-99** (half-life 66 h) via β decay; a few µg of Mo-99 yields enough Tc-99 for ~10,000 tests. Mo-99 decays to pertechnetate (TcO₄⁻), separated from molybdate (MoO₄²⁻) by column chromatography.
- **Thallium-201 (Tl-201)** — concentrates in healthy heart tissue; used with Tc-99 to study cardiac tissue.
- **Iodine-131 (I-131)** — concentrates in thyroid, liver, and brain regions; monitors goiter and treats thyroid conditions (Grave's disease), liver tumors, and brain tumors.
- **Sodium-24 (Na-24)** — salt solution injected into the bloodstream to locate blood-flow obstructions.

**Radiation therapy** uses high-energy radiation to damage cancer-cell DNA, killing cells or preventing division: external beam therapy and internal therapy / **brachytherapy**. **Cobalt-60** (made by neutron activation of Co-59, then β decay to Ni-60 with γ emission): $^{59}_{27}\text{Co} + ^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow ^{60}_{27}\text{Co} \rightarrow ^{60}_{28}\text{Ni} + ^{0}_{-1}\beta + 2\,^{0}_{0}\gamma$; focused γ beams treat cancer while minimizing dose to nearby tissue.

### Research Applications
Radioisotopes study reaction mechanisms in plants and animals — fertilizer nutrient uptake, livestock digestion, growth and metabolism. **Carbon-14** elucidated the photosynthesis pathway: plants exposed to ¹⁴C-rich CO₂ were analyzed at intervals to reveal which organic compounds contained ¹⁴C and when.

### Commercial / Industrial Applications
- Film and thin metal-sheet thickness gauging via radiation penetration
- Metal flaw detection using cobalt-60 γ rays (like X-ray inspection)
- Pest control: γ-sterilization of male flies prevents offspring
- Food preservation by killing spoilage microorganisms

### Smoke Detection
**Americium-241 (Am-241)** — α emitter, half-life 458 years. In ionization-type smoke detectors, α emissions ionize the air between electrode plates; smoke impedes the ions, lowering conductivity and current, triggering the alarm.

## 21.6 Biological Effects of Radiation

### Ionizing vs. Nonionizing Radiation
- **Nonionizing radiation**: lower-energy emissions (light, microwaves) that speed up atomic/molecular movement (heating) without removing electrons.
- **Ionizing radiation**: emissions energetic enough to knock electrons out of molecules — α and β particles, γ rays, X-rays, and high-energy ultraviolet.

**Damage mechanisms:**
- **Direct**: radiation directly breaks chemical bonds or ionizes biomolecules.
- **Indirect**: radiation ionizes water (H₂O → H₂O⁺ …), forming hydronium (H₃O⁺) and highly reactive hydroxyl radicals that damage DNA, proteins, and enzymes.

Effects: **somatic** (whole body) and **genetic** (eggs/sperm) damage; most pronounced in rapidly reproducing cells (stomach lining, hair follicles, bone marrow, embryos).

### Penetrating Power and Ionizing Ability
Penetration (least → most): **alpha** (stopped by paper/skin; dangerous if ingested or inhaled) < **beta** (through skin/paper, stopped by thin metal) < **neutrons** (through concrete, stopped by water) < **gamma** (very penetrating; thick materials needed; dense lead shields well).

Relative ionizing power: alpha ≈ 10× beta and ≈ 20× gamma/X-rays; neutrons ≈ 5× beta.

### Units of Radiation Measurement
**Activity (decay rate):**
- **Becquerel (Bq)** (SI): 1 Bq = 1 disintegration per second.
- **Curie (Ci)** (traditional, common in medicine): 1 Ci = 3.7 × 10¹⁰ disintegrations per second.

**Absorbed dose:**
- **Gray (Gy)** (SI): 1 Gy = 1 J absorbed per kg of tissue.
- **rad** (radiation absorbed dose): 1 rad = 0.01 Gy = 0.01 J/kg.

**Biologically effective dose (dose equivalent):**
- **Sievert (Sv)** (SI): Sv = RBE × Gy.
- **rem** (roentgen equivalent for man): 100 rem = 1 Sv; rem = RBE × rad.

**Relative biological effectiveness (RBE)** — approximate relative damage: alpha ≈ 10; protons and neutrons ≈ 2+; beta and gamma ≈ 1. Conversion: number of rems = RBE × number of rads.

Key conversions: 1 Ci = 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq; 1 Gy = 100 rad; 1 Sv = 100 rem.

### Radon Exposure
**Radon-222** — α emitter, half-life 3.82 days, from the U-238 decay chain in soil and rocks. ~8× denser than air; accumulates in basements/lower floors. Average US indoor level ~1.25 pCi/L (~3× outdoor). ~1 in 6 US houses need remediation; high exposure can be as harmful as a carton of cigarettes a day; believed to cause >20,000 US deaths annually — leading cause of lung cancer in nonsmokers, second overall.

### Radiation Detection Devices
- **Geiger counter (Geiger-Müller)** — radiation ionizes gas in a tube; ionization rate ∝ amount of radiation.
- **Scintillation counter** — a scintillator luminesces under ionizing radiation; a sensor converts light to an electric signal.
- **Dosimeters** — measure personal exposure (electronic, film badge, thermoluminescent, quartz fiber).

### Health Effects of Radiation Exposure
**Background sources:** cosmic rays, radon (from ground uranium), medical exposures (CT, X-ray, radioisotope tests), airplane flights, consumer products, ingested/inhaled radionuclides (C-14, K-40, Sr-90, I-131). Total US average ≈ **620 mrem/year** (~310 mrem natural background; remainder medical/other).

**Regulatory limits (NRC):** nuclear worker 5,000 mrem/yr; public 100 mrem/yr; whole-body CT ≈ 1,000 mrem.

**Acute effects:** a short, large dose causes effects from blood-chemistry changes to death:

| Exposure (rem) | Effect | Onset |
|---|---|---|
| 5–10 | Blood chemistry changes | — |
| 50 | Nausea | Hours |
| 55 | Fatigue | — |
| 70 | Vomiting | — |
| 75 | Hair loss | 2–3 weeks |
| 90 | Diarrhea | — |
| 100 | Hemorrhage | — |
| 400 | Possible death | Within 2 months |
| 1000 | Destruction of intestinal lining; internal bleeding; death | 1–2 weeks |
| 2000 | CNS damage; loss of consciousness (minutes); death | Hours to days |

**Cumulative:** exposure has a cumulative lifetime effect. **LD₅₀:** ~500 rem gives ~50% probability of death within 30 days.

**Safety:** minimize exposure by shielding, increasing distance, and limiting duration; some background exposure is unavoidable.
