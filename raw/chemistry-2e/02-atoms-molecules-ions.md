## Chapter Introduction

The chapter opens with the detection of lung disease through breath analysis. Analysis of molecules in an exhaled breath can provide valuable information, leading to early diagnosis of diseases or detection of environmental exposure to harmful substances.

**Biomarkers** are substances that indicate an organism's disease or physiological state. Early detection of biomarkers enables diagnosis and treatment before conditions become serious or irreversible. Research demonstrates that exhaled breath contains molecules serving as biomarkers for environmental contaminant exposure and pathological conditions ranging from asthma to lung cancer. Scientists work to develop biomarker "fingerprints" for disease-specific diagnosis based on molecular composition in exhaled breath; mass spectrometry serves as the analytical method for identifying these molecules.

The fundamental chemical principle underlying the chapter: a molecule's identity is determined by the numbers and types of atoms it contains, and how they are bonded together.

## 2.1 Early Ideas in Atomic Theory

### Historical Background

Ancient Greek philosophers including Leucippus and Democritus proposed that matter consisted of small, indivisible particles called *atomos*. However, Aristotle's view—that matter comprised combinations of four elements (fire, earth, air, water) and could be infinitely divided—dominated for over two thousand years.

### Dalton's Atomic Theory Postulates

English schoolteacher John Dalton revolutionized chemistry with his hypothesis, first published in 1807. The postulates are:

1. **Matter composition**: Matter is composed of exceedingly small particles called atoms. An atom is the smallest unit of an element that can participate in a chemical change.
2. **Elemental uniformity**: An element consists of only one type of atom, which has a mass that is characteristic of the element and is the same for all atoms of that element. All atoms of an element have identical chemical properties.
3. **Elemental distinction**: Atoms of one element differ in properties from atoms of all other elements.
4. **Compound composition**: A compound consists of atoms of two or more elements combined in a small, whole-number ratio. In a given compound, the numbers of atoms of each of its elements are always present in the same ratio.
5. **Conservation principle**: Atoms are neither created nor destroyed during a chemical change, but are instead rearranged to yield substances that are different from those present before the change.

### Law of Conservation of Matter

Since atoms are neither created nor destroyed during chemical changes, the total mass of matter present when matter changes from one type to another will remain constant.

### Law of Definite Proportions

Also called the law of constant composition: all samples of a pure compound contain the same elements in the same proportion by mass. This observation by French chemist Joseph Proust is consistent with Dalton's postulate that element atoms in compounds exist in fixed ratios.

**Isooctane example** — Different samples show consistent carbon-to-hydrogen mass ratios:
- Sample A: 14.82 g C, 2.78 g H = 5.33:1
- Sample B: 22.33 g C, 4.19 g H = 5.33:1
- Sample C: 19.40 g C, 3.64 g H = 5.33:1

Note: samples with identical mass ratios are not necessarily the same substance.

### Law of Multiple Proportions

When two elements react to form more than one compound, a fixed mass of one element will react with masses of the other element in a ratio of small, whole numbers.

**Copper-chlorine example** — Copper and chlorine form two compounds:
- Green crystalline solid: 0.558 g chlorine to 1 g copper
- Brown crystalline solid: 1.116 g chlorine to 1 g copper

Ratio of mass ratios = (1.116 g Cl/1 g Cu) / (0.558 g Cl/1 g Cu) = 2:1. This reflects atomic composition: the brown compound is CuCl₂ (1 Cu : 2 Cl), the green compound is CuCl (1 Cu : 1 Cl).

**Worked Examples:**
- *Example 2.1*: A reaction showing two bonded green spheres and two bonded blue spheres converting to one green sphere bonded to one blue sphere violates Dalton's postulate because atoms appear destroyed rather than redistributed.
- *Example 2.2*: Compound A (4.27 g C, 5.69 g O) and Compound B (5.19 g C, 13.84 g O) demonstrate the law of multiple proportions. Oxygen-to-carbon ratios are 1.33:1 and 2.67:1, yielding a 1:2 ratio—indicating A and B are different compounds (likely CO and CO₂).

## 2.2 Evolution of Atomic Theory

Learning objectives: outline milestones in modern atomic theory; summarize/interpret Thomson, Millikan, and Rutherford experiments; describe the three subatomic particles; define isotopes.

### J.J. Thomson and the Cathode Ray Tube

In the late 1800s, English physicist J.J. Thomson investigated electrical discharges in low-pressure gases using a cathode ray tube—a sealed glass tube with most air removed, containing two metal electrodes. When high voltage was applied, a visible cathode ray appeared between the electrodes.

Observations:
- The beam deflected toward positive charges and away from negative charges.
- The beam had identical properties regardless of which metals served as electrodes.
- Using simultaneous electric and magnetic deflection, Thomson calculated the charge-to-mass ratio of the cathode ray particles.

Thomson concluded these particles were much lighter than atoms and proposed they were fundamental, negatively charged subatomic constituents present in all atoms—**electrons** (mass more than one thousand times less than that of an atom). The term "electron" was coined in 1891 by Irish physicist George Stoney from "electric ion."

### Robert Millikan's Oil Drop Experiment (1909)

American physicist Robert A. Millikan determined the charge on individual electrons. He created microscopic oil droplets electrically charged by friction or X-rays; an electric field could slow or reverse their gravitational fall.

Result: the charge on oil droplets was always a multiple of a specific charge: **1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C** — the fundamental charge of a single electron. Droplets carried 1×, 2×, 3× this charge, etc.

Electron mass calculation: with charge e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and Thomson's charge-to-mass ratio 1.759 × 10¹¹ C/kg:
mass = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C × (1 kg / 1.759 × 10¹¹ C) = **9.107 × 10⁻³¹ kg**.

### Atomic Models Before Rutherford

In 1904, Thomson proposed the **"plum pudding" model**: a positively charged mass with an equal amount of negative charge as electrons embedded throughout it. In 1903, Hantaro Nagaoka proposed an alternative **"Saturn model"** with a positively charged sphere surrounded by a halo of orbiting electrons.

### Ernest Rutherford's Gold Foil Experiment

Ernest Rutherford (New Zealand) used high-speed, positively charged alpha particles (α particles, = 2 protons + 2 neutrons) from radioactive decay of radium. With Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, he directed α particles at thin gold foil and observed scattering on a luminescent screen.

Results:
- Most particles passed through undeflected.
- Some were diverted slightly.
- A very small number were deflected almost straight back.

Rutherford: "It was almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you."

Conclusions:
1. The volume occupied by an atom must consist of a large amount of empty space.
2. A small, relatively heavy, positively charged body—the **nucleus**—must be at the center of each atom.

### The Nuclear Model of the Atom

An atom consists of a very small, positively charged nucleus, in which most of the mass is concentrated, surrounded by negatively charged electrons, so the atom is electrically neutral. Rutherford found other elements' nuclei contain the hydrogen nucleus as a building block and named this fundamental particle the **proton** (positively charged, found in the nucleus).

### Discovery of Isotopes

In the early 1900s, "mesothorium" (from thorium decay) was initially considered a new element but was chemically identical to radium despite a different atomic mass. English chemist Frederick Soddy realized elements could have atoms of different masses that are chemically indistinguishable—**isotopes** (atoms of the same element that differ in mass). Soddy received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1921.

### James Chadwick and the Neutron

The number of protons provided only half or less of the nuclear mass. In 1932, James Chadwick found evidence of **neutrons**—uncharged subatomic particles with mass approximately equal to that of protons. Neutrons explain isotopes: they differ in mass because of different neutron counts, but are chemically identical because of the same proton count.

## 2.3 Atomic Structure and Symbolism

### Atomic Structure Overview

An atom comprises a nucleus containing positively charged protons and neutral neutrons, surrounded by negatively charged electrons. The diameter of an atom is on the order of 10⁻¹⁰ m, whereas the diameter of the nucleus is roughly 10⁻¹⁵ m—about 100,000 times smaller.

### Unified Atomic Mass Unit and Fundamental Charge

The unified atomic mass unit (amu) is defined as exactly 1/12 of the mass of a single atom of the carbon-12 isotope and equals 1.6605 × 10⁻²⁴ g. The dalton (Da) is an equivalent unit; older literature uses "atomic mass unit (amu)."

The fundamental unit of charge (elementary charge) equals the magnitude of the charge of an electron, e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

### Subatomic Particle Properties

| Particle | Charge (C) | Unit Charge | Mass (amu) | Mass (g) | Location |
|----------|-----------|-------------|-----------|---------|----------|
| Proton | 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ | 1+ | 1.00727 | 1.67262 × 10⁻²⁴ | Nucleus |
| Neutron | 0 | 0 | 1.00866 | 1.67493 × 10⁻²⁴ | Nucleus |
| Electron | −1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ | 1− | 0.00055 | 0.00091 × 10⁻²⁴ | Outside nucleus |

### Atomic Number and Mass Number

- **Atomic number (Z)** = number of protons in the nucleus; the defining characteristic of an element. In a neutral atom, electrons = protons.
- **Mass number (A)** = number of protons + number of neutrons.
- Number of neutrons = A − Z.

### Ions and Electrical Charge

Atoms are electrically neutral when protons = electrons. When unequal, the atom is electrically charged and is called an **ion**. Atomic charge = number of protons − number of electrons. An **anion** forms when an atom gains electrons (negative charge); a **cation** forms when an atom loses electrons (positive charge).

### Chemical Symbols

Symbols contain one or two letters, with only the first letter capitalized. For example, Co is the symbol for cobalt, but CO is the notation for carbon monoxide.

### Isotopes

Isotopes are variants of an element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Isotope symbol: mass number as a superscript to the left, atomic number optionally as a subscript:

$$^A_Z X$$

Magnesium has three naturally occurring isotopes: ²⁴Mg (12 neutrons), ²⁵Mg (13 neutrons), ²⁶Mg (14 neutrons). Hydrogen-2 is deuterium (²H or D); hydrogen-3 is tritium (³H or T).

### Atomic Mass and Isotopic Abundance

Because most elements are natural mixtures of isotopes, the average atomic mass on periodic tables is a weighted average:

$$\text{average mass} = \sum_i (\text{fractional abundance} \times \text{isotopic mass})_i$$

No individual atom has this exact mass.

**Boron example**: 19.9% ¹⁰B (10.0129 amu) and 80.1% ¹¹B (11.0093 amu):
(0.199 × 10.0129) + (0.801 × 11.0093) = 1.99 + 8.82 = **10.81 amu**.

### Mass Spectrometry

Mass spectrometry (MS) is used across chemistry, forensics, medicine, and environmental science to analyze/identify substances. Samples are vaporized, ionized, and separated by mass-to-charge ratios using electric or magnetic fields. Each peak's height is proportional to the fraction of cations with the specified mass-to-charge ratio.

## 2.4 Chemical Formulas

Learning objectives: symbolize composition with molecular and empirical formulas; represent bonding with structural formulas.

### Molecular Formula

A molecular formula uses chemical symbols to indicate atom types, followed by subscripts showing the number of each atom type in a molecule. Subscripts appear only when more than one atom of a given type is present.

### Structural Formula

A structural formula conveys the same information as a molecular formula plus how atoms connect. Lines represent chemical bonds—an attraction between atoms or ions that holds them together in a molecule or crystal.
- **Ball-and-stick models**: show geometric arrangement; atomic sizes not to scale.
- **Space-filling models**: display relative sizes of atoms.

Methane (CH₄): one carbon bonded to four hydrogens.

### Diatomic and Polyatomic Elements

- H₂, O₂, N₂ exist as diatomic molecules.
- F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂ also form diatomic molecules.
- Sulfur commonly exists as S₈.

### Subscripts vs. Coefficients

- **H₂**: a diatomic hydrogen molecule (two bonded atoms).
- **2H**: two separate, unbonded hydrogen atoms.
- **2H₂**: two diatomic hydrogen molecules.

### Empirical Formula

An empirical formula gives the types of atoms present and the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms (or ions). Example: titanium dioxide TiO₂ (twice as many O as Ti; used as white pigment in paint and sunscreen).

### Relationship Between Molecular and Empirical Formulas

A molecular formula is always a whole-number multiple of an empirical formula.
- **Benzene**: molecular C₆H₆, empirical CH.
- **Glucose**: molecular C₆H₁₂O₆, ratio 6:12:6 → empirical CH₂O.
- **Acetic acid**: molecular C₂H₄O₂, ratio 2:4:2 → empirical CH₂O.

### Isomers

Isomers have the same chemical formula but different molecular structures.
- **Structural isomers** differ in how atoms connect. Acetic acid and methyl formate are both C₂H₄O₂.
- **Spatial isomers** differ in the relative orientation of atoms in space. Carvone exists as mirror-image isomers: S-(+)-carvone smells like caraway; R-(−)-carvone smells like spearmint.

## 2.5 The Periodic Table

### Historical Development

Dimitri Mendeleev (Russia, 1869) and Lothar Meyer (Germany, 1870) independently recognized periodic relationships among element properties. Both arranged elements by increasing atomic mass; Mendeleev went further by predicting undiscovered elements with properties like aluminum and silicon. Discoveries of gallium (1875) and germanium (1886) validated his predictions.

### The Periodic Law

Modern periodic law: the properties of the elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers. The modern table arranges elements by increasing atomic number with similar-property atoms in vertical columns.

### Table Organization

118 elements:
- **Seven horizontal rows** called periods or series.
- **18 vertical columns** called groups, labeled 1–18 (per IUPAC recommendation).
- Each box shows atomic number, symbol, average atomic mass, and sometimes name.

### Element Classification by Properties

- **Metals**: shiny, malleable (deformable without breaking), ductile (drawable into wires), good conductors of heat and electricity.
- **Nonmetals**: dull, poor conductors of heat and electricity.
- **Metalloids**: moderate conductors with both metallic and nonmetallic properties.

### Element Categories by Position

- **Main-group (representative) elements**: columns 1, 2, and 13–18.
- **Transition metals**: columns 3–12.
- **Inner transition metals** (two bottom rows): lanthanides (atomic numbers 57–71) and actinides (89–103).

### Named Groups and Families

- **Group 1 (Alkali metals)**: form compounds with one element atom and one hydrogen atom; similar chemical properties.
- **Group 2 (Alkaline earth metals)**: form compounds with one element atom and two hydrogen atoms.
- **Group 15 (Pnictogens)**: include nitrogen and phosphorus.
- **Group 16 (Chalcogens / oxygen family)**: include oxygen and sulfur.
- **Group 17 (Halogens)**: fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine.
- **Group 18 (Noble/inert gases)**: chemically unreactive.

### Hydrogen

Hydrogen is unique—a nonmetal with properties similar to both group 1 and group 17, often shown at the top of both groups or separately.

### Radioactive Elements

Elements 43 (technetium), 61 (promethium), and most elements with atomic number 84 and higher consist entirely of unstable, radioactive isotopes. Their atomic masses appear in square brackets (mass number of the most stable isotope).

### Discovery of Missing Elements

Ida Tacke and Walter Noddack discovered missing group 7 elements (43 and 75). Rhenium was one of the last natural elements discovered and remains the last stable element discovered. Francium, the final natural element discovered, was identified by Marguerite Perey in 1939.

## 2.6 Ionic and Molecular Compounds

### Ion Formation and Electron Transfer

Atoms can gain, lose, or share electrons during compound formation. When atoms transfer electrons, they become electrically charged ions; the nucleus and element identity remain unchanged—only electrons transfer.

### Cations (Positive Ions)

Main-group metals lose electrons to achieve the same electron count as the preceding noble gas:
- **Group 1 metals** lose one electron → 1+ (e.g., Na⁺).
- **Group 2 metals** lose two electrons → 2+ (e.g., Ca²⁺).

Example: neutral calcium (20 p, 20 e) loses two electrons → Ca²⁺ (20 p, 18 e, matching argon). Metal ion names match the metal atom name.

### Anions (Negative Ions)

Nonmetals gain electrons to match the following noble gas:
- **Group 17** gain one electron → 1−.
- **Group 16** gain two electrons → 2−.

Example: neutral bromine (35 p, 35 e) gains one electron → Br⁻ (35 p, 36 e, matching krypton).

### Predicting Ion Charges from the Periodic Table

Moving left to right, main-group elements form cations with charges equal to their group number. Moving right to left from the noble gases, elements form anions; the charge equals the number of groups moved left from the noble gases (Group 17 → 1−, Group 16 → 2−).

**Limitation**: transition metals have variable charges (e.g., copper Cu⁺ or Cu²⁺; iron Fe²⁺ or Fe³⁺).

### Monatomic and Polyatomic Ions

- **Monatomic ions** form from single atoms only.
- **Polyatomic ions** are electrically charged molecules—groups of bonded atoms with an overall charge that act as discrete units.

Common polyatomic ions:
- Ammonium NH₄⁺ · Hydroxide OH⁻ · Nitrate NO₃⁻ · Nitrite NO₂⁻
- Sulfate SO₄²⁻ · Sulfite SO₃²⁻ · Phosphate PO₄³⁻ · Carbonate CO₃²⁻
- Bicarbonate HCO₃⁻ · Acetate CH₃COO⁻ · Cyanide CN⁻ · Peroxide O₂²⁻
- Perchlorate ClO₄⁻ · Chlorate ClO₃⁻ · Chlorite ClO₂⁻ · Hypochlorite ClO⁻
- Chromate CrO₄²⁻ · Dichromate Cr₂O₇²⁻ · Permanganate MnO₄⁻

**Oxyanion naming**: -ate = standard oxygen count; -ite = fewer O than -ate; per- prefix = more O than -ate; hypo- prefix = fewer O than -ite. Example: perchlorate ClO₄⁻ > chlorate ClO₃⁻ > chlorite ClO₂⁻ > hypochlorite ClO⁻. The number of oxygen atoms is inconsistent across families (nitrate NO₃⁻ vs sulfate SO₄²⁻).

### Ionic Compounds

When a metal (loses electrons) reacts with a nonmetal (gains electrons), electron transfer produces ions held together by **ionic bonds**—electrostatic forces of attraction between cations and anions. An ionic compound contains ions and is held together by ionic bonds.

**Recognition rule**: when a metal is combined with one or more nonmetals, the compound is usually ionic. (Exception: AlCl₃ is not ionic.)

**Physical properties**: solids that melt at high temperatures and boil higher (NaCl melts at 801 °C, boils at 1413 °C). Not conductive when solid (ions can't flow); conductive when molten.

**Electrical neutrality**: in every ionic compound, total positive charge = total negative charge. Formula determination balances charges.
- Aluminum oxide: Al³⁺ and O²⁻ → 2 Al (6+) and 3 O (6−) → Al₂O₃.
- Compounds with polyatomic ions: treat ion as a unit, use parentheses. Calcium phosphate: 3 Ca²⁺ and 2 PO₄³⁻ → Ca₃(PO₄)₂.
- Sodium oxalate: Na⁺ and C₂O₄²⁻ in 2:1 → Na₂C₂O₄ (not the empirical NaCO₂, to preserve the polyatomic anion).

### Molecular (Covalent) Compounds

Molecular compounds are discrete, neutral molecules formed when atoms share electrons. **Covalent bonds** are attractive forces between the positively charged nuclei of bonded atoms and one or more shared electron pairs located between the atoms.

**Recognition rule**: covalent compounds are usually formed by a combination of nonmetals. (Shades of gray exist between ionic and molecular; exceptions occur.)

**Physical properties**: often gases, low-boiling liquids, or low-melting solids.

### Distinguishing Ionic vs. Molecular Compounds

- Metal + nonmetal(s) → typically ionic.
- Nonmetal + nonmetal(s) → typically molecular.

## 2.7 Chemical Nomenclature

Nomenclature is a collection of rules for naming things (e.g., NaCl, CaCO₃, N₂O₄). Focus: binary compounds, ionic compounds with polyatomic ions, and acids, following IUPAC guidelines.

### Ionic Compounds — Monatomic Ions

Name = cation name (metal) + anion name (nonmetal with -ide suffix).
- NaCl: sodium chloride · Na₂O: sodium oxide · KBr: potassium bromide · CdS: cadmium sulfide

### Ionic Compounds — Polyatomic Ions

Same pattern: cation name + anion name.
- KC₂H₃O₂: potassium acetate · NH₄Cl: ammonium chloride · NaHCO₃: sodium bicarbonate · CaSO₄: calcium sulfate · Al₂(CO₃)₃: aluminum carbonate

### Variable-Charge Metals (Stock Nomenclature)

Charge of the metal ion is specified by a Roman numeral in parentheses after the metal name.
- FeCl₂: iron(II) chloride · FeCl₃: iron(III) chloride · HgO: mercury(II) oxide · SnF₂: tin(II) fluoride

Legacy -ic/-ous suffixes (ferric/ferrous) are now largely abandoned.

### Ionic Hydrates

Names include a Greek prefix for the number of water molecules + "hydrate."
- CuSO₄·5H₂O: copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate · Na₂CO₃·10H₂O: sodium carbonate decahydrate

Greek prefixes: 1 mono-, 2 di-, 3 tri-, 4 tetra-, 5 penta-, 6 hexa-, 7 hepta-, 8 octa-, 9 nona-, 10 deca-.

### Molecular (Covalent) Compounds

Name the more metallic element first with a Greek prefix for atom count (omit mono- for the first element); name the second element with the -ide suffix and its prefix. Terminal prefix vowel may drop before a vowel (monoxide, not monooxide).
- CO: carbon monoxide · CO₂: carbon dioxide · SO₂: sulfur dioxide · SO₃: sulfur trioxide
- NO₂: nitrogen dioxide · N₂O₄: dinitrogen tetroxide · BCl₃: boron trichloride · SF₆: sulfur hexafluoride

Common names deviate: NO nitric oxide, N₂O nitrous oxide, H₂O water.

### Acids — Binary Acids

Hydrogen + one other nonmetal. Rules: hydrogen → hydro- prefix; nonmetal name + -ic; add "acid."
- HF(aq): hydrofluoric acid · HCl(aq): hydrochloric acid · HBr(aq): hydrobromic acid · H₂S(aq): hydrosulfuric acid

### Acids — Oxyacids

Hydrogen + oxygen + another element. Rules: omit "hydrogen"; start with anion root; -ate → -ic, -ite → -ous; add "acid."
- H₂CO₃: carbonic acid (carbonate) · HNO₃: nitric acid (nitrate) · HNO₂: nitrous acid (nitrite)
- HClO₄: perchloric acid (perchlorate) · H₂SO₄: sulfuric acid · H₂SO₃: sulfurous acid · H₃PO₄: phosphoric acid

Exceptions: H₂SO₄ is sulfuric (not sulfic); H₂SO₃ is sulfurous (not sulfous).
