## 7.0 Introduction

Carbon exists in multiple allotropic forms. Buckminsterfullerene (C₆₀), discovered in 1985, was named after architect and inventor R. Buckminster Fuller (1895–1983), whose signature design was the geodesic dome. Bonding theory — the topic of this chapter — explains how individual atoms connect to form more complex structures, using molecules such as C₆₀ to illustrate how understanding atomic connections lets scientists determine molecular architecture. Sections: 7.1 Ionic Bonding; 7.2 Covalent Bonding; 7.3 Lewis Symbols and Structures; 7.4 Formal Charges and Resonance; 7.5 Strengths of Ionic and Covalent Bonds; 7.6 Molecular Structure and Polarity.

## 7.1 Ionic Bonding

### Ion Formation
- **Ions** are atoms or molecules bearing an electrical charge.
- **Cation** (positive ion): forms when a neutral atom loses one or more valence-shell electrons.
- **Anion** (negative ion): forms when a neutral atom gains one or more valence-shell electrons.

### Ionic Compounds and Bonds
- **Ionic compounds** (salts): compounds composed of ions held together by ionic bonds.
- **Ionic bonds**: electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged cations and anions.
- Properties of ionic solids: crystalline structure; rigid and brittle; high melting/boiling points (very strong ionic bonds); poor conductors in solid state; readily dissolve in water; excellent conductors of electricity and heat when dissolved or melted (ions move freely).

### Formation of Binary Ionic Compounds
- Binary ionic compounds contain a metal (forming cations) and a nonmetal (forming anions).
- Metals have low ionization potentials and lose electrons easily (left side of periods, bottom of groups); nonmetals have high electron affinities and readily gain electrons (upper-right corner).
- **Electrical neutrality**: total positive charge on cations = total negative charge on anions.
- **Ionic formula** = simplest whole-number ratio of ions needed for charge balance. Example: Al₂O₃ = two Al³⁺ + three O²⁻; (2 × +3) + (3 × −2) = 0.

### Three-Dimensional Lattice
- The formula does not represent the physical arrangement of ions. Ionic bonds are isotropic (same in all directions), so ions arrange in tightly bound 3-D lattices rather than discrete pairs.
- NaCl: regular arrangement of equal numbers of Na⁺ and Cl⁻, each ion bonded to six oppositely charged neighbors.

### Lattice Energy (intro)
- It requires 769 kJ of energy to dissociate one mole of solid NaCl into separate gaseous ions:
  - NaCl(s) → Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g), ΔH = 769 kJ

### Electronic Structures of Cations
- Main-group atoms tend to lose all valence electrons, assuming the configuration of the preceding noble gas.
- Groups 1–2: group number = valence electrons = cation charge. Ca (group 2) → Ca²⁺, [Ar] (1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶), isoelectronic with Ar.
- Groups 13–17: cation charge = group number − 10. Al (group 13) → Al³⁺.
- **Inert pair effect**: heavy elements (groups 13–15) can lose fewer valence electrons than expected (Tl⁺, Sn²⁺, Pb²⁺, Bi³⁺) due to low energy of the valence s-electron pair.
- Mercury (group 12) forms both Hg²⁺ and Hg₂²⁺ (diatomic ion with Hg–Hg bond).
- **Transition metals**: form 2+ or 3+ cations by losing outermost s electrons first, then d electrons. Fe ([Ar]3d⁶4s²) → Fe²⁺ ([Ar]3d⁶, loses 4s) → Fe³⁺ ([Ar]3d⁵, loses 4s + one 3d). Though d fills last by Aufbau, the s electrons are removed first.
- Inner transition metals usually form 3+ ions.
- **Example 7.1**: Zn ([Ar]3d¹⁰4s²) → Zn²⁺ [Ar]3d¹⁰; Cr ([Ar]3d⁵4s¹) → Cr³⁺ [Ar]3d³. Check: K⁺ = [Ar]; Mg²⁺ = [Ne].

### Electronic Structures of Anions
- Nonmetal atoms gain enough electrons to fill outer s and p orbitals, reaching the next noble-gas configuration; anion charge = electrons needed to fill s+p.
- O (1s²2s²2p⁴) + 2e⁻ → O²⁻ (Ne configuration).
- **Example 7.2**: Se²⁻ = [Ar]3d¹⁰4s²4p⁶; I⁻ = [Kr]4d¹⁰5s²5p⁶. Check: P ([Ne]3s²3p³) → P³⁻ ([Ne]3s²3p⁶).

## 7.2 Covalent Bonding

### Definition
- **Covalent bonds** form between two atoms with similar tendencies to attract electrons (similar ionization energies and electron affinities); they result from mutual attraction of atoms for a shared pair of electrons (vs. ionic = electrostatic attraction of oppositely charged ions).
- Covalent compounds: weaker intermolecular attraction (molecules are neutral) → much lower melting/boiling points than ionic compounds; often liquids or gases at room temperature; softer solids; most are insoluble in water; poor conductors of electricity in any state.

### Formation of Covalent Bonds
- H₂: as two H atoms approach, their 1s valence orbitals overlap; each single electron interacts with both nuclei.
- **Potential energy / bond length**: shared electrons attracted to both nuclei stabilize the system; potential energy decreases as distance decreases; too close → nuclei repel → energy rises. **Bond length** = distance of lowest potential energy. For H₂: minimum at 74 pm, energy = −7.24 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

### Bond Energy
- Energy must be added to break bonds (endothermic); forming bonds releases energy (exothermic).
- H₂(g) → 2H(g), ΔH = 436 kJ; 2H(g) → H₂(g), ΔH = −436 kJ.

### Pure vs. Polar Covalent Bonds
- **Pure (nonpolar) covalent bond**: identical atoms (H₂, Cl₂) share electrons equally. In Cl₂ each atom contributes 7 valence electrons, shares 1 pair → 6 nonbonding + 2 bonding electrons (8 total, matching Ar).
- **Polar covalent bond**: different atoms share bonding electrons unequally; electron density shifts toward one atom → partial positive (δ+) on one atom, partial negative (δ−) on the other. The more strongly attracting atom gets δ−.
- HCl: electrons spend more time near Cl → Cl is δ−, H is δ+.

### Electronegativity
- **Electronegativity**: a measure of the tendency of an atom to attract electrons (electron density) toward itself; it determines how shared electrons are distributed. The more electronegative atom holds the δ− charge; greater electronegativity difference → larger partial charges.
- **Pauling scale** (proposed by Linus Pauling). Electronegativity increases left→right across a period and decreases down a group. Nonmetals (upper right) are highest; **fluorine is most electronegative (EN = 4.0)**; group 1 metals are lowest. Noble gases are excluded (full valence shell, do not share).
- **Electronegativity vs. electron affinity**: electron affinity is a measurable physical quantity (energy released/absorbed when a gas-phase atom gains an electron, kJ/mol). Electronegativity is a dimensionless, calculated quantity describing how tightly an atom attracts electrons in a bond.

### Electronegativity Difference and Bond Type
- |ΔEN| of two bonded atoms is a rough measure of bond polarity / bond type:

| Bond Type | Electronegativity Difference (ΔEN) |
|---|---|
| Pure (nonpolar) covalent | < 0.4 |
| Polar covalent | 0.4 – 1.8 |
| Ionic | > 1.8 |

- Examples: H–H ΔEN = 0 (nonpolar); H–Cl ΔEN = 0.9 (polar covalent); Na–Cl ΔEN = 2.1 (ionic).
- Best guide: bonds between two nonmetals are generally covalent; metal–nonmetal bonds are often ionic. Sharing ranges from completely equal (pure covalent) to not at all (ionic).
- Some compounds have both bond types: polyatomic ions (OH⁻, NO₃⁻, NH₄⁺) are held by polar covalent bonds, then form ionic compounds with oppositely charged ions. KNO₃ = ionic between K⁺ and NO₃⁻, covalent between N and O within NO₃⁻.

### Worked Example — Bond Polarity (amino-acid bonds, increasing polarity)
| Bond | ΔEN | Polarity |
|---|---|---|
| C–H | 0.4 | Cδ⁻–Hδ⁺ |
| S–H | 0.4 | Sδ⁻–Hδ⁺ |
| C–N | 0.5 | Cδ⁺–Nδ⁻ |
| N–H | 0.9 | Nδ⁻–Hδ⁺ |
| C–O | 1.0 | Cδ⁺–Oδ⁻ |
| O–H | 1.4 | Oδ⁻–Hδ⁺ |

Polarity increases as |ΔEN| increases; the δ− atom is the more electronegative. (Silicone check: C–C 0.0 nonpolar; C–H 0.4; Si–C 0.7; Si–O 1.7.)

## 7.3 Lewis Symbols and Structures

### Lewis Symbols
- A **Lewis symbol** (electron-dot symbol) shows valence electrons as dots around the element symbol (one dot per valence electron). Examples: Na (1 dot), Mg (2), P (5), Cl (7), Ar (8).
- **Cations**: fewer dots after losing electrons (Na → Na⁺ + e⁻, 0 dots; Ca → Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻).
- **Anions**: more dots after gaining electrons (Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻ with 8 dots; S + 2e⁻ → S²⁻ with 8). Total electrons conserved.
- Ionic-compound formation: NaCl: Na (1) + Cl (7) → Na⁺ + [Cl]⁻ (8). MgO: Mg²⁺ + [O]²⁻. CaF₂: Ca²⁺ + 2[F]⁻.

### Lewis Structures
- Lewis structures depict bonding in molecules and polyatomic ions, showing **bonding pairs** (shared) and **lone pairs** (nonbonding). A dash/line = a shared pair (H—H, Cl—Cl).
- **Single bond** = one shared pair (Cl₂: single bond + three lone pairs per Cl; HBr). **Double bond** = two shared pairs (C=O in CH₂O; C=C in C₂H₄). **Triple bond** = three shared pairs (C≡O in CO; C≡N in CN⁻).

### The Octet Rule
- **Octet rule**: the tendency of main-group atoms to form enough bonds to obtain eight valence electrons. Applies primarily to second-period nonmetals (C, N, O, F). Hydrogen is an exception (needs only 2). Transition/inner-transition elements do not follow it.
- Bonding patterns: group 14 (C) — 4 valence e⁻, 4 bonds (CCl₄, SiH₄); group 15 (N) — 5 valence e⁻ (1 lone pair + 3 unpaired), 3 bonds (NH₃); group 16 (O) — 6 valence e⁻ (2 lone pairs), 2 bonds (H₂O); group 17 (halogens) — 7 valence e⁻ (3 lone pairs), 1 bond (HF).

### Five-Step Procedure for Writing Lewis Structures
1. **Total valence electrons.** Neutral molecule: sum atom valences (SiH₄ = 4 + 4×1 = 8; H₃CCH₃ = 2×4 + 6×1 = 14; HCCH = 2×4 + 2×1 = 10; NH₃ = 5 + 3×1 = 8). Anion: add one e⁻ per negative charge (CHO₂⁻ = 4 + 1 + 2×6 + 1 = 18). Cation: subtract one e⁻ per positive charge (NO⁺ = 5 + 6 − 1 = 10).
2. **Skeleton**: least electronegative atom is central (never H; never F). Single-bond each terminal atom to the center. Polyatomic ion: brackets + charge.
3. **Lone pairs on terminal atoms** to complete their octets (except H).
4. **Remaining electrons on central atom** as lone pairs.
5. **Rearrange to multiple bonds**: if the central atom lacks an octet, move terminal lone pairs into double/triple bonds.
- Worked: HCN → H–C≡N; H₃CCH₃ (no multiple bonds); HCCH → H–C≡C–H; NH₃ (one lone pair on N); CHO₂⁻ (one C=O, one C–O, H on C); NO⁺ → N≡O; OF₂ (O central, two lone pairs).

### Exceptions to the Octet Rule
- **Odd-electron molecules (free radicals)**: odd total valence electrons → unpaired electron, cannot give every atom an octet. NO: 5 + 6 = 11 e⁻ (odd) → N=O with an unpaired electron on N; reactive.
- **Electron-deficient molecules**: central atom from groups 2 or 13 cannot reach a full octet. BeH₂: two Be–H bonds, Be has only 4 electrons. BF₃: three B–F single bonds, B has only 6 electrons; B–F bond lengths confirm single bonds (not double); highly reactive — accepts an electron pair. BF₃ + NH₃ → F₃B–NH₃ (N lone pair forms a dative bond, B reaches octet).
- **Hypervalent / expanded-octet molecules**: period 3+ elements (n ≥ 3) have empty valence d orbitals, allowing more than four electron pairs. PCl₅ (5 bonds), SF₆ (6 bonds), IF₅ (5 bonds + 1 lone pair, 12 e⁻), XeF₄ (4 bonds + 2 lone pairs, 12 e⁻), XeF₂ (22 e⁻: 2 bonds + 3 lone pairs on Xe), XeF₆ (50 e⁻: 6 bonds + 1 lone pair). Interhalogens: BrCl₃, ICl₄⁻.

## 7.4 Formal Charges and Resonance

### Formal Charge
- **Formal charge** = the hypothetical charge an atom would have if bonding electrons were divided evenly between bonded atoms.
- **Equation**: formal charge = (valence electrons of free atom) − (lone-pair electrons) − ½(bonding electrons).
- Sum of formal charges = 0 for a neutral molecule; = the ion charge for an ion. Formal charge is a bookkeeping device, not the actual atomic charge.

### Using Formal Charge to Predict Structure
1. A structure with all formal charges zero is preferable.
2. If nonzero, prefer the smallest formal charges.
3. Prefer adjacent formal charges that are zero or of opposite sign.
4. Among similar distributions, prefer negative formal charges on the more electronegative atoms.
- **CO₂**: O=C=O preferred (all formal charges 0).
- **SCN⁻**: N=C=S (−1, 0, 0) preferred over N≡C–S (−2, +1, 0) and N=S=C (−2, +2, +1) — least electronegative atom (C) central, negative charge on most electronegative (N).
- **N₂O**: N≡N–O (0, +1, −1) preferred over N=N=O (−1, +2, −1).
- **NO₂⁻**: ONO⁻ arrangement preferred over NOO⁻.

### Resonance
- When two or more Lewis structures with the same atomic arrangement can be drawn, the actual electron distribution is an **average** of all of them.
- Individual structures = **resonance forms**; the average = **resonance hybrid**; a double-headed arrow (↔) links resonance forms.
- A resonance hybrid never has the structure of any single form and does not fluctuate between forms — it is always the average.
- **NO₂⁻**: two resonance forms (double bond alternates); both N–O bonds are experimentally identical in length/strength. **CO₃²⁻**: three resonance forms; all three C–O bonds equivalent.

## 7.5 Strengths of Ionic and Covalent Bonds

### Covalent Bond Strength (Bond Energy)
- **Bond energy / bond dissociation energy**: the energy required to break a specific covalent bond in one mole of gaseous molecules. For diatomic XY: XY(g) → X(g) + Y(g), D(X–Y) = ΔH°. H–H = 436 kJ/mol.
- **Bond order**: bond strength increases as the number of shared electron pairs increases; as bond strength increases, bond length decreases. Triple > double > single (strength); triple < double < single (length).
- C–X examples: C–F 439, C–Cl 330, C–Br 275 kJ/mol — strength decreases down a group.
- Bond energies (kJ/mol): H–H 436, H–F 569, H–Cl 432, C–H 415, C–C 345, C=C 611, C≡C 837, C–O 350, C=O 741, C≡O 1080, N≡N 946, O=O 498.
- Length (Å) / energy (kJ/mol): C–C 1.54/345, C=C 1.34/611, C≡C 1.20/837; C–O 1.43/350, C=O 1.23/741, C≡O 1.13/1080.

### Using Bond Energies to Estimate ΔH
- **ΔH = Σ D(bonds broken) − Σ D(bonds formed)**. All D values positive. Exothermic (ΔH < 0): products have stronger bonds. Endothermic (ΔH > 0): products have weaker bonds.
- Example H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl: broken 436 + 243 = 679; formed 2×432 = 864; ΔH = 679 − 864 = −185 kJ (exothermic).
- Methanol: CO + 2H₂ → CH₃OH; broken C≡O 1080 + 2×436 = 1952; formed 3×C–H (3×415=1245) + C–O 350 + O–H 464 = 2059; ΔH = 1952 − 2059 = −107 kJ (actual ΔHf° = −90.5 kJ — average D values give only estimates).

### Ionic Bond Strength (Lattice Energy)
- **Lattice energy (ΔH_lattice)**: energy required to separate one mole of an ionic solid into its gaseous ions. MX(s) → M^(n+)(g) + X^(n−)(g). Positive (endothermic) by convention. NaCl = 769 kJ/mol.
- **Equation (from Coulomb's law)**: ΔH_lattice = C(Z⁺)(Z⁻)/R₀, where C = a constant (depends on crystal structure), Z⁺ and Z⁻ = ion charges, R₀ = interionic distance (sum of ionic radii).
- **Charge effect**: doubling both charges quadruples lattice energy. LiF (Z=1) 1023 kJ/mol vs. MgO (Z=2) 3900 kJ/mol.
- **Size effect**: smaller ions → larger lattice energy. MgF₂ 2957 vs. MgI₂ 2327 kJ/mol. Larger magnitude → more stable compound (higher melting point, harder).

### Born-Haber Cycle
- A thermochemical cycle applying Hess's law to compute lattice energy from steps: ΔH_s° (sublimation of metal), IE (ionization energy of metal), ½D (half the nonmetal bond dissociation energy), EA (electron affinity of nonmetal), −ΔH_lattice (ions combining).
- **Overall**: ΔHf° = ΔH_s° + ½D + IE + EA − ΔH_lattice.
- **CsF example**: ΔH_s° = 76.5; ½D = 79.4; IE = 375.7; EA = −328.2; ΔHf° = −553.5 kJ/mol. Solve: ΔH_lattice = 76.5 + 79.4 + 375.7 + (−328.2) − (−553.5) = 756.9 kJ/mol.

### Ionic vs. Covalent Magnitudes
- Lattice energies ~600–4000 kJ/mol; covalent single-bond dissociation energies ~150–400 kJ/mol. Not directly comparable — lattice energy is the sum of many electrostatic interactions in an extended crystal, while bond energy is a two-atom interaction.

## 7.6 Molecular Structure and Polarity

### Geometry Fundamentals
- **Bond angle**: the angle between two bonds sharing a common atom (degrees). **Bond distance / bond length**: distance between nuclei of two bonded atoms (Å, where 1 Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m; 1 pm = 10⁻¹² m; 100 pm = 1 Å).

### VSEPR Theory
- **Valence Shell Electron-Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) Theory** predicts structure and bond angles from the number of bonds and lone pairs in a Lewis structure. Electron pairs in the central atom's valence shell adopt the arrangement that minimizes repulsion by maximizing distance.
- Regions of electron density: **bonding pairs** and **lone pairs**. A single, double, or triple bond each counts as one region of electron density.

### Electron-Pair Geometries
| Electron Regions | Electron-Pair Geometry | Bond Angles |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Linear | 180° |
| 3 | Trigonal planar | 120° |
| 4 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° |
| 5 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 90° and 120° |
| 6 | Octahedral | 90° |

### Electron-Pair Geometry vs. Molecular Structure
- **Electron-pair geometry**: arrangement of all regions (bonds + lone pairs). **Molecular structure**: arrangement of atoms only. They match when there are no lone pairs; they differ when lone pairs are present.
- CH₄ (4 bonds, 0 lone pairs): both tetrahedral. NH₃ (3 bonds, 1 lone pair): electron-pair geometry tetrahedral, molecular structure trigonal pyramidal.

### Lone-Pair Effects on Angles
- Repulsion order (greatest → least): lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bonding pair > bonding pair–bonding pair. Spatial order (largest → smallest): lone pair > triple bond > double bond > single bond.
- Lone pairs push bonding pairs together → angles shrink below ideal (NH₃ H–N–H slightly < 109.5°).
- Trigonal bipyramidal: axial positions (90°), equatorial positions (120°, more room); lone pairs always occupy equatorial positions.
- Octahedral with two lone pairs: lone pairs sit 180° apart (opposite sides) → square planar molecular structure.

### Procedure for Predicting Molecular Structure
1. Write the Lewis structure. 2. Count regions of electron density (lone pairs + bonds) on the central atom. 3. Identify electron-pair geometry from the number of regions. 4. Use the number of lone pairs to determine molecular structure (minimize repulsions).

### Molecular Polarity and Dipole Moments
- **Bond dipole moment**: arises from a polar covalent bond. Magnitude μ = Qr (Q = magnitude of partial charges, r = distance between charges). Vector arrow points from the less electronegative atom toward the more electronegative atom; a plus sign marks the δ+ end.
- **Molecular dipole moment**: net charge separation for the whole molecule. Polar (a dipole) if nonzero; nonpolar if zero.
- Diatomics: homonuclear (Br₂, N₂) have zero dipole moment; heteronuclear (CO, HF) have nonzero.
- Polyatomics: if bond moments cancel (vector sum = 0) the molecule is nonpolar. CO₂ (linear): C=O moments cancel → nonpolar despite polar bonds. H₂O (bent, two lone pairs on O): moments do not cancel → polar. CH₃Cl (tetrahedral): moments sum toward Cl → polar. Symmetric BF₃, CH₄, PF₅, SF₆ → nonpolar (zero net moment). Asymmetric H₂S, NH₃ → polar (incomplete cancellation).
- **Criteria for a polar molecule** (both required): (1) at least one polar covalent bond; (2) a structure in which the vector sum of bond dipole moments does not cancel.
- Polar molecules align in an electric field (δ+ end toward the negative plate, δ− toward the positive plate); nonpolar molecules are not attracted by charged objects. Polar solvents dissolve polar solutes; nonpolar solvents dissolve nonpolar solutes ("like dissolves like").
