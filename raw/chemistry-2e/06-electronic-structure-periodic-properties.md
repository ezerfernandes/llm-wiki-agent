## Chapter Introduction

In 1054, Chinese astronomers recorded the appearance of a "guest star" in the sky, visible even during the day, which then disappeared slowly over the next two years. This supernova explosion produced the Crab Nebula, which continues emitting energy today through visible light, infrared radiation, X-rays, and other electromagnetic forms. Studying this radiation helps scientists identify the elements present in distant celestial objects (by observing the wavelengths of light emitted) and relates directly to the electronic structure of atoms. This chapter explains how the wavelengths of emitted light let scientists determine elemental composition, demonstrating practical applications of atomic physics principles.

## 6.1 Electromagnetic Energy

### Learning Objectives
Understand wave behavior (traveling and standing waves); describe light's wave nature; calculate light-wave properties; distinguish between line and continuous emission spectra; describe light's particle nature.

### Historical Context
Newton demonstrated white light comprises rainbow colors and explained light using a corpuscular (particle) model. Huygens explained optical phenomena through wave theory. Young demonstrated interference patterns contradicting particle models. Maxwell's electromagnetic radiation theory established light as waves. By the late 1800s, scientists divided physics into two domains: particles following Newton's laws (classical mechanics) and electromagnetic waves governed by Maxwell's equations (classical electrodynamics). Unexplained phenomena led to wave-particle duality, fundamentally connecting particles and waves.

### Waves
A wave is "an oscillation or periodic movement that can transport energy from one point in space to another." Electromagnetic waves consist of electric and magnetic fields oscillating perpendicular to each other and to the travel direction, moving through vacuum at constant speed **c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s**.

**Wave properties:**
- **Wavelength (λ)**: distance between consecutive peaks (or troughs), measured in meters. Electromagnetic wavelengths range from kilometers (10³ m) to picometers (10⁻¹² m).
- **Frequency (ν)**: number of wave cycles passing a point per unit time, expressed in hertz (Hz, cycles per second). 1 MHz = 1 × 10⁶ Hz; 1 GHz = 1 × 10⁹ Hz.
- **Amplitude**: magnitude of wave displacement, half the peak-to-trough height; relates to intensity (brightness for light, loudness for sound).

**Fundamental wave equation** (electromagnetic radiation in vacuum):

> **c = λν = 2.998 × 10⁸ m·s⁻¹**

Wavelength and frequency are inversely proportional: as λ increases, ν decreases.

### Electromagnetic Spectrum
Ranges from gamma radiation (~10⁻¹² m) through X-rays, ultraviolet, visible light (~10⁻⁷ to 10⁻⁶ m), infrared, terahertz, microwave, to broadcast/wireless radio (~10³ m). Visible light is only a small portion. Different regions use different convenient units: radio in MHz/GHz, visible in nm or angstroms.

**Example 6.1 — Frequency and Wavelength:**
Sodium streetlight, yellow light at λ = 589 nm:
ν = c/λ = (2.998 × 10⁸ m·s⁻¹)/(589 × 10⁻⁹ m) = 5.09 × 10¹⁴ s⁻¹
Cellular signal at 850 MHz: λ = c/ν = (2.998 × 10⁸ m·s⁻¹)/(850 × 10⁶ Hz) = 0.353 m = 35.3 cm

### Wireless Communication
Radio frequency region spans 3 kHz–300 GHz. AM radio (540–2830 kHz) varies wave amplitude to carry information; FM radio (87.5–108.0 MHz) keeps amplitude constant and varies frequency. Other technologies: 4G cellular (~880 MHz), GPS (1.228 and 1.575 GHz), Wi-Fi (2.4–5 GHz), highway toll sensors (5.8 GHz).

### Wave Interference
When waves meet, they interfere. Light through two closely-spaced slits produces fringe patterns; shorter wavelengths produce closer spacing. Each slit acts as a wave source. Destructive interference (dark regions) occurs where peaks meet troughs; constructive interference (bright regions) where peaks meet peaks (or troughs meet troughs). Such patterns cannot be explained by particles under classical mechanics.

### Standing Waves
Standing (stationary) waves remain constrained within a spatial region. A string fixed at both ends exemplifies one-dimensional standing waves. "Only those waves having an integer number, n, of half-wavelengths between the end points can form." This is **quantization** — only discrete values from a continuous set are observed. Points of no motion are **nodes**. Energy increases with the number of half-wavelengths n; since nodes equal n − 1, energy generally increases with node count. Two-dimensional standing waves have nodal lines; three-dimensional waves have nodal surfaces.

### Blackbody Radiation and the Ultraviolet Catastrophe
Continuous spectra contain unbroken wavelength ranges from heated solids, liquids, or condensed gases. Sunlight (UV + visible + IR) is approximated by **blackbody radiation** curves. A **blackbody** is "an ideal emitter that approximates the behavior of many materials when heated." Temperature is the single parameter determining each distribution curve.

**Wien's law observation**: λmax shifts to shorter wavelengths as temperature increases (dull red → bright → white-hot).

**The Ultraviolet Catastrophe**: Classical physics fit observations at long wavelengths but predicted intensity becoming infinitely large as wavelength decreased — absurdly implying room-temperature objects emit large amounts of UV.

**Max Planck's solution (c. 1900)**: matched experiment exactly by restricting vibrating atom energies to discrete values for each frequency, rather than a continuum:

> **E = nhν, where n = 1, 2, 3, …**

where h is **Planck's constant = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s**. The extremely small value of h explains why quantization wasn't observed macroscopically. Planck could not explain *why* energy was quantized.

### The Photoelectric Effect
Electrons eject from clean metal surfaces only when light *above a threshold frequency* strikes. Ejected-electron kinetic energy depends on light **frequency**, not brightness. Classical wave theory predicted energy should depend on intensity (amplitude). Brighter light increases the *number* of ejected electrons but not their kinetic energy.

**Einstein's resolution (1905)**: light consists of particles called **photons**, each with energy:

> **E = hν**, or (using c = νλ) **E = hc/λ**

Electrons eject when struck by photons of sufficient energy (frequency above threshold). Greater frequency → greater kinetic energy. Light intensity = number of photons per unit time, so brightness increases the count of ejected electrons without changing individual kinetic energy.

### Wave-Particle Duality
Some light phenomena are explained by waves (double-slit interference contradicts the particle view); others by particles (photoelectric effect contradicts the wave view). "Somehow, at a deep fundamental level still not fully understood, light is both wavelike and particle-like."

**Example 6.2 — Photon Energy:**
Neon sign at 640 nm: E = hc/λ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s)(2.998 × 10⁸ m·s⁻¹)/(640 × 10⁻⁹ m) = 3.10 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
Microwave at 3 × 10⁹ Hz: E = hν = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s)(3 × 10⁹ Hz) = 2 × 10⁻²⁴ J

**Example 6.3 — Photoelectric Effect:** Increasing brightness does NOT increase ejected-electron kinetic energy (only photon energy/frequency does). Above threshold, brighter light increases the *number* of ejected electrons. Threshold energy for aluminum (lowest frequency 9.87 × 10¹⁴ Hz): E = hν = 6.53 × 10⁻¹⁹ J/photon = 394 kJ/mol.

### Line Spectra
Heated solids/liquids/condensed gases produce **continuous spectra**. Excited low-pressure gases (electrically or by heat) produce **line spectra** — discrete narrow lines (fluorescent bulbs, neon signs). Each element shows a characteristic set of lines; each line is a single wavelength, implying emitted light consists of discrete energies. Hydrogen gas broken into H atoms by electric discharge emits four visible-wavelength lines.

**Balmer's empirical equation (1885)** for the four visible hydrogen wavelengths:

> **1/λ = k(1/4 − 1/n²), where n = 3, 4, 5, 6**

**Rydberg formula** (generalized to all hydrogen lines; n₁ < n₂ integers):

> **1/λ = R∞(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²)**, with **R∞ = 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹**

Niels Bohr's 1913 explanation of these spectra convinced scientists to abandon classical physics and spurred modern quantum mechanics.

**Portrait — Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin (1910–1993):** Pioneer of X-ray crystallography (X-rays ~0.01–10 nm, comparable to crystal atomic spacing, producing diffraction patterns that reveal atomic positions). Determined structures of cholesterol iodide, penicillin, and vitamin B₁₂; led the 1969 team that deduced insulin's structure. Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 1964.

## 6.2 The Bohr Model

### Overview
The Bohr model resolved the atomic stability paradox by incorporating quantization. After Rutherford's planetary model, classical physics predicted that an orbiting (accelerating) electron should continuously emit radiation and spiral into the nucleus. Niels Bohr (1913) rejected this: electrons occupy **stationary states** without emission, emitting or absorbing photons only during transitions between orbits.

### Key Equations

**Energy difference between orbits** (absorbed or emitted as a photon):

> **|ΔE| = |E_f − E_i| = hν = hc/λ**

**Quantized orbital energies** (Bohr's postulate):

> **E_n = −k/n², n = 1, 2, 3, …**

where k is a constant comprising electron mass, charge, and Planck's constant; n is the principal quantum number.

**Rydberg equation** (combining the two above):

> **1/λ = (k/hc)(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²)** — identical to Rydberg's, where R∞ = k/hc.

For hydrogen-like (single-electron) species:

> **E_n = −kZ²/n²**, where Z = nuclear charge (+1 H, +2 He⁺, +3 Li²⁺, …)

The Rydberg constant in energy units: **R∞ = k = 2.179 × 10⁻¹⁸ J**.

**Orbital radius** (hydrogen-like):

> **r = n²a₀/Z**, where a₀ = Bohr radius = 5.292 × 10⁻¹¹ m

### Fundamental Concepts
- **Ground state**: lowest-energy state; hydrogen's electron normally at n = 1. **Excited state**: higher n after absorbing energy.
- **Absorption/Emission**: transition to a higher orbit absorbs a photon (positive ΔE); transition to a lower orbit emits a photon. Energy is conserved.
- **Quantized energy levels**: "The energies of electrons (energy levels) in an atom are quantized, described by quantum numbers: integer numbers having only specific allowed values." Electron energy increases with distance from the nucleus. Discrete spectral lines result from quantized energies.
- **Ionization limit**: as n → ∞ and r → ∞, E → 0, the electron is fully removed. For ground-state hydrogen (n = 1), ionization energy equals k.

**Example 6.4 — Energy in a Bohr orbit:** H, n = 3: E = −(2.179 × 10⁻¹⁸ J)(1)²/(3)² = −2.421 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. n = 6: E = −6.053 × 10⁻²⁰ J.

**Example 6.5 — Transition energy and wavelength:** H, n = 4 → n = 6: ΔE = 2.179 × 10⁻¹⁸(1/16 − 1/36) = 7.566 × 10⁻²⁰ J (positive → absorption); λ = hc/E = 2.626 × 10⁻⁶ m (infrared). He⁺ (Z = 2), n = 5 → n = 3: E = 6.198 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; λ = 3.205 × 10⁻⁷ m.

### Significance and Limitations
Bohr's theoretical Rydberg constant matched experiment with remarkable precision. The model explained blackbody radiation, the photoelectric effect, and the hydrogen spectrum. But it failed for multi-electron atoms (even helium) and relied on classical precise orbits, later found untenable.

**Key takeaways** — the model established three features adopted by all later atomic models:
1. Electron energies are quantized (specific allowed values).
2. Electron energy increases with distance from the nucleus.
3. Discrete spectral lines result from quantized electronic energies.
The most important is the postulate of quantized energy levels, foundational to quantum mechanics.

## 6.3 Development of Quantum Theory

### Learning Objectives
Understand wave-particle duality applied to matter; the quantum-mechanical description of electrons via three-dimensional wavefunctions (orbitals); and the four quantum numbers specifying an electron's state.

### de Broglie Wavelength
Louis de Broglie extended wave-particle duality to matter: particles have a wavelength

> **λ = h/(mv) = h/p**

(m = mass, v = velocity, p = momentum). For a circular orbit of radius r, de Broglie's standing-wave condition requires

> **2πr = nλ, n = 1, 2, 3, …**

— explaining Bohr's quantization: only an integer number of wavelengths fits the orbit.

**Experimental confirmation**: Davisson and Germer observed electron diffraction off a crystalline nickel target, producing interference patterns like light through double slits. Individual electrons arrive as localized particles, but their accumulated pattern is wavelike — their motion is governed by a wave equation.

### Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle
Werner Heisenberg: "It is fundamentally impossible to determine simultaneously and exactly both the momentum and the position of a particle."

> **Δx · Δpₓ = (Δx)(mΔv) ≥ ℏ/2**, where ℏ = h/(2π)

Example: for Δx = 1 pm, Δp = ℏ/(2Δx) = (1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ kg·m²/s)/(2 × 10⁻¹² m) = 5 × 10⁻²³ kg·m/s. The principle also applies to energy and time: **ΔE · Δt ≥ ℏ/2**. It is a consequence of wave-particle duality.

### The Quantum-Mechanical Model
Erwin Schrödinger described electrons as three-dimensional stationary waves (**wavefunctions**, ψ). Max Born's interpretation: **|ψ|²** gives the probability of finding the particle near a location. The Schrödinger equation:

> **Ĥψ = Eψ** (Ĥ = Hamiltonian operator for total energy; E = total energy)

The wavefunction determines the electron's density distribution about the nucleus.

### Quantum Numbers
An electron is fully described by four quantum numbers: n, l, m_l, m_s.

| Name | Symbol | Allowed values | Physical meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Principal | n | 1, 2, 3, 4, … | Shell; general region/energy level and orbital size |
| Angular momentum (azimuthal) | l | 0 ≤ l ≤ n − 1 | Subshell; shape of orbital |
| Magnetic | m_l | −l, …, 0, …, +l | Orientation of orbital |
| Spin | m_s | +½ (α), −½ (β) | Direction of intrinsic electron spin |

**Subshell letters by l**: l=0 → s; l=1 → p; l=2 → d; l=3 → f; l=4 → g; l=5 → h.
**Orbitals per subshell** = 2l + 1: s=1, p=3, d=5, f=7.
**Electron spin**: an intrinsic quantum "spinning"; each electron acts as a tiny magnet. It is purely quantum (no classical analogue) and cannot be derived from the Schrödinger equation. In a magnetic field, the two spin states differ in energy (fine-structure splitting).

### Orbital Shapes and Nodes
s subshell is spherical; p subshell is dumbbell-shaped; d and f are more complex. **Radial nodes** are distances where ψ = 0 (probability density zero).

> **Number of radial nodes = n − l − 1**

(e.g., 1s: 0 nodes; 2s: 1; 3s: 2).

### Degeneracy
For hydrogen / one-electron ions, all orbitals with the same n have the same energy (**degeneracy**). In multi-electron atoms, electron-electron interactions remove this; orbitals in different subshells differ in energy, but orbitals within the same subshell remain degenerate.

Electronic transitions in hydrogen-like atoms:

> **ΔE = E_final − E_initial = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ (1/n_f² − 1/n_i²) J**

### The Pauli Exclusion Principle
Wolfgang Pauli: "No two electrons in the same atom can have exactly the same set of all four quantum numbers." Two electrons may share an orbital (same n, l, m_l) only if their m_s differ. Since m_s has only two values, **any orbital holds at most two electrons** (with opposite spins).

**Example 6.6 — Particle wavelength:** electron at v = 1.000 × 10⁷ m/s, m = 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg: λ = h/(mv) = 7.274 × 10⁻¹¹ m (comparable to atomic dimensions → wave behavior observable). Softball (0.1 kg, 35 m/s): λ = 1.9 × 10⁻³⁴ m (undetectable → particle behavior).

**Example 6.7 — Shells/subshells:** n = 4 → l = 0,1,2,3 → subshells 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f → 1+3+5+7 = 16 orbitals.

**Example 6.8 — Max electrons:** n = 2 → 8; n = 5 → 50; general formula **max electrons = 2n²** (n = 4 → 32).

**Example 6.9 — Quantum numbers:** 4f → n=4, l=3, 2l+1 = 7 orbitals, radial nodes = 0. 5d → n=5, l=2, 5 orbitals, radial nodes = 2.

## 6.4 Electronic Structure of Atoms (Electron Configurations)

### Learning Objectives
Derive ground-state electron configurations; identify and explain exceptions; relate configurations to periodic-table classification.

### Orbital Energies
Energy increases with n. In multi-electron atoms, electron repulsion splits subshells by l, with ordering **s < p < d < f** within a shell. 1s is lowest. As l increases, electrons are less penetrating → more shielded → higher energy. For small orbitals (1s–3p), n dominates; for larger orbitals, n and l effects become comparable and require empirical data (e.g., **3d is higher in energy than 4s**).

### Electron Configuration Notation
Three pieces: principal quantum number n, subshell letter (l), and a superscript electron count. E.g., 2p⁴ = four electrons in the n=2 p subshell; 3d⁸ = eight electrons in the n=3 d subshell.

### The Aufbau Principle
"Build" the atom by adding protons and electrons one at a time; each electron fills the lowest-energy available subshell (subject to allowed quantum numbers and the Pauli principle). Filling order:

> **1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p 5s 4d 5p 6s 4f 5d 6p 7s 5f 6d 7p**

### The Pauli Exclusion Principle (applied)
No two electrons share all four quantum numbers; two electrons in one orbital have opposite spins (shown as ↑↓ in an orbital diagram).

### Hund's Rule
"The lowest-energy configuration for an atom with electrons within a set of degenerate orbitals is that having the maximum number of unpaired electrons." Electrons fill degenerate orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing (applies to p (3 orbitals), d (5), f (7) sets).

### Orbital Diagrams
Boxes = orbitals; single ↑ = one electron (m_s = +½); ↑↓ = two opposite-spin electrons.

### Example Configurations (H → Ne)
- H (Z=1): 1s¹ — [1s: ↑]
- He (Z=2): 1s² — [1s: ↑↓] (n=1 shell full)
- Li (Z=3): 1s² 2s¹
- Be (Z=4): 1s² 2s²
- B (Z=5): 1s² 2s² 2p¹
- C (Z=6): 1s² 2s² 2p² — Hund: [2p: ↑ | ↑ | ]
- N (Z=7): 1s² 2s² 2p³ — [2p: ↑ | ↑ | ↑]
- O (Z=8): 1s² 2s² 2p⁴ — [2p: ↑↓ | ↑ | ↑]
- F (Z=9): 1s² 2s² 2p⁵
- Ne (Z=10): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ (n=1 and n=2 full)

### Core and Valence Electrons
**Valence electrons**: occupy the outermost shell (highest n); participate in bonding and determine chemical properties. **Core electrons**: inner shells.

**Noble gas notation**: replace the noble-gas core with its symbol in brackets. E.g., Na (Z=11) = 1s²2s²2p⁶3s¹ = **[Ne] 3s¹**. Comparisons: Li [He]2s¹ / Na [Ne]3s¹; Be [He]2s² / Mg [Ne]3s²; B [He]2s²2p¹ / Al [Ne]3s²3p¹.

### Transition Metals (d-block)
At K (Z=19), the next electron enters 4s (not 3d): [Ar]4s¹, because 3d lacks radial nodes (less penetrating, more shielded than 4s). Ca (Z=20): [Ar]4s². From Sc (Z=21), electrons fill 3d (10 electrons max: 5 orbitals); then 4p fills.

### Inner Transition Elements (f-block)
Lanthanides (La–Lu) and actinides (Ac–Lr): 14 f electrons added to the (n−2) shell (7 orbitals, capacity 14), bringing that shell from 18 to 32 electrons.

### Exceptions
Increased stability of half-filled / fully-filled d subshells:
- Cr (Z=24): expected [Ar]4s²3d⁴ → observed **[Ar]4s¹3d⁵**
- Cu (Z=29): expected [Ar]4s²3d⁹ → observed **[Ar]4s¹3d¹⁰**
- Nb (Z=41): expected [Kr]5s²4d³ → observed **[Kr]5s¹4d⁴**
Exceptions arise when same-orbital electron repulsions exceed subshell energy gaps; not predictable without empirical data.

### Configurations and the Periodic Table
Periodic recurrence of chemical properties tracks the recurrence of outer-shell configurations. Same group = same number of valence electrons (Group 1 = 1; Group 2 = 2; Group 17 = 7).

**Classification by which orbital receives the last electron:**
- **Main group (representative)**: last electron in s or p of the outermost shell. Valence electrons = highest-n s and p. E.g., Ga (Z=31) = [Ar]4s²3d¹⁰4p¹ → valence = 4s²4p¹ (the filled 3d are core).
- **Transition metals**: last electron in a d orbital; valence = ns and (n−1)d. (IUPAC: transition elements have *partially* filled d orbitals; Zn, Cd, Hg, Cu, Ag, Au have full d, so technically not transition, though the term is applied to the whole d-block colloquially.)
- **Inner transition**: last electron in an f orbital; valence = (n−2)f, (n−1)d, ns. Lanthanide (La–Lu) and actinide (Ac–Lr) series.

### Electron Configurations of Ions
- **Cations**: remove the electrons added *last* (highest n). For transition/inner-transition metals, remove ns first, then (n−1)d or (n−2)f. s electrons are easier to remove than d/f.
- **Anions**: add electrons by Aufbau order.

**Example — P (Z=15):** 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p³; last electron is 3p: n=3, l=1, m_l = −1/0/+1, m_s = +½.

**Example — Ions:**
- Na⁺: 1s²2s²2p⁶ ([Ne])
- P³⁻: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶
- Al²⁺: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s¹
- Fe²⁺: [Ar]3d⁶ (loses both 4s electrons): 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶3d⁶
- Sm³⁺: loses two 6s then one 4f → …5p⁶4f⁵

## 6.5 Periodic Variations in Element Properties

### Learning Objectives
Describe and explain periodic trends in atomic size, ionization energy, and electron affinity.

### Effective Nuclear Charge (Z_eff)
The net nuclear pull on a specific electron, accounting for shielding by other electrons:

> **Z_eff = Z − shielding**

For hydrogen, Z_eff = Z. For all other atoms, inner electrons partially shield outer ones; core electrons shield more effectively than valence electrons in the same shell.

### Covalent Radius / Atomic Size
**Covalent radius** = "one-half the distance between the nuclei of two identical atoms when they are joined by a covalent bond."
- **Down a group**: radius increases (n increases → outer electrons farther out).
- **Across a period**: radius decreases (Z rises faster than shielding → larger Z_eff pulls electrons in).

Halogen example (Table 6.2): F 64 pm (+9), Cl 99 pm (+17), Br 114 pm (+35), I 133 pm (+53), At 148 pm (+85).

### Ionic Radius
- **Cations**: smaller than parent (electron removal raises Z_eff per remaining electron). Larger positive charge → smaller (V²⁺ = 79 pm; V³⁺ = 64 pm).
- **Anions**: larger than parent (added electrons increase repulsion, lower Z_eff per electron). S atom 104 pm → S²⁻ 170 pm.
- **Isoelectronic species** (same electron configuration, e.g. N³⁻, O²⁻, F⁻, Ne, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ all 1s²2s²2p⁶): more protons → smaller radius.

### Ionization Energy (IE)
**IE₁** (first): energy to remove the most loosely bound electron from a gaseous atom — X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻. **IE₂**: X⁺(g) → X²⁺(g) + e⁻. All IE values are positive (endothermic).
- **Down a group**: IE₁ decreases (valence electrons farther out, easier to remove).
- **Across a period**: IE₁ generally increases (stronger Z_eff).

**Exceptions:**
- B (Z=5) < Be (Z=4): B removes a 2p electron, Be a 2s (s lower-energy, harder to remove).
- O < N: removing an electron from O leaves a stable half-filled 2p³; similar dip for S after P.

**Successive IEs** always increase; large jumps occur when core-electron removal begins, marking the valence/core boundary (e.g., Sc and Ga each jump sharply after the third ionization).

### Electron Affinity (EA)
Energy change for adding an electron to a gaseous atom — X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g). May be exothermic (negative EA, energy released) or endothermic (positive EA).
- **Across a period (L→R)**: EAs tend to become more negative (rising Z_eff).
- **Down a group**: the second-period element often has the most negative EA (small n=2 shell, large repulsions).

**Exceptions** (less favorable EA): Group 18 (full shells, electron must enter a higher n), Group 2 (filled ns, next electron enters higher np), Group 15 (half-filled np, next electron must pair → repulsion).

**Chlorine** has the most negative EA of any element: **−348 kJ/mol** (vs fluorine **−322 kJ/mol**) — Cl's entering electron occupies the larger n=3 shell with less repulsion than F's n=2.

**Example 6.12 — Atomic radii:** increasing covalent radius for Ge, Fl, Br, Kr → **Kr < Br < Ge < Fl** (decreases across a period; increases down a group).

**Example 6.13 — Ionization energies:** increasing order → **IE₁(In) < IE₁(Al) < IE₃(Al) < IE₂(Na)** (In's 5p¹ easiest; IE₂(Na) removes a core electron → highest).

### Chemical Significance
Atomic size, IE, and EA are central to chemical reactivity. Fluorine's favorable EA plus large IE makes anion formation easier than cation formation. **Metallic character** (conductivity, malleability) tracks ease of electron removal: it increases down a group and decreases across a period, following atomic-size trends.
