## Introduction

The chapter opens with the history of leprosy treatment. Early twentieth-century medicine relied on chaulmoogra tree oil, but its extreme thickness (high viscosity) limited effectiveness and caused pain during application. Alice Ball, a young chemist at the University of Hawaii, developed a solution based on her master's thesis: a sequence of repeated acidification and purification to change the characteristics of the oil and isolate the active substances (esters). This became the "Ball Method," the standard leprosy treatment for decades.

Core framing: "In the liquid and solid states, atomic and molecular interactions are of considerable strength and play an important role in determining a number of physical properties of the substance." The chaulmoogra oil's viscosity specifically resulted from intermolecular forces. The chapter explores these interactions and their effects on physical properties across liquid and solid phases.

## 10.1 Intermolecular Forces

### Kinetic Molecular Theory and Phase States
The kinetic molecular theory explains behavior of solids, liquids, and gases through particle arrangement and motion:
- **Solids:** Particles tightly packed in regular patterns; vibrate about fixed positions; do not move relative to one another.
- **Liquids:** Particles close together without regular arrangement; move past each other while remaining in constant contact.
- **Gases:** Particles far apart without regular arrangement; move independently except during collisions.

"The phase in which a substance exists depends on the relative extents of its intermolecular forces (IMFs) and the kinetic energies (KE) of its molecules." IMFs hold particles close together; kinetic energy provides energy to overcome attractive forces and increase particle distance. Temperature changes alter average kinetic energy, inducing phase transitions. Cooling gaseous water enough causes condensation; compressing gases at moderate temperatures can liquefy them by bringing molecules close enough that IMFs become strong relative to kinetic energy.

### Intramolecular vs. Intermolecular Forces
- **Intramolecular forces** hold atoms together within molecules (covalent bonds).
- **Intermolecular forces** are attractions between molecules; they determine physical properties.

IMFs are typically much weaker than intramolecular forces. Overcoming IMFs in one mole of liquid HCl requires ~17 kJ, while breaking the covalent H–Cl bonds requires ~430 kJ.

### Van der Waals Forces
"All of the attractive forces between neutral atoms and molecules are known as van der Waals forces," commonly called intermolecular attractions. Three types exist:
1. London dispersion forces
2. Dipole-dipole attractions
3. Hydrogen bonding

### Dispersion Forces (London Forces)
"This attractive force is called the London dispersion force in honor of German-born American physicist Fritz London who, in 1928, first explained it." Dispersion forces result from temporary (instantaneous) dipole formation: because electrons constantly move, atoms/molecules can momentarily develop instantaneous dipoles when electrons distribute asymmetrically; this dipole distorts neighboring particles' electron clouds, producing induced dipoles, creating weak electrostatic attraction. Dispersion forces "are present in all condensed phases, regardless of the nature of the atoms or molecules composing the substance."

Factors affecting strength: larger, heavier atoms/molecules exhibit stronger dispersion forces.

Halogen data:

| Halogen | Molar Mass | Atomic Radius | Melting Point | Boiling Point |
|---------|-----------|--------------|--------------|--------------|
| F₂      | 38 g/mol  | 72 pm       | 53 K         | 85 K         |
| Cl₂     | 71 g/mol  | 99 pm       | 172 K        | 238 K        |
| Br₂     | 160 g/mol | 114 pm      | 266 K        | 332 K        |
| I₂      | 254 g/mol | 133 pm      | 387 K        | 457 K        |
| At₂     | 420 g/mol | 150 pm      | 575 K        | 610 K        |

**Polarizability:** "The measure of how easy or difficult it is for another electrostatic charge ... to distort a molecule's charge distribution (its electron cloud) is known as polarizability." Larger atoms have valence electrons farther from nuclei and less tightly held, forming temporary dipoles more easily → larger dispersion forces.

**Molecular shape:** pentane isomers (all C₅H₁₂): n-pentane 36 °C (elongated, large surface area, strongest attractions) > isopentane 27 °C > neopentane 9.5 °C (most compact, weakest). "The elongated shape of n-pentane provides a greater surface area available for contact between molecules, resulting in correspondingly stronger dispersion forces."

Example: nonpolar CH₄ (16) < SiH₄ (32) < GeH₄ (77) < SnH₄ (123 g/mol) in boiling point — larger = more polarizable = stronger dispersion.

### Dipole-Dipole Attractions
Polar molecules contain partial charges. "This attractive force is called a dipole-dipole attraction—the electrostatic force between the partially positive end of one polar molecule and the partially negative end of another." In HCl, Cl bears δ–, H bears δ+. Dipole-dipole attractions are stronger than dispersion forces but weaker than hydrogen bonding.

Comparison: HCl (polar, ~36 amu, normal bp 188 K) vs F₂ (nonpolar, ~36 amu, normal bp 85 K). Comparison: CO (polar, ~28 amu) has higher bp than N₂ (nonpolar, ~28 amu).

### Hydrogen Bonding
Hydrogen bonding is "a particularly strong type of dipole-dipole attraction" occurring when H atoms bond to F, O, or N. "The very large difference in electronegativity between the H atom (2.1) and the atom to which it is bonded (4.0 for an F atom, 3.5 for an O atom, or 3.0 for a N atom), combined with the very small size of a H atom and the relatively small sizes of F, O, or N atoms, leads to highly concentrated partial charges with these atoms." Requirement: molecule must contain F–H, O–H, or N–H. Examples: HF⋯HF, H₂O⋯HOH, H₃N⋯HNH₂.

"Despite use of the word 'bond,' keep in mind that hydrogen bonds are intermolecular attractive forces, not intramolecular attractive forces (covalent bonds)." "Hydrogen bonds are much weaker than covalent bonds, only about 5 to 10% as strong, but are generally much stronger than other dipole-dipole attractions and dispersion forces."

Anomalous boiling points of period-2 hydrides due to H-bonding:
- Group 17: HF 25 °C; HCl −80 °C; HBr −60 °C; HI −40 °C
- Group 16: H₂O 100 °C; H₂S −55 °C; H₂Se −45 °C; H₂Te −3 °C
- Group 15: NH₃ −40 °C; PH₃ −80 °C; AsH₃ −55 °C; SbH₃ −10 °C

Predicted (no H-bonding): NH₃ ~−120 °C, H₂O ~−80 °C, HF ~−110 °C.

Example (similar molar masses 44–46 g/mol): propane −42.1 °C (dispersion only) < dimethylether −24.8 °C (dipole-dipole) < ethanol 78.4 °C (hydrogen bonding via –OH).

**DNA:** complementary base pairs held by hydrogen bonds — adenine–thymine (2 H-bonds), guanine–cytosine (3 H-bonds). "The cumulative effect of millions of hydrogen bonds effectively holds the two strands of DNA together," yet strands "unzip" easily because H-bonds are weak relative to covalent bonds, enabling replication.

**Summary (weakest → strongest):** London dispersion (all substances) < dipole-dipole (polar only) < hydrogen bonding (F–H, O–H, N–H required). IMF strength determines melting points, boiling points, enthalpies of vaporization, and enthalpies of fusion.

## 10.2 Properties of Liquids

### Viscosity
"The viscosity of a liquid is a measure of its resistance to flow." Measured by rate a metal ball falls through it, or rate it flows through a narrow tube. Increases with IMF strength and molecular size/complexity; decreases as temperature increases (molecules' KE overcomes IMFs).

| Substance | Formula | Viscosity (mPa·s) |
|-----------|---------|-------------------|
| Water | H₂O | 0.890 |
| Mercury | Hg | 1.526 |
| Ethanol | C₂H₅OH | 1.074 |
| Octane | C₈H₁₈ | 0.508 |
| Ethylene glycol | CH₂(OH)CH₂(OH) | 16.1 |

### Cohesive Forces and Surface Tension
**Cohesive forces:** "The various IMFs between identical molecules of a substance are examples of cohesive forces." Bulk molecules are attracted equally in all directions; surface molecules feel a net inward pull, so liquids contract to minimize surface area (small drops are spherical).

**Surface tension:** "Surface tension is defined as the energy required to increase the surface area of a liquid, or the force required to increase the length of a liquid surface by a given amount." Surface behaves like a stretched membrane.

| Substance | Formula | Surface Tension (mN/m) |
|-----------|---------|------------------------|
| Water | H₂O | 71.99 |
| Mercury | Hg | 458.48 |
| Ethanol | C₂H₅OH | 21.97 |
| Octane | C₈H₁₈ | 21.14 |
| Ethylene glycol | CH₂(OH)CH₂(OH) | 47.99 |

Water's distinctly high surface tension (strong H-bonding) lets it support steel needles and insects.

### Adhesive Forces and Meniscus
**Adhesive forces:** "The IMFs of attraction between two different molecules are called adhesive forces." Weak adhesion vs cohesion → water does not wet surface (waxed surfaces, polyethylene). Strong adhesion → water spreads (glass). **Meniscus:** water in glass is concave (wets and creeps up); mercury is convex (cohesion > adhesion to glass).

### Capillary Action
"Capillary action—when a liquid flows within a porous material due to the attraction of the liquid molecules to the surface of the material and to other liquid molecules." Examples: wine wicking up paper towels, water in cloth, plant xylem, thin-layer chromatography.

Capillary rise height:

$$h = \frac{2T\cos\theta}{r\rho g}$$

- *h* = height of liquid in tube relative to outside surface
- *T* = surface tension
- *θ* = contact angle between liquid and tube (water in glass: θ = 0°, cos θ = 1)
- *r* = tube radius
- *ρ* = liquid density
- *g* = 9.8 m/s²

Worked example: 25 °C, glass capillary inner diameter 0.25 mm, T = 71.99 mN/m = 0.07199 kg/s², ρ = 1000 kg/m³, r = 0.000125 m → h = 0.12 m = 12 cm. Check: rise of 8.4 cm → diameter 0.36 mm.

Biomedical: capillary blood collection — droplet held by surface tension; adhesion to glass draws blood up; cohesion holds it together; rise stops when weight balances upward capillary force; smaller tube → greater rise.

## 10.3 Phase Transitions

### Vaporization and Condensation; Dynamic Equilibrium
In a closed container, gas molecules cannot escape; some re-enter the liquid (condensation). When condensation rate equals vaporization rate, the system reaches **dynamic equilibrium**—"the status of a system in which reciprocal processes (for example, vaporization and condensation) occur at equal rates."

### Vapor Pressure
The pressure exerted by vapor in equilibrium with its liquid in a closed container at a given temperature is the **vapor pressure** (equilibrium vapor pressure). Independent of surface area or vessel size (these affect only time to reach equilibrium). Strong IMFs → low vapor pressure; weak IMFs → high vapor pressure.

Alcohols at 20 °C (vapor pressure decreases with size/dispersion): methanol 11.9 kPa; ethanol 5.95 kPa; propanol 2.67 kPa; butanol 0.56 kPa. Higher temperature → larger fraction of molecules with enough KE to escape → higher vapor pressure.

### Boiling Points
**Boiling point:** "the temperature at which its equilibrium vapor pressure is equal to the pressure exerted on the liquid by its gaseous surroundings." **Normal boiling point:** boiling point at 1 atm (101.3 kPa).

### Clausius-Clapeyron Equation

$$P = Ae^{-\Delta H_{vap}/RT}$$

- ΔH_vap = enthalpy of vaporization (J/mol)
- R = 8.3145 J/mol·K
- A = constant dependent on chemical identity
- T = temperature (K)

Logarithmic form:

$$\ln P = -\frac{\Delta H_{vap}}{RT} + \ln A$$

Two-point form:

$$\ln\left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right) = \frac{\Delta H_{vap}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2}\right)$$

(T in K; consistent pressure units.)

Applications: at Leadville, CO (~68 kPa), water boils ~90 °C. Isooctane: 10.0 kPa at 307.2 K, 100.0 kPa at 372.0 K → ΔH_vap = R·ln(P₂/P₁)/(1/T₁ − 1/T₂) = 33,800 J/mol = 33.8 kJ/mol. Ethanol ≈ 41.4 kJ/mol. Benzene (normal bp 80.1 °C = 353.3 K, ΔH_vap 30.8 kJ/mol) boils in Denver (83.4 kPa) at T₂ = (−R·ln(P₂/P₁)/ΔH_vap + 1/T₁)⁻¹ = 346.9 K = 73.8 °C.

### Enthalpy of Vaporization
Vaporization is endothermic. H₂O(l) → H₂O(g), ΔH_vap = 44.01 kJ/mol; condensation H₂O(g) → H₂O(l), ΔH_con = −44.01 kJ/mol. To evaporate 1.5 L (1.5 kg) water at 37 °C (ΔH_vap = 43.46 kJ/mol): 3.6 × 10³ kJ.

### Melting and Freezing; Enthalpy of Fusion
At the melting point temperature stays constant while heat is added until all solid melts. **Melting/freezing point:** "the temperature at which the solid and liquid phases of a given substance are in equilibrium." **Enthalpy of fusion** (ΔH_fus): heat to melt one mole. Fusion endothermic: H₂O(s) → H₂O(l), ΔH_fus = 6.01 kJ/mol; freezing exothermic: ΔH_frz = −6.01 kJ/mol. Stronger forces → higher melting points.

### Sublimation and Deposition; Enthalpy of Sublimation
**Sublimation:** solid → gas directly (dry ice, CO₂). **Deposition:** gas → solid directly (frost). **Enthalpy of sublimation** (ΔH_sub): CO₂(s) → CO₂(g), ΔH_sub = 26.1 kJ/mol; deposition ΔH_dep = −26.1 kJ/mol.

Relationships: ΔH_fus < ΔH_vap (full vs partial overcoming of attractions). Sublimation ≈ melting then vaporizing: ΔH_sub = ΔH_fus + ΔH_vap.

### Heating and Cooling Curves
No phase change: q = m·c·ΔT. Phase change: q = n·ΔH. Temperature is constant during transitions. Example — 135 g ice at −15 °C → vapor at 120 °C: q_total = (m c ΔT)_ice + n ΔH_fus + (m c ΔT)_water + n ΔH_vap + (m c ΔT)_steam = 4.23 + 45.0 + 56.5 + 305 + 5.02 = 416 kJ. (c_ice 2.09, c_water 4.18, c_steam 1.86 J/g·°C; ΔH_fus 6.01, ΔH_vap 40.67 kJ/mol; M = 18.02 g/mol.)

### Critical Point and Supercritical Fluids
(see 10.4) Above the **critical temperature** a gas cannot be liquefied at any pressure; the pressure needed to liquefy at the critical temperature is the **critical pressure**. For water the liquid-vapor curve terminates at the **critical point** 374 °C and 22,000 kPa (~218 atm); a **supercritical fluid** has properties intermediate between gas and liquid.

## 10.4 Phase Diagrams

A phase diagram combines P-vs-T plots for liquid-gas, solid-liquid, and solid-gas equilibria; it shows which physical state exists at given P and T, and the pressure dependence of transition temperatures. Axes: temperature (x), pressure (y). Three regions (solid, liquid, gas); three boundary curves.

- **Solid-gas curve** (sublimation/deposition): ice ⇌ vapor equilibria; basis of freeze-drying (frozen sample in vacuum below ice's vapor pressure sublimes).
- **Solid-liquid curve** (melting/freezing): for most substances positive slope (mp rises with P); **water is unusual — slight negative slope** (mp decreases slightly as P increases), partly responsible for glacier sliding on a melted layer.
- **Liquid-gas curve** (vaporization/condensation): vapor pressure vs T; gives boiling point at any pressure; terminates at the critical point.

**Triple point:** intersection of all three curves; three phases coexist. For water: 0.01 °C and 0.6 kPa. Below the triple-point pressure, liquid cannot exist at any temperature.

**Critical point** (water): 374 °C and 22,089 kPa.

Critical constants:

| Substance | Critical T (°C) | Critical P (kPa) |
|-----------|-----------------|------------------|
| Hydrogen | −240.0 | 1300 |
| Nitrogen | −147.2 | 3400 |
| Oxygen | −118.9 | 5000 |
| Carbon dioxide | 31.1 | 7400 |
| Ammonia | 132.4 | 11,300 |
| Sulfur dioxide | 157.2 | 7800 |
| Water | 374.0 | 22,000 |

**Water phase diagram:** at 1 atm water boils at 100 °C; negative-slope solid-liquid line; triple point 0.01 °C / 0.6 kPa; critical 374 °C / 22,089 kPa. Examples: −10 °C & 50 kPa = solid; 50 °C & 50 kPa = liquid; 25 kPa & 200 °C = gas.

**CO₂ phase diagram:** solid-liquid curve has positive slope; triple point well above 1 atm, so CO₂ cannot be liquid at ambient pressure (solid CO₂ sublimes at 1 atm); critical point 31.1 °C / 7400 kPa; pressure axis often logarithmic.

**Supercritical fluids:** expand to fill a container like a gas but have liquid-like density; dissolve nonvolatile solutes; essentially no surface tension and very low viscosity, so they penetrate small openings. Supercritical CO₂ (above 304.2 K and 7376 kPa) decaffeinates coffee (removes 97–99% caffeine, preserving flavor/aroma), removes fats from potato chips, extracts citrus flavor/fragrance; nontoxic, inexpensive, recoverable by reducing pressure. Fire-extinguisher example: at 18 °C (below CO₂'s 31 °C critical T) liquid CO₂ sloshes; at 35 °C (above) no liquid. Ammonia (critical T 405.5 K) is liquefiable by compression at room temperature; oxygen is not (critical T below room temperature).

## 10.5 The Solid State of Matter

**Crystalline solids:** "the atoms, ions, or molecules are arranged in a definite repeating pattern." **Amorphous (noncrystalline) solids / glasses:** "lack an ordered internal structure and are randomly arranged" (e.g., candle waxes). Metals and ionic compounds form crystalline structures; large or restricted molecules form amorphous solids. SiO₂ can be either.

### Four Types of Crystalline Solids

| Type | Particles | Attractions | Properties | Examples |
|------|-----------|-------------|-----------|----------|
| Ionic | Ions | Ionic bonds | Hard, brittle; high–very high mp; conduct only when molten/dissolved | NaCl, NiO, Al₂O₃ |
| Metallic | Atoms of electropositive elements | Metallic bonds ("sea" of delocalized electrons) | Shiny, malleable, ductile; conduct heat & electricity; variable hardness/mp | Cu, Fe, Ti, Pb, U |
| Covalent network | Atoms of electronegative elements | Covalent bonds throughout | Very hard, very high mp (diamond >3500 °C), not conductive | C(diamond), SiO₂, SiC, Si |
| Molecular | Molecules or atoms | IMFs | Variable hardness; not conductive; low mp (vary with size/polarity) | H₂O, CO₂, I₂, C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ |

Molecular-solid melting points: small symmetric nonpolar molecules (H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂) below −200 °C; ice 0 °C; CO₂ −78 °C; iodine 114 °C; sugar 185 °C. **Graphite** is an atypical covalent solid: planar sheets held by noncovalent forces; soft and electrically conductive.

**Melting behavior:** crystalline solids have precise melting temperatures (all attractions equal strength); amorphous materials soften gradually over a range (weakest attractions break first).

**Crystal defects:** vacancies (missing atoms/ions), interstitial sites (atoms between regular positions), impurity defects (foreign species distorting structure), and **doping** (deliberate trace impurities to tune properties — e.g., doped silicon for semiconductors/computer chips).

## 10.6 Lattice Structures in Crystalline Solids

Over 90% of solids are crystalline. "The overall attractive interactions between particles are maximized, and the total intermolecular energy is minimized, when the particles pack in the most efficient manner."

### Unit Cells of Metals
A **unit cell** is "the simplest repeating unit" of the structure; lattice points mark atom/ion positions; the structure is the unit cell repeated in 3D.

| Cubic cell | Atoms/cell | Coordination # | Packing efficiency | Edge–radius | Examples |
|------------|-----------|----------------|--------------------|-------------|----------|
| Simple (primitive) cubic | 1 (8 corners × 1/8) | 6 | ~52% | l = 2r (contact along edges) | Po |
| Body-centered cubic (BCC) | 2 (8×1/8 + 1 center) | 8 | ~68% | corner atoms contact center, not each other | K, Ba, Cr, Mo, W, Fe |
| Face-centered cubic (FCC) | 4 (8×1/8 + 6×1/2) | 12 | 74% (most efficient) | corner atoms touch face-centered along face diagonals | Al, Cu, Pb, Ag, Ni, Ca, Pt |

FCC = **cubic closest packing (CCP)**: ABCABC layering. **Hexagonal closest packing (HCP):** ABABAB layering, coordination 12 (Cd, Co, Li, Mg, Na, Zn). ~2/3 of all metals crystallize in closest-packed arrays (coordination 12).

A unit cell is defined by three axis lengths (a, b, c) and three angles (α, β, γ). There are **seven lattice systems and 14 unit cell types** (cubic, tetragonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic, triclinic, hexagonal, rhombohedral).

### Structures of Ionic Crystals
Stable ionic structures: ions of one charge surround as many opposite ions as possible, and cations contact anions. Larger anions usually form the closest-packed array with cations in the holes.

| Hole type | Cation:anion radius ratio | Holes per anion |
|-----------|---------------------------|-----------------|
| Tetrahedral (4 anions) | ~22.5%–41.4% | 2 |
| Octahedral (6 anions) | ~41.4%–73.2% | 1 |
| Cubic | ~73.2%–100% | — |

Maximum cation:anion ratios: all tetrahedral holes filled → 2:1 (Li₂O, Na₂O, Li₂S, Na₂S); all octahedral filled → 1:1 (NiO, MnS, NaCl, KH); all cubic filled → 1:1 (CsCl).

Representative ionic unit cells:
- **CsCl (simple cubic):** Cl⁻ simple cubic, Cs⁺ at center (1:1); 1 Cs⁺ + 1 Cl⁻ per cell; contact along body diagonal; NOT BCC (ions differ). Cs⁺ 174 pm, Cl⁻ 181 pm.
- **NaCl (FCC):** Cl⁻ FCC, Na⁺ in octahedral holes; 4 Na⁺ + 4 Cl⁻ per cell; contact along edges. Na⁺ 102 pm, Cl⁻ 181 pm.
- **Zinc blende (ZnS, FCC):** S²⁻ FCC, Zn²⁺ in alternating tetrahedral holes; 4 Zn²⁺ + 4 S²⁻ per cell; Zn radius ~40% of S²⁻.
- **Fluorite (CaF₂, FCC):** Ca²⁺ FCC, all tetrahedral sites filled by F⁻; 4 Ca²⁺ + 8 F⁻ per cell (Ca:F = 1:2).

**Ionic radii from edge length** (LiCl-type FCC, edge 0.514 nm): Cl⁻ ions contact across the face diagonal = 4r; a² + a² = (4r)² → (0.514)² + (0.514)² = 16r² → r_Cl = 0.182 nm. (Values rely on assumptions like perfect spheres.)

**Polonium density** (edge 336 pm, simple cubic): r = 168 pm; mass/cell = 1 × 208.998 / 6.022×10²³ = 3.47×10⁻²² g; V = (336×10⁻¹⁰ cm)³ = 3.79×10⁻²³ cm³; density = 9.16 g/cm³.

### X-Ray Crystallography
X-rays (wavelengths ~few Å, comparable to interatomic distances) are diffracted by crystal atoms; scattered waves interfere. Constructive interference (Bragg condition) occurs when path difference is an integer multiple of the wavelength.

**Bragg equation:** nλ = 2d sin θ
- n = order of diffraction (integer)
- λ = X-ray wavelength
- d = distance between diffracting planes
- θ = angle of the diffracted beam

A diffractometer measures diffraction angles; the Bragg equation then gives interatomic distances. Example: λ = 0.1315 nm, n = 1, θ = 25.25° → d = nλ/(2 sin θ) = 0.154 nm.

**Historical context — Rosalind Franklin:** her X-ray diffraction images provided essential information confirming DNA's double-helix structure and dimensions; she was not included in the 1962 Nobel Prize awarded to Crick, Watson, and Wilkins.
