Solid-fuel rockets serve as a central component in space exploration initiatives, including NASA's Space Launch System. ## 4.1 Writing and Balancing Chemical Equations

### What is a Chemical Equation?

A chemical equation uses element symbols and formulas to represent both the identities and relative quantities of substances undergoing a chemical or physical change. For example:

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

### Fundamental Aspects of Chemical Equations

1. **Reactants**: Substances undergoing reaction, placed on the left side of the equation
2. **Products**: Substances generated by the reaction, placed on the right side
3. **Separators**: Plus signs (+) separate individual formulas; an arrow (→) separates reactants from products
4. **Coefficients**: Numbers placed immediately to the left of each formula representing relative numbers of reactant and product species. A coefficient of 1 is typically omitted.

### Interpreting Coefficients

Coefficients represent relative numbers and may be interpreted as ratios or with any amount unit:

- "One methane molecule and two oxygen molecules react to yield one carbon dioxide molecule and two water molecules."
- "One mole of methane molecules and 2 moles of oxygen molecules react to yield 1 mole of carbon dioxide molecules and 2 moles of water molecules."

### Law of Conservation of Matter

A balanced chemical equation must satisfy the law of conservation of matter: equal numbers of atoms for each element must be represented on both the reactant and product sides.

To verify balance, multiply the coefficient of any formula containing an element by the element's subscript, then sum these values for each side of the equation.

**Example verification for CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O:**

| Element | Reactants | Products | Balanced? |
|---------|-----------|----------|-----------|
| C | 1 × 1 = 1 | 1 × 1 = 1 | 1 = 1, yes |
| H | 4 × 1 = 4 | 2 × 2 = 4 | 4 = 4, yes |
| O | 2 × 2 = 4 | (1 × 2) + (2 × 1) = 4 | 4 = 4, yes |

### Balancing by Inspection

A systematic approach to balance an unbalanced equation.

**Example: Decomposition of water**

Unbalanced: H₂O → H₂ + O₂

Step 1: Count atoms on each side
| Element | Reactants | Products | Balanced? |
|---------|-----------|----------|-----------|
| H | 1 × 2 = 2 | 1 × 2 = 2 | 2 = 2, yes |
| O | 1 × 1 = 1 | 1 × 2 = 2 | 1 ≠ 2, no |

Step 2: Change coefficient for H₂O to 2
2H₂O → H₂ + O₂ (unbalanced)

| Element | Reactants | Products | Balanced? |
|---------|-----------|----------|-----------|
| H | 2 × 2 = 4 | 1 × 2 = 2 | 4 ≠ 2, no |
| O | 2 × 1 = 2 | 1 × 2 = 2 | 2 = 2, yes |

Step 3: Change coefficient for H₂ to 2
**2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂** (balanced)

| Element | Reactants | Products | Balanced? |
|---------|-----------|----------|-----------|
| H | 2 × 2 = 4 | 2 × 2 = 4 | 4 = 4, yes |
| O | 2 × 1 = 2 | 1 × 2 = 2 | 2 = 2, yes |

### Key Rule for Balancing

Only coefficients may be changed to balance an equation. Formula subscripts cannot be altered, as changing them modifies the substance's identity. For example, changing H₂O to H₂O₂ would change water to hydrogen peroxide.

### Using Fractional Coefficients

When an odd number of atoms appears on one side, fractional coefficients may be used as intermediate steps. After achieving balance, multiply all coefficients by a whole number to convert fractions to integers.

**Example: Combustion of ethane**

Unbalanced: C₂H₆ + O₂ → H₂O + CO₂

Provisional balance: C₂H₆ + (7/2)O₂ → 3H₂O + 2CO₂

Multiply all coefficients by 2:
**2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 6H₂O + 4CO₂**

### Convention: Smallest Whole-Number Coefficients

Chemical equations should use the smallest possible whole-number coefficients. For example:

Avoid: 3N₂ + 9H₂ → 6NH₃

Preferred: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

(Divide each coefficient by the greatest common factor, 3.)

### Physical States in Chemical Equations

Common parenthetical abbreviations following formulas:
- **(s)**: solids
- **(l)**: liquids
- **(g)**: gases
- **(aq)**: aqueous solutions (dissolved in water)

**Example:** 2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g)

### Special Conditions

Special conditions for reactions may be indicated above or below the equation's arrow. For example, heating may be indicated by the Greek letter delta (Δ):

CaCO₃(s) →^Δ CaO(s) + CO₂(g)

### Equations for Ionic Reactions

Three levels of detail exist for representing ionic reactions in aqueous solutions.

#### 1. Molecular Equation

Represents all reactants and products as complete formulas without explicitly showing dissociated ions:

CaCl₂(aq) + 2AgNO₃(aq) → Ca(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2AgCl(s)

#### 2. Complete Ionic Equation

Shows all dissolved ionic compounds as dissociated ions:

Ca²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) + 2Ag⁺(aq) + 2NO₃⁻(aq) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2NO₃⁻(aq) + 2AgCl(s)

When ionic compounds dissolve in water, they may dissociate into their constituent ions dispersed homogeneously throughout the solution. However, some compounds like AgCl do not dissolve significantly in water (indicated by the (s) notation).

#### 3. Net Ionic Equation

Removes spectator ions—ions whose presence is required to maintain charge neutrality but are neither chemically nor physically changed by the process—to show only the essential chemistry. From the complete ionic equation above, Ca²⁺(aq) and NO₃⁻(aq) are spectator ions.

**Complete ionic equation:** Ca²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) + 2Ag⁺(aq) + 2NO₃⁻(aq) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2NO₃⁻(aq) + 2AgCl(s)

**Net ionic equation:** 2Cl⁻(aq) + 2Ag⁺(aq) → 2AgCl(s)

Using the convention of smallest whole-number coefficients:

**Cl⁻(aq) + Ag⁺(aq) → AgCl(s)**

The net ionic equation indicates that solid silver chloride may be produced from dissolved chloride and silver(I) ions, regardless of the source of these ions. The molecular and complete ionic equations provide additional information about the ionic compounds used as sources of Cl⁻ and Ag⁺.

## 4.2 Classifying Chemical Reactions

### Learning Objectives

- Define and classify three chemical reaction types: precipitation, acid-base, and oxidation-reduction
- Classify reactions given descriptions or equations
- Identify common acids and bases
- Predict solubility using solubility rules
- Compute oxidation states

### Precipitation Reactions and Solubility Rules

A **precipitation reaction** occurs when dissolved substances react to form one or more solid products. These are also called double displacement, double replacement, or metathesis reactions.

**Solubility** is defined as "the maximum concentration of a substance that can be achieved under specified conditions."

Substances with relatively large solubilities are **soluble**. When a substance's concentration exceeds its solubility, it **precipitates**. Substances with relatively low solubilities are **insoluble** and readily precipitate.

#### Solubility Guidelines

**Soluble Ionic Compounds:**
- NH₄⁺: no exceptions
- Group I cations (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, Rb⁺, Cs⁺): no exceptions
- Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻: exceptions with Ag⁺, Hg₂²⁺, Pb²⁺
- F⁻: exceptions with Group 2 metal cations, Pb²⁺, Fe³⁺
- C₂H₃O₂⁻, HCO₃⁻, NO₃⁻, ClO₃⁻: no exceptions
- SO₄²⁻: exceptions with Ag⁺, Ba²⁺, Ca²⁺, Hg₂²⁺, Pb²⁺, Sr²⁺

**Insoluble Ionic Compounds:**
- CO₃²⁻, CrO₄²⁻, PO₄³⁻, S²⁻: exceptions with Group 1 cations and NH₄⁺
- OH⁻: exceptions with Group 1 cations and Ba²⁺

#### Example Precipitation Reaction

When potassium iodide and lead nitrate solutions mix:

**Molecular equation:** 2KI(aq) + Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2KNO₃(aq)

**Net ionic equation:** Pb²⁺(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → PbI₂(s)

Lead iodide forms a bright yellow precipitate (formerly used as artist's pigment "iodine yellow").

### Acid-Base Reactions

An **acid-base reaction** involves transfer of a hydrogen ion (H⁺) from one chemical species to another.

#### Acids

An **acid** dissolves in water to yield hydronium ions (H₃O⁺). Example:

HCl(aq) + H₂O(l) → Cl⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq)

**Strong acids** completely react in water; reaction is essentially 100% efficient:
- HBr (hydrobromic acid)
- HCl (hydrochloric acid)
- HI (hydroiodic acid)
- HNO₃ (nitric acid)
- HClO₄ (perchloric acid)
- H₂SO₄ (sulfuric acid)

**Weak acids** partially react with water, leaving most dissolved molecules in original form and generating relatively small amounts of H₃O⁺. Example—acetic acid in vinegar:

CH₃CO₂H(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ CH₃CO₂⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq)

Under typical conditions, only about 1% of acetic acid molecules ionize.

#### Bases

A **base** dissolves in water to yield hydroxide ions (OH⁻). The most common bases are ionic compounds of alkali or alkaline earth metal cations combined with hydroxide ions (e.g., NaOH, Ca(OH)₂).

**Strong bases** completely dissociate in water. Example—sodium hydroxide:

NaOH(s) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

**Weak bases** produce hydroxide ions by chemically reacting with water molecules and react only partially. Example—ammonia:

NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

Under typical conditions, only about 1% of dissolved ammonia exists as NH₄⁺.

#### Neutralization Reactions

A **neutralization reaction** is a specific acid-base reaction where reactants are an acid and a base, and products are often a salt and water:

acid + base → salt + water

Example—antacid treating stomach acid:

Mg(OH)₂(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l)

This produces magnesium chloride (a salt) in addition to water.

### Oxidation-Reduction (Redox) Reactions

**Oxidation-reduction reactions** are those in which one or more elements undergo a change in oxidation number.

#### Electron Transfer Processes

In some redox reactions, electrons transfer between reactant entities. Example:

2Na(s) + Cl₂(g) → 2NaCl(s)

**Half-reactions** represent individual reactant fates:

2Na(s) → 2Na⁺(s) + 2e⁻
Cl₂(g) + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻(s)

**Oxidation** = loss of electrons
**Reduction** = gain of electrons

In this reaction:
- Sodium is oxidized (loses electrons)
- Chlorine is reduced (gains electrons)
- Sodium functions as a **reducing agent** (reductant)—species that is oxidized
- Chlorine functions as an **oxidizing agent** (oxidant)—species that is reduced

#### Oxidation Numbers

The **oxidation number** (or oxidation state) is the charge an atom would possess if the compound were ionic.

**Rules for assigning oxidation numbers:**

1. The oxidation number of an atom in an elemental substance is zero.
2. The oxidation number of a monatomic ion equals the ion's charge.
3. Oxidation numbers for common nonmetals:
   - **Hydrogen:** +1 when combined with nonmetals; −1 when combined with metals
   - **Oxygen:** −2 in most compounds; −1 in peroxides (O₂²⁻); very rarely −½ in superoxides (O₂⁻); positive when combined with F
   - **Halogens:** −1 for F always; −1 for other halogens except when combined with oxygen or other halogens (positive oxidation numbers in these cases)
4. The sum of oxidation numbers for all atoms in a molecule or polyatomic ion equals the charge on the molecule or ion.

**Note:** Proper convention writes charge as number first, then sign (e.g., 2+), while oxidation number uses reversed sequence, sign then number (e.g., +2).

#### Revised Redox Definitions

**Oxidation** = increase in oxidation number
**Reduction** = decrease in oxidation number

Example—hydrogen and chlorine:

H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g)

- Hydrogen is oxidized (oxidation number increases from 0 to +1)
- Chlorine is reduced (oxidation number decreases from 0 to −1)

#### Subclasses of Redox Reactions

**Combustion reactions** are redox reactions where reductant (fuel) and oxidant (often O₂) react vigorously, producing significant heat and often light (flame). Example—solid rocket fuel:

10Al(s) + 6NH₄ClO₄(s) → 4Al₂O₃(s) + 2AlCl₃(s) + 12H₂O(g) + 3N₂(g)

**Single-displacement (replacement) reactions** are redox reactions where an ion in solution is displaced by oxidation of a metallic element.

Example—acid oxidation of metal:
Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)

Example—metal oxidation by metal salt solution:
Cu(s) + 2AgNO₃(aq) → Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2Ag(s)

**Disproportionation reactions** involve the same element undergoing both oxidation and reduction. Example:

2H₂O₂(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g)

Oxygen is oxidized (from −1 in H₂O₂ to 0 in O₂) and reduced (from −1 in H₂O₂ to −2 in H₂O).

#### Half-Reaction Method for Balancing

Systematic steps for balancing redox reactions in aqueous media:

1. Write the two half-reactions representing the redox process.
2. Balance all elements except oxygen and hydrogen.
3. Balance oxygen atoms by adding H₂O molecules.
4. Balance hydrogen atoms by adding H⁺ ions.
5. Balance charge by adding electrons.
6. Multiply each half-reaction's coefficients by smallest possible integers to yield equal electron numbers.
7. Add balanced half-reactions and remove redundant species.
8. For reactions in basic media (excess OH⁻):
   - Add OH⁻ ions to both sides equal to H⁺ ion count
   - Combine H⁺ and OH⁻ to form H₂O
   - Simplify by removing redundant water molecules
9. Check atom and charge balance.

**Example:** Dichromate ion reacts with iron(II) in acidic solution:

Cr₂O₇²⁻ + Fe²⁺ → Cr³⁺ + Fe³⁺

Half-reactions:
Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻
Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O

Multiply first by 6 and add:
6Fe²⁺ + Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ → 6Fe³⁺ + 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O

## 4.3 Reaction Stoichiometry

### Definition of Stoichiometry

"The quantitative relationships between the amounts of substances consumed and produced by the reaction" are known as the reaction's stoichiometry. The term derives from Greek: *stoicheion* (element) and *metron* (measure).

A balanced chemical equation conveys both qualitative information (identities of reactants and products) and quantitative information (relative numbers of chemical species through coefficients).

### Stoichiometric Factors and Mole Ratios

Coefficients in balanced equations establish ratios between substances that can be used to derive stoichiometric conversion factors. These factors allow computation of desired quantities in any unit system.

**Example**: For the reaction N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g)

Stoichiometric factors relating ammonia and hydrogen:
- 2 NH₃ molecules / 3 H₂ molecules
- 2 doz NH₃ molecules / 3 doz H₂ molecules
- 2 mol NH₃ / 3 mol H₂

These factors enable calculation of product molecules from reactant molecules, or vice versa.

### Worked Examples

#### Example 4.8: Moles of Reactant Required

**Given equation**: 2Al + 3I₂ → 2AlI₃

**Question**: How many moles of I₂ are required to react with 0.429 mol of Al?

**Solution**:
0.429 mol Al × (3 mol I₂ / 2 mol Al) = 0.644 mol I₂

The stoichiometric factor (3 mol I₂ / 2 mol Al) derives directly from the balanced equation coefficients.

#### Example 4.9: Number of Product Molecules

**Given equation**: C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

**Question**: How many CO₂ molecules are produced when 0.75 mol of propane is combusted?

**Solution**:
0.75 mol C₃H₈ × (3 mol CO₂ / 1 mol C₃H₈) × (6.022 × 10²³ CO₂ molecules / mol CO₂) = 1.4 × 10²⁴ CO₂ molecules

This calculation converts moles to molecules using Avogadro's number.

#### Example 4.10: Relating Masses of Reactants and Products

**Given equation**: MgCl₂(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) → Mg(OH)₂(s) + 2NaCl(aq)

**Question**: What mass of NaOH is required to produce 16 g of Mg(OH)₂?

**Solution**:
16 g Mg(OH)₂ × (1 mol Mg(OH)₂ / 58.3 g Mg(OH)₂) × (2 mol NaOH / 1 mol Mg(OH)₂) × (40.0 g NaOH / mol NaOH) = 22 g NaOH

This demonstrates the conversion pathway: mass → moles (using molar mass) → moles of other substance (using stoichiometric factor) → mass (using molar mass).

#### Example 4.11: Relating Masses of Reactants

**Given equation**: 2C₈H₁₈ + 25O₂ → 16CO₂ + 18H₂O

**Question**: What mass of O₂ is consumed in the combustion of 702 g of octane?

**Solution**:
702 g C₈H₁₈ × (1 mol C₈H₁₈ / 114.23 g C₈H₁₈) × (25 mol O₂ / 2 mol C₈H₁₈) × (32.00 g O₂ / mol O₂) = 2.46 × 10³ g O₂

### General Stoichiometric Calculation Flowchart

The computational pathway for most stoichiometry problems:

**Starting with Mass of Substance A:**
- Mass of A ↔ Moles of A (conversion factor: molar mass)
- Moles of A ↔ Moles of B (conversion factor: stoichiometric ratio from balanced equation)
- Moles of B ↔ Mass of B (conversion factor: molar mass)

**Alternative pathways available:**
- Mass ↔ Volume (conversion factor: density)
- Moles ↔ Volume of solution (conversion factor: molarity)
- Moles ↔ Number of particles (conversion factor: Avogadro's number, 6.022 × 10²³)

Regardless of whether the problem involves volume, molarity, density, or pure mass, the essential component remains "the use of stoichiometric factors derived from balanced chemical equations."

## 4.4 Reaction Yields

### Learning Objectives

- Explain the concepts of theoretical yield and limiting reactants/reagents
- Derive the theoretical yield for a reaction under specified conditions
- Calculate the percent yield for a reaction

### Stoichiometric Amounts

"The relative amounts of reactants and products represented in a balanced chemical equation are often referred to as stoichiometric amounts." Previous calculations assumed other reactants were available in stoichiometric amounts or greater. This section addresses more realistic scenarios where reactants are not present in stoichiometric amounts.

### Limiting Reactant

#### Definition and Concept

A **limiting reactant** is the substance that is entirely consumed during a reaction, thus limiting the amount of product that can be generated. The **excess reactant** is any substance provided in greater than the stoichiometric amount needed.

#### Identifying the Limiting Reactant

**Method 1: Compare molar ratios**

Compute the molar amounts of each reactant provided and compare the ratio to the stoichiometric ratio in the balanced equation. The reactant present in less-than-stoichiometric amount is the limiting reactant.

**Method 2: Calculate expected product from each reactant**

Use each reactant amount separately to calculate the amount of product that would form according to the reaction's stoichiometry. The reactant yielding the lesser amount of product is the limiting reactant.

#### Example: H₂ and Cl₂ Reaction

For the reaction: H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g)

If 3 moles of H₂ and 2 moles of Cl₂ are combined:
- Provided ratio: 3:2 (or 1.5:1)
- Stoichiometric ratio: 1:1
- Since the provided ratio is greater than stoichiometric, hydrogen is in excess and chlorine is limiting

Using Method 2:
- Complete reaction of H₂: 3 mol H₂ × (2 mol HCl / 1 mol H₂) = 6 mol HCl
- Complete reaction of Cl₂: 2 mol Cl₂ × (2 mol HCl / 1 mol Cl₂) = 4 mol HCl

Chlorine yields less product, confirming it as the limiting reactant.

#### Worked Example: Silicon Nitride

Reaction: 3Si(s) + 2N₂(g) → Si₃N₄(s)

Given: 2.00 g Si and 1.50 g N₂

**Molar amounts:**
mol Si = 2.00 g Si × (1 mol Si / 28.09 g Si) = 0.0712 mol Si
mol N₂ = 1.50 g N₂ × (1 mol N₂ / 28.02 g N₂) = 0.0535 mol N₂

**Provided ratio:** 0.0712 mol Si / 0.0535 mol N₂ = 1.33 mol Si per 1 mol N₂
**Stoichiometric ratio:** 3 mol Si / 2 mol N₂ = 1.5 mol Si per 1 mol N₂

Since the provided ratio (1.33) is less than stoichiometric (1.5), silicon is the limiting reactant.

**Alternative approach—product calculation:**
From Si: 0.0712 mol Si × (1 mol Si₃N₄ / 3 mol Si) = 0.0237 mol Si₃N₄
From N₂: 0.0535 mol N₂ × (1 mol Si₃N₄ / 2 mol N₂) = 0.0268 mol Si₃N₄

Silicon yields less product, confirming it as limiting.

### Theoretical Yield

"The amount of product that may be produced by a reaction under specified conditions, as calculated per the stoichiometry of an appropriate balanced chemical equation, is called the theoretical yield of the reaction."

Theoretical yield is calculated using stoichiometry based on the limiting reactant.

### Actual Yield

"In practice, the amount of product obtained is called the actual yield, and it is often less than the theoretical yield for a number of reasons."

Reasons actual yield is less than theoretical:
- Side reactions that generate other products
- Incomplete reactions (partial reactions in weak acids/bases)
- Product loss during collection and recovery

### Percent Yield

**Formula:**
percent yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100%

Actual and theoretical yields may be expressed as masses, molar amounts, or other appropriate properties (e.g., volume for gases). Units must be consistent between numerator and denominator.

#### Worked Example: Copper Metal Reduction

Reaction: CuSO₄(aq) + Zn(s) → Cu(s) + ZnSO₄(aq)

Given: 1.274 g CuSO₄ with excess Zn; actual yield = 0.392 g Cu

**Theoretical yield calculation:**
1.274 g CuSO₄ × (1 mol CuSO₄ / 159.62 g CuSO₄) × (1 mol Cu / 1 mol CuSO₄) × (63.55 g Cu / 1 mol Cu) = 0.5072 g Cu

**Percent yield:**
percent yield = (0.392 g Cu / 0.5072 g Cu) × 100 = 77.3%

## 4.5 Quantitative Chemical Analysis

### Learning Objectives

- Describe fundamental aspects of titrations and gravimetric analysis
- Perform stoichiometric calculations using typical titration and gravimetric data

### Titration Analysis

Titration analysis is a quantitative technique for determining the concentration of a substance in a sample. In this method, a buret delivers incremental volumes of a solution with known concentration (the **titrant**) to a sample solution containing the substance to be measured (the **analyte**). The titrant and analyte undergo a chemical reaction of known stoichiometry.

#### Key Definitions

- **Equivalence point**: The point at which the titrant and analyte have completely reacted according to stoichiometric ratios
- **End point**: The volume of titrant actually measured when the reaction appears complete
- **Indicator**: Special dyes added to sample solutions to produce a visible color change at or near the equivalence point
- **Standard solution**: A solution containing a known concentration of titrant

#### Detection Methods

The equivalence point may be detected by:
1. Visual observation of distinct changes (e.g., cessation of bubble formation)
2. Color change from added indicators
3. Measurement of solution properties that change predictably during titration

Properly designed methods ensure negligible difference between the equivalence and end points.

#### Example Calculation: Titration Analysis

**Problem**: A 50.00 mL sample of aqueous HCl required 35.23 mL of 0.250 M NaOH to reach the end point.

Reaction: HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)

**Solution Steps**:

Moles of HCl:
35.23 mL NaOH × (1 L / 1000 mL) × (0.250 mol NaOH / 1 L) × (1 mol HCl / 1 mol NaOH) = 8.81 × 10⁻³ mol HCl

Molarity of HCl:
M = 8.81 × 10⁻³ mol / (50.00 mL × (1 L / 1000 mL)) = 0.176 M

**Shortcut using millimolar concentrations**:
35.23 mL NaOH × (0.250 mmol NaOH / mL) × (1 mmol HCl / 1 mmol NaOH) ÷ 50.00 mL = 0.176 M HCl

### Gravimetric Analysis

Gravimetric analysis determines the amount or concentration of a substance by subjecting a sample to treatment that causes a change in physical state, allowing separation of the analyte from other sample components. Mass measurements, combined with known stoichiometry, permit calculation of analyte concentration.

#### Methods

**Moisture determination**: Sample mass is measured before and after controlled heating that evaporates water.

**Precipitation method**: The analyte undergoes a precipitation reaction. The precipitate is isolated by filtration, dried, and weighed.

#### Example Calculation: Gravimetric Analysis

**Problem**: A 0.4550 g solid mixture containing MgSO₄ was dissolved in water and treated with excess Ba(NO₃)₂, producing 0.6168 g of BaSO₄.

Reaction: MgSO₄(aq) + Ba(NO₃)₂(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + Mg(NO₃)₂(aq)

**Solution**:

Mass of MgSO₄:
0.6168 g BaSO₄ × (1 mol BaSO₄ / 233.43 g) × (1 mol MgSO₄ / 1 mol BaSO₄) × (120.37 g MgSO₄ / 1 mol) = 0.3181 g MgSO₄

Mass percent:
Percent MgSO₄ = (0.3181 g / 0.4550 g) × 100% = 69.91%

### Combustion Analysis

Combustion analysis determines elemental composition of hydrocarbons and related compounds. A weighed sample is heated to high temperature under oxygen gas, resulting in complete combustion to yield gaseous products of known identities. For hydrocarbons, the only products are carbon dioxide and water.

#### Procedure

1. Weighed sample is combusted at high temperature in oxygen atmosphere
2. Gaseous products pass through separate, preweighed collection devices
3. Collection devices selectively absorb each product:
   - H₂O absorber (e.g., Mg(ClO₄)₂)
   - CO₂ absorber (e.g., NaOH)
4. Mass increase of each device corresponds to absorbed product mass
5. Stoichiometric calculations determine mass of relevant element

#### Example Calculation: Combustion Analysis

**Problem**: A 0.00126 g polyethylene sample yielded 0.00394 g CO₂ and 0.00161 g H₂O. Determine the empirical formula.

**Assumption**: All carbon converts to CO₂; all hydrogen converts to H₂O.

**General combustion equation**: CₓHᵧ(s) + excess O₂(g) → xCO₂(g) + (y/2)H₂O(g)

**Solution**:

Moles of carbon:
0.00394 g CO₂ × (1 mol CO₂ / 44.01 g) × (1 mol C / 1 mol CO₂) = 8.95 × 10⁻⁵ mol C

Moles of hydrogen:
0.00161 g H₂O × (1 mol H₂O / 18.02 g) × (2 mol H / 1 mol H₂O) = 1.79 × 10⁻⁴ mol H

H-to-C molar ratio:
(1.79 × 10⁻⁴ mol H) / (8.95 × 10⁻⁵ mol C) = 2 mol H / 1 mol C

**Empirical formula: CH₂**
