## 8.0 Introduction

The foundational concepts of chemical bonding establish that atoms share electrons to create molecules with stable Lewis structures, and VSEPR theory helps predict molecular shapes. However, these models have limitations in explaining real substance behavior. A key puzzle emerges: how can we reconcile the geometries of *s*, *p*, and *d* atomic orbitals with molecular shapes that show angles like 120° and 109.5°?

The chapter also examines electromagnetic phenomena related to electron behavior. While N₂ and O₂ possess similar Lewis structures with lone pairs, they exhibit strikingly different magnetic properties: liquid nitrogen passes through a magnetic field without interaction, whereas liquid oxygen is attracted to the magnet and floats in the magnetic field. Understanding these observations requires deeper investigation into valence bond theory, orbital hybridization, and molecular orbital theory — concepts that extend beyond basic bonding models.

## 8.1 Valence Bond Theory

A scientific theory must explain experimental data and predict behavior. Valence bond theory provides the explanation of chemical bonding that VSEPR theory lacks. While quantum mechanics can predict orbital shapes around free atoms (spherical for *s* orbitals, dumbbell-shaped for *p* orbitals), a more complete understanding of electron distributions requires a model that can account for the electronic structure of molecules.

### Fundamental principle

> "Valence bond theory describes a covalent bond as the overlap of half-filled atomic orbitals (each containing a single electron) that yield a pair of electrons shared between the two bonded atoms."

**Orbital overlap** occurs when "a portion of one orbital and a portion of a second orbital occupy the same region of space."

A covalent bond results when:
1. An orbital on one atom overlaps an orbital on a second atom, and
2. The single electrons in each orbital combine to form an electron pair.

> "The mutual attraction between this negatively charged electron pair and the two atoms' positively charged nuclei serves to physically link the two atoms through a force we define as a covalent bond."

### Energy and bond strength

> "The strength of a covalent bond depends on the extent of overlap of the orbitals involved. Orbitals that overlap extensively form bonds that are stronger than those that have less overlap."

As two atoms approach:
- At great distances there is no overlap; energy is set to zero by convention.
- As atoms move together, orbitals begin to overlap; each electron feels attraction from the other nucleus, while electrons repel each other and nuclei repel each other.
- While widely separated, attractions exceed repulsions, so system energy decreases (the bond begins to form).
- At the optimum distance, energy reaches its minimum (most stable) value — this distance is the **bond distance**.
- If the nuclei move closer than the optimal distance, nuclear repulsions and electron-confinement repulsions exceed attractions and energy rises (destabilizes).

**Bond energy** is "the difference between the energy minimum (which occurs at the bond distance) and the energy of the two separated atoms. This is the quantity of energy released when the bond is formed."

Example — H₂ molecule:
- Bond distance: 74 pm
- Energy difference: −7.24 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per bond
- Per mole: 4.36 × 10⁵ J required to break all H–H bonds (i.e., 436 kJ/mol)

### Orbital orientation and overlap quality

Beyond distance, orbital orientation affects overlap (except for spherically symmetric *s* orbitals). "Greater overlap is possible when orbitals are oriented such that they overlap on a direct line between the two nuclei." End-to-end overlap of *p* orbitals produces greater overlap than angled arrangements.

### Sigma (σ) bonds

> "A σ bond is a covalent bond in which the electron density is concentrated in the region along the internuclear axis; that is, a line between the nuclei would pass through the center of the overlap region."

σ bonds form from:
- Overlap of two *s* orbitals (as in H₂)
- Overlap of an *s* orbital and a *p* orbital (as in HCl)
- End-to-end overlap of two *p* orbitals (as in Cl₂)

> "Single bonds in Lewis structures are described as σ bonds in valence bond theory."

### Pi (π) bonds

> "A pi bond (π bond) is a type of covalent bond that results from the side-by-side overlap of two *p* orbitals."

> "In a π bond, the regions of orbital overlap lie on opposite sides of the internuclear axis. Along the axis itself, there is a node, that is, a plane with no probability of finding an electron."

### Multiple bonds: combinations of σ and π bonds

> "While all single bonds are σ bonds, multiple bonds consist of both σ and π bonds."

- **Single bond** (e.g., H–Cl): one σ bond, no π bonds
- **Double bond** (e.g., O₂): one σ bond + one π bond
- **Triple bond** (e.g., N₂): one σ bond + two π bonds

> "Between any two atoms, the first bond formed will always be a σ bond, but there can only be one σ bond in any one location. In any multiple bond, there will be one σ bond, and the remaining one or two bonds will be π bonds."

### Comparative bond strengths

π bonds are generally weaker than σ bonds because there is greater orbital overlap in a σ bond. Using carbon–carbon bonds:
- C–C single bond (all σ): 347 kJ/mol
- C=C double bond (σ + π): 347 + 267 = 614 kJ/mol total
- C≡C triple bond (σ + 2π): 614 + 225 = 839 kJ/mol total

> "Thus, each individual π bond is generally weaker than a corresponding σ bond between the same two atoms."

## 8.2 Hybrid Atomic Orbitals

Hybrid orbitals form when atomic orbitals combine in covalently bonded atoms. The concept addresses limitations in simple valence bond theory: oxygen's unpaired 2*p* orbitals in water are perpendicular (90°), yet experiment shows H₂O has a bond angle of 104.5°. Hybridization explains this discrepancy through the **linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO)**.

### Key principles

1. "Hybrid orbitals do not exist in isolated atoms. They are formed only in covalently bonded atoms."
2. Hybrid orbitals have shapes and orientations markedly different from atomic orbitals in isolated atoms.
3. "The number of hybrid orbitals in a set is equal to the number of atomic orbitals that were combined to produce the set."
4. All orbitals in a set of hybrid orbitals are equivalent in shape and energy.
5. The type of hybrid orbitals depends on the electron-pair geometry predicted by VSEPR theory.
6. "Hybrid orbitals overlap to form σ bonds. Unhybridized orbitals overlap to form π bonds."

### sp hybridization
- **Electron-density regions:** 2 · **Geometry:** linear · **Bond angle:** 180°
- The *s* orbital combines with one *p* orbital to produce two equivalent *sp* hybrid orbitals.
- Example — BeCl₂: beryllium's two valence electrons occupy two *sp* hybrids, which overlap with chlorine 3*p* orbitals to form two identical σ bonds. Other examples: HgCl₂, Zn(CH₃)₂, HCCH, CO₂.

### sp² hybridization
- **Electron-density regions:** 3 · **Geometry:** trigonal planar · **Bond angle:** 120°
- "The mixing of one s orbital and two p orbitals" produces three equivalent *sp²* hybrid orbitals; one unhybridized *p* orbital remains (along the z-axis).
- Example — BH₃: boron's three valence electrons occupy three *sp²* hybrids, each pairing with a hydrogen electron in a trigonal planar structure. Other examples: ClNO, formaldehyde (CH₂O), ethene (H₂C=CH₂).

### sp³ hybridization
- **Electron-density regions:** 4 · **Geometry:** tetrahedral · **Bond angle:** 109.5°
- The *s* orbital and all three *p* orbitals combine to produce four equivalent *sp³* hybrid orbitals; "each of these hybrid orbitals points toward a different corner of a tetrahedron."
- Example — CH₄ (methane): carbon's four valence electrons distribute equally in the hybrids; each pairs with a hydrogen 1*s* electron forming four equivalent C–H σ bonds.
- Example — C₂H₆ (ethane): each carbon is *sp³*; one *sp³* orbital of one carbon overlaps end-to-end with an *sp³* orbital of the second to form the C–C σ bond; remaining *sp³* orbitals overlap with hydrogen 1*s* orbitals. Rotation around σ bonds occurs easily.
- **Lone pairs:** *sp³* hybrids can hold lone pairs. In NH₃, nitrogen is *sp³* with one hybrid holding a lone pair (bond angle 107.3°). In H₂O, oxygen is *sp³* with two hybrids holding lone pairs and two holding bonding pairs (bond angle 104.5°). Lone pairs occupy more space, distorting angles below the ideal 109.5°. Other examples: CCl₄, PCl₃, NCl₃.

### sp³d hybridization
- **Electron-density regions:** 5 · **Geometry:** trigonal bipyramidal · **Bond angles:** 120° (equatorial) and 90° (axial)
- "To describe the five bonding orbitals in a trigonal bipyramidal arrangement, we must use five of the valence shell atomic orbitals (the s orbital, the three p orbitals, and one of the d orbitals)," yielding five *sp³d* hybrid orbitals.
- Example — PCl₅: phosphorus uses the 3*s*, three 3*p*, and one 3*d* orbital to form five *sp³d* hybrids directed toward the corners of a trigonal bipyramid. Other examples: SF₄, ClF₃, ClF₄⁺.

### sp³d² hybridization
- **Electron-density regions:** 6 · **Geometry:** octahedral · **Bond angle:** 90°
- "With an octahedral arrangement of six hybrid orbitals, we must use six valence shell atomic orbitals (the s orbital, the three p orbitals, and two of the d orbitals in its valence shell)," giving six *sp³d²* hybrid orbitals, each "directed toward a different corner of an octahedron."
- Example — SF₆: sulfur uses the 3*s*, three 3*p*, and two 3*d* orbitals to form six equivalent *sp³d²* hybrids; six S–F bonds result with no central lone pairs. Other examples: PCl₆⁻, IF₆⁺, IF₅, ICl₄⁻, IF₄⁻, XeF₄.

> Note: *sp³d* and *sp³d²* hybridizations are only possible for atoms with *d* orbitals in their valence subshells (i.e., not in the first or second period).

### Determining hybridization of central atoms

1. Determine the Lewis structure of the molecule.
2. "Determine the number of regions of electron density around an atom using VSEPR theory, in which single bonds, multiple bonds, radicals, and lone pairs each count as one region."
3. Assign hybridization from the electron-density count:
   - 2 regions → *sp*
   - 3 regions → *sp²*
   - 4 regions → *sp³*
   - 5 regions → *sp³d*
   - 6 regions → *sp³d²*

The arrangements predicted by hybridization are identical to the electron-pair geometries predicted by VSEPR theory.

Worked examples:
- **Sulfate ion (SO₄²⁻):** sulfur has four regions (four S–O bonds) → *sp³*.
- **SeF₄:** four fluorines plus one lone pair = five regions → *sp³d*.
- **Urea (NH₂C(O)NH₂):** the carbon has one C=O double bond and two N single bonds = three regions (trigonal planar) → *sp²*.
- **Acetic acid (H₃CC(O)OH):** the methyl carbon has four bonding regions → *sp³*; the carboxyl carbon has three regions (C=O, C–O, C–C) → *sp²*.

### Limitations and context

Hybridization works well for small central atoms with closely spaced valence electron pairs. For larger central atoms farther from the nucleus, with fewer repulsions, observed structures often differ from VSEPR predictions and do not require hybridization. For example, H₂S has a bond angle of 92.1° (versus water's 104.5°), indicating less hybridization on sulfur; H₂Te has an observed angle of 90°, consistent with pure 5*p* orbital overlap without hybridization. "We invoke hybridization where it is necessary to explain the observed structures."

## 8.3 Multiple Bonds

The hybrid orbital model accounts for molecular geometry in molecules with single, double, and triple covalent bonds. Multiple bonds comprise σ (sigma) and π (pi) bonds, which differ fundamentally in their orbital overlap and rotational properties.

### Double bonds: ethene (C₂H₄)

In ethene, each carbon bonds to one other carbon and two hydrogens, creating three bonding regions → trigonal planar electron-pair geometry → *sp²* hybridization (hybridization of two of the 2*p* orbitals and the 2*s* orbital).

- **σ framework:** the *sp²* hybrids form all σ bonds — one C–C σ bond (sp²–sp² overlap) and four C–H σ bonds (sp²–s overlap).
- **π bond:** the unhybridized *p* orbital on each carbon remains after hybridization and is perpendicular to the plane of the *sp²* hybrids; these *p* orbitals "overlap in a side-by-side fashion, above and below the internuclear axis" to form a single π bond.
- **Planarity:** the molecule is planar because that is the most stable arrangement; if the two planes of *sp²* hybrids tilted relative to each other, "the p orbitals would not be oriented to overlap efficiently to create the π bond."
- **Rotation:** "rotation around single (σ) bonds occurs easily because the end-to-end orbital overlap does not depend on the relative orientation of the orbitals on each atom." By contrast, "rotation about the internuclear axis is much more difficult for multiple bonds since this would drastically decrease the off-axis overlap of the π bonding orbitals, essentially breaking the π bond."

### Triple bonds: acetylene (C₂H₂)

Acetylene is linear; each carbon is *sp* hybridized. "In molecules with sp hybrid orbitals, two unhybridized p orbitals remain on the atom."

- **σ framework:** the *sp* hybrids of the two carbons "overlap end to end to form a σ bond between the carbon atoms," and the remaining *sp* orbitals "form σ bonds with hydrogen atoms" — two C–H σ bonds and one C–C σ bond.
- **π bonds:** the two unhybridized *p* orbitals on each carbon "overlap side by side and, hence, form two π bonds." So acetylene contains "one σ bond and two π bonds, giving a triple bond."

### Determining hybridization in multiply-bonded atoms

> "Hybridization involves only σ bonds, lone pairs of electrons, and single unpaired electrons (radicals). Structures that account for these features describe the correct hybridization of the atoms."

The presence or arrangement of π bonds does not affect hybridization assignment: "Since the arrangement of π bonds involves only the unhybridized orbitals, resonance does not influence the assignment of hybridization."

### Resonance and delocalized π systems

In molecules with multiple resonance structures (e.g., benzene), each carbon remains *sp²* hybridized "independently of which resonance form is considered." The π electrons are "not located in one set of p orbitals or the other, but rather delocalized throughout the molecule."

> "Valence bond theory does not easily address delocalization. Bonding in molecules with resonance forms is better described by molecular orbital theory."

Example — SO₂: sulfur dioxide has two resonance forms with one S–O double bond and one S–O single bond (positions interchanging). The sulfur is "surrounded by two bonds and one lone pair of electrons in either resonance structure. Therefore, the electron-pair geometry is trigonal planar, and the hybridization of the sulfur atom is sp²."

## 8.4 Molecular Orbital Theory

Molecular orbital (MO) theory explains chemical bonding phenomena that Lewis structures cannot. Most notably, it explains why O₂ is paramagnetic (attracted to a magnetic field) despite having all paired electrons in its Lewis structure. Magnetic-susceptibility measurements (a Gouy balance) show that "Each O₂ molecule has two unpaired electrons. The Lewis-structure model does not predict the presence of these two unpaired electrons."

### Core principles

Unlike valence bond theory, which assigns orbitals to specific atoms, "MO theory uses the combination of atomic orbitals to yield molecular orbitals that are *delocalized* over the entire molecule rather than being localized on its constituent atoms." MO theory:
- considers electrons delocalized throughout the entire molecule,
- creates bonding and antibonding interactions based on orbital overlap,
- predicts the arrangement of electrons in molecules, and
- can handle cases where Lewis structures fail.

### Linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO)

> "The mathematical process of combining atomic orbitals to generate molecular orbitals is called the linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO)."

When atomic orbital wave functions combine:
1. **Constructive interference** — "in-phase waves producing regions with a higher probability of electron density" (bonding).
2. **Destructive interference** — "out-of-phase waves producing nodes, or regions of no electron density" (antibonding).

### σ and σ* molecular orbitals

From overlap of two *s* atomic orbitals:
- **σₛ bonding orbital:** "In-phase combination produces a lower energy σₛ molecular orbital in which most of the electron density is directly between the nuclei."
- **σₛ* antibonding orbital:** "Out-of-phase addition produces a higher energy σₛ* molecular orbital in which there is a node between the nuclei."

> "Electrons in a σₛ orbital are attracted by both nuclei at the same time and are more stable (of lower energy) than they would be in the isolated atoms... electrons in the σₛ* orbitals are located well away from the region between the two nuclei."

The asterisk (*) signifies an antibonding orbital. Electrons fill lower-energy bonding orbitals before higher-energy antibonding orbitals (Aufbau principle).

### π and π* molecular orbitals

When *p* orbitals overlap end-to-end along the internuclear axis they form σₚ and σₚ* orbitals (analogous to *s*-orbital combinations). Side-by-side *p*-orbital overlap creates π orbitals:
- **π bonding orbital:** "Electrons in this orbital interact with both nuclei and help hold the two atoms together, making it a bonding orbital."
- **π* antibonding orbital:** "For the out-of-phase combination, there are two nodal planes created, one along the internuclear axis and a perpendicular one between the nuclei."

For diatomic molecules, each atom has two sets of side-by-side *p* orbitals (*pᵧ* and *pz*), creating two π bonding orbitals and two π* antibonding orbitals. "The πₚᵧ and πₚz orbitals are identical and have the same energy; they are degenerate orbitals."

### Molecular orbital energy diagrams

A MO diagram shows atomic orbitals of one atom on the left, the second atom on the right, and the molecular orbitals in the center; each horizontal line is one orbital (max 2 electrons). "For each pair of atomic orbitals that combine, one lower-energy (bonding) molecular orbital and one higher-energy (antibonding) orbital result." Electrons fill by the Aufbau principle (lowest first), with degenerate orbitals receiving single electrons before pairing (Hund's rule), max two electrons of opposite spin per orbital.

### Bond order

> "The bond order is calculated by subtracting the destabilizing (antibonding) electrons from the stabilizing (bonding) electrons. Since a bond consists of two electrons, we divide by two to get the bond order."

$$\text{bond order} = \frac{(\text{number of bonding electrons}) - (\text{number of antibonding electrons})}{2}$$

"The order of a covalent bond is a guide to its strength; a bond between two given atoms becomes stronger as the bond order increases." A bond order of zero indicates no stable bond forms.

### Homonuclear diatomic molecules

- **H₂:** (σ₁ₛ)²; bond order = (2 − 0)/2 = 1 (single bond). H₂ readily forms because its energy is lower than that of two H atoms.
- **He₂ (hypothetical):** (σ₁ₛ)²(σ₁ₛ*)²; bond order = (2 − 2)/2 = 0 → no stable bond; helium exists as discrete atoms.

**Second-period elements.** The order of MO energies differs between lighter and heavier elements due to **s-p mixing**: "s-p mixing does not create new orbitals; it merely influences the energies of the existing molecular orbitals. The σₛ wavefunction mathematically combines with the σₚ wavefunction, with the result that the σₛ orbital becomes more stable, and the σₚ orbital becomes less stable."
- For Li, Be, B, C, N (three or fewer *p* electrons), the *s–p* energy difference is small enough that mixing occurs, raising the σ₂ₚ orbital above the π₂ₚ orbitals.
- For O, F, Ne, the larger *s–p* energy difference makes mixing negligible (not sufficient to change ordering), so their MO diagrams follow the normal pattern (σ₂ₚ below π₂ₚ).

| Molecule | Electron configuration | Bond order | Stability |
|----------|------------------------|------------|-----------|
| Li₂ | (σ₂ₛ)² | 1 | Stable |
| Be₂ | (σ₂ₛ)²(σ₂ₛ*)² | 0 | Unstable |
| B₂ | (σ₂ₛ)²(σ₂ₛ*)²(π₂ₚᵧ, π₂ₚz)² | 1 | Stable |
| C₂ | (σ₂ₛ)²(σ₂ₛ*)²(π₂ₚᵧ, π₂ₚz)⁴ | 2 | Stable |
| N₂ | (σ₂ₛ)²(σ₂ₛ*)²(π₂ₚᵧ, π₂ₚz)⁴(σ₂ₚₓ)² | 3 | Stable |
| O₂ | (σ₂ₛ)²(σ₂ₛ*)²(σ₂ₚₓ)²(π₂ₚᵧ, π₂ₚz)⁴(π₂ₚᵧ*, π₂ₚz*)² | 2 | Stable |
| F₂ | (σ₂ₛ)²(σ₂ₛ*)²(σ₂ₚₓ)²(π₂ₚᵧ, π₂ₚz)⁴(π₂ₚᵧ*, π₂ₚz*)⁴ | 1 | Stable |
| Ne₂ | (σ₂ₛ)²(σ₂ₛ*)²(σ₂ₚₓ)²(π₂ₚᵧ, π₂ₚz)⁴(π₂ₚᵧ*, π₂ₚz*)⁴(σ₂ₚₓ*)² | 0 | Unstable |

### Oxygen and paramagnetism

O₂ has bond order 2. Crucially, "The O₂ molecule has enough electrons to half fill the (π₂ₚᵧ*, π₂ₚz*) level. We expect the two electrons that occupy these two degenerate orbitals to be unpaired." By Hund's rule, the two electrons occupy the degenerate π* orbitals singly before pairing, giving O₂ two unpaired electrons. "In fact, the unpaired electrons of the oxygen molecule provide a strong piece of support for the molecular orbital theory," because Lewis structures cannot predict this paramagnetism.

- **Paramagnetic:** "Attraction to a magnetic field arises in molecules that have unpaired electrons."
- **Diamagnetic:** "Materials in which all of the electrons are paired are diamagnetic and weakly repel a magnetic field."

"Magnetic susceptibility measures the force experienced by a substance in a magnetic field." A Gouy balance compares a sample's weight with and without a magnetic field to determine the number of unpaired electrons.

### Band theory (extended application)

In solids with vast numbers of atoms (>10²³), closely spaced molecular orbitals form bands:
- **Valence band:** all bonding orbitals packed closely in energy.
- **Conduction band:** all antibonding orbitals packed closely in energy.

Conduction depends on the **band gap** (energy difference between bands): conductors have a very small gap (electrons move easily), semiconductors have a moderate gap (conduct under moderate energy input), and insulators have a large gap (few electrons move; poor conductivity).
