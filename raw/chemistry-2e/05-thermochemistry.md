## Introduction

Chemical reactions — including those as simple as striking a match — involve both energy and matter transformations. Modern societies depend on energy released through chemical processes. In 2012, approximately 85% of U.S. energy consumption derived from combusting petroleum products, coal, wood, and garbage. This energy powered electricity generation (38%), transportation (27%), industrial processes (21%), and heating/cooling of buildings (10%). Despite meeting critical energy needs, these combustion reactions contribute significantly to climate change.

Beyond combustion, many other chemical reactions provide useful energy: batteries in phones, vehicles, and flashlights generate energy through chemical reactions. This chapter explores the fundamental concepts connecting chemical changes to energy, emphasizing thermal-energy relationships.

## 5.1 Energy Basics

### Core Definitions

- **Energy**: the capacity to supply heat or do work.
- **Work (w)**: the process of causing matter to move against an opposing force.
- **Potential energy**: energy an object possesses because of its relative position, composition, or condition.
- **Kinetic energy**: energy an object possesses because of its motion.
- **Thermal energy**: kinetic energy associated with the random motion of atoms and molecules.
- **Temperature**: a quantitative measure of "hot" or "cold," reflecting the average kinetic energy of atoms and molecules in a substance.
- **Heat (q)**: the transfer of thermal energy between two bodies at different temperatures.
- **Exothermic process**: a change that releases heat.
- **Endothermic process**: a reaction or change that absorbs heat.

### Law of Conservation of Energy

"During a chemical or physical change, energy can be neither created nor destroyed, although it can be changed in form." This is also the **first law of thermodynamics**.

### Heat and Temperature Relationship

When two substances at different temperatures are placed in contact, thermal energy flows spontaneously from the higher-temperature substance to the lower-temperature substance until **thermal equilibrium** is reached — both substances at the same temperature, with equal average kinetic energy in their molecules. When thermal energy increases in a sample with no chemical reaction or phase change, temperature increases; when thermal energy decreases (no reaction or phase change), temperature decreases.

### Heat Capacity and Specific Heat

- **Heat capacity (C)**: the quantity of heat (q) a body absorbs or releases when it experiences a temperature change (ΔT) of 1 degree Celsius (equivalently, 1 kelvin).

  **C = q / ΔT**

  Heat capacity is an *extensive* property (proportional to the amount of substance).

- **Specific heat capacity (c)** ("specific heat"): the quantity of heat required to raise the temperature of 1 gram of a substance by 1 degree Celsius (or 1 kelvin).

  **c = q / (m × ΔT)**

  Specific heat is an *intensive* property (depends only on the type of substance).

### Calculating Heat Transfer

**q = c × m × ΔT = c × m × (T_final − T_initial)**

where q = heat absorbed/released, c = specific heat capacity, m = mass, ΔT = temperature change.

**Sign convention for q**:
- Positive q: substance gains thermal energy; temperature increases.
- Negative q: substance loses thermal energy; temperature decreases.

### Units

- **Calorie (cal)**: energy required to raise 1 gram of water by 1 °C.
- **Calorie (Cal)** with capital C (food/nutritional calorie): equals one kilocalorie (kcal).
- **Joule (J)**: the SI unit of heat, work, and energy; the energy when a force of 1 newton moves an object 1 meter. 1 J = 1 kg·m²/s² = 1 N·m.
- 1 kJ = 1000 J.
- **1 calorie = 4.184 joules.**

### Specific Heat Values (at 25 °C and 1 bar)

| Substance | Specific heat (J/g·°C) |
|---|---|
| Water (liquid) | 4.184 |
| Water (ice, at −10 °C) | 2.093 |
| Water (vapor) | 1.864 |
| Ethanol | 2.376 |
| Iron | 0.449 |
| Aluminum | 0.897 |
| Copper | 0.385 |
| Lead | 0.130 |
| Gold | 0.129 |

Water has a relatively high specific heat (~4.2 J/g·°C liquid); most metals have much lower specific heats (typically < 1 J/g·°C).

### Worked Examples (takeaways)

- **Heating water**: 8.0 × 10² g water from 21 °C to 85 °C → q = (4.184)(800)(64) = 210,000 J = 2.1 × 10² kJ (positive: water absorbs heat).
- **Iron skillet**: 502 g iron, 25 °C → 250 °C, c = 0.449 → q = 5.07 × 10⁴ J.
- **Identifying a metal**: 348 g metal absorbs 6.64 kJ, 22.4 °C → 43.6 °C → c = 6640 / (348 × 21.2) = 0.900 J/g·°C, matching aluminum.

### Applications

Over 90% of currently used energy originates from the sun. Solar thermal systems concentrate sunlight to heat a substance, generating electricity directly or storing heat in molten salts. Water is used because of its large heat capacity and heat of vaporization.

## 5.2 Calorimetry

### Definitions

- **Calorimetry**: a technique used to measure amounts of heat transferred to or from a substance. The measurement requires defining a **system** (the substance undergoing chemical or physical change) and its **surroundings** (all other matter that provides or absorbs heat).
- **Calorimeter**: a device used to measure the amount of heat involved in a chemical or physical process.
- **Coffee-cup calorimeter** (constant pressure): two nested polystyrene cups with an insulated cover, a thermometer, and a stirring rod. Easy to use but allow more heat exchange with the environment, producing less accurate energy values than well-insulated calorimeters.
- **Bomb calorimeter** (constant volume): a robust steel container ("the bomb") submerged in water, filled with reactants and oxygen at high pressure and ignited by an electrical spark. Used for reactions producing large heat amounts and gaseous products (combustion).

### Core Principle: Heat Transfer Between Substances

In an isolated calorimeter, heat flows from the hot substance to the cool substance until thermal equilibrium is reached. Under ideal conditions with no external heat exchange, for substances M and W:

**q_substance M + q_substance W = 0**  →  **q_substance M = −q_substance W**

The heat gained by one substance equals the heat lost by the other. The negative sign indicates opposite directions of heat flow (gain vs. loss).

### Sign Convention

- **Exothermic process**: q is negative; heat transfers from system to surroundings; surroundings' temperature increases.
- **Endothermic process**: q is positive; heat transfers from surroundings to system; surroundings' temperature decreases.

### Heat Calculations

Heat absorbed/released by a substance: **q = c × m × ΔT** (c in J/g·°C, m in g, ΔT in °C or K).

For a chemical reaction carried out in solution within a calorimeter:

**q_reaction + q_solution = 0**  →  **q_reaction = −q_solution**

### Worked Examples (takeaways)

- **Ex 5.3 — Rebar & water**: 360.0 g steel rebar into 425 mL water at 24.0 °C, final 42.7 °C, c_steel ≈ 0.449 → initial rebar temperature ≈ 248 °C.
- **Ex 5.4 — Metal ID**: 59.7 g metal from boiling water (100.0 °C) into 60.0 mL water at 22.0 °C, final 28.5 °C → c = 0.38 J/g·°C → copper (lit. 0.39).
- **Ex 5.5 — HCl + NaOH**: 50.0 mL 1.00 M HCl + 50.0 mL 1.00 M NaOH, both 22.0 °C, max temp 28.9 °C → q = −2.9 kJ (exothermic).
- **Ex 5.6 — Ice pack (NH₄NO₃)**: 3.21 g NH₄NO₃ in 50.0 g water, 24.9 °C → 20.3 °C → q_reaction = +1.0 kJ (endothermic).
- **Ex 5.7 — Glucose combustion (bomb)**: 3.12 g glucose, 23.8 °C → 35.6 °C, 775 g water, bomb heat capacity 893 J/°C → q = −48.8 kJ.

### Additional Concepts

- **Nutritional Calories**: 1 Calorie (kilocalorie) = 1000 cal = 4.184 kJ. Macronutrients provide ~4 Cal/g (protein, carbohydrates) or ~9 Cal/g (fats).
- **Bomb calorimeter calibration**: performed with a known reaction (e.g., benzoic acid) to determine the calorimeter's heat capacity before research measurements.

## 5.3 Enthalpy

### Internal Energy (U or E)

The total of all possible kinds of energy present in a substance is called the **internal energy (U)**, sometimes symbolized **E**. Energy is stored when the kinetic energy of atoms/molecules increases through translations, vibrations, or rotations; when thermal energy is lost, these motions and kinetic energy decrease.

### First Law of Thermodynamics

**ΔU = q + w**

where ΔU = change in internal energy; q = heat (positive when absorbed by the system, negative when released); w = work (positive when done on the system, negative when done by the system). The internal energy of a system changes through heat flow into/out of the system or work done on/by the system.

### Expansion (Pressure-Volume) Work

Expansion work occurs when a system pushes back the surroundings against a restraining pressure, or when the surroundings compress the system:

**PΔV = −w**

The arithmetic signs of ΔV and w are always opposite.

### State Function

The value of a **state function** depends only on the state a system is in, not on how that state is reached. If a quantity is not a state function, its value depends on the path taken. **Internal energy and enthalpy are state functions; heat and work are not.** Analogy: altitude at the summit of Mt. Kilimanjaro (5895 m) is a state function regardless of route; distance traveled is not.

### Enthalpy

**H = U + PV**

where H = enthalpy, U = internal energy, P = pressure, V = volume. Enthalpy is a state function. Absolute enthalpy values cannot be measured directly — only enthalpy *changes* for processes can be determined.

At constant pressure with only expansion work:

**ΔH = ΔU + PΔV = (q_p + w) − w = q_p**

So at constant pressure (only expansion work), the heat flow (q_p) and the enthalpy change (ΔH) for the process are equal.

### Sign Conventions for ΔH

- **Negative ΔH (ΔH < 0)**: exothermic reaction (releases heat).
- **Positive ΔH (ΔH > 0)**: endothermic reaction (absorbs heat).
- Reversing a chemical equation changes the arithmetic sign of its ΔH (endothermic in one direction is exothermic in the reverse).

### Thermochemical Equations

A **thermochemical equation** represents the changes in both matter and energy: the enthalpy change is shown as a ΔH value following the chemical equation. The ΔH value applies to the number of moles of reactants and products *as written*.

Example: H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l)   ΔH = −286 kJ
(1 mol H₂ + ½ mol O₂ → 1 mol H₂O releases 286 kJ.)

**Extensive-property rule**: if the coefficients are multiplied by a factor, ΔH is multiplied by the same factor.
- 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)   ΔH = 2 × (−286) = −572 kJ
- ½H₂(g) + ¼O₂(g) → ½H₂O(l)   ΔH = ½ × (−286) = −143 kJ

**Physical-state dependence**: ΔH depends on the physical states of reactants and products, so states must be shown.
- H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l)   ΔH = −286 kJ
- H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(g)   ΔH = −242 kJ

### Standard State and Standard Enthalpy

A **standard state** is an accepted set of reference conditions. The IUPAC standard state refers to materials at a pressure of **1 bar** and solutions at **1 M** and does not specify a temperature (many thermochemical tables use 1 atm). Notation: **ΔH°** denotes the enthalpy change under standard-state conditions; the standard temperature is usually **298.15 K (25 °C)** unless otherwise specified. ΔH without the superscript denotes nonstandard conditions.

### Standard Enthalpy of Combustion (ΔHc°)

The enthalpy change when 1 mole of a substance burns (combines vigorously with oxygen) under standard-state conditions.

Example: C₂H₅OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l)   ΔH° = −1366.8 kJ.

| Substance | Reaction | ΔHc° (kJ/mol at 25 °C) |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon | C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g) | −393.5 |
| Hydrogen | H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l) | −285.8 |
| Methane | CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l) | −890.8 |
| Ethanol | C₂H₅OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l) | −1366.8 |
| Isooctane | C₈H₁₈(l) + 25/2 O₂(g) → 8CO₂(g) + 9H₂O(l) | −5465.5 |

### Standard Enthalpy of Formation (ΔHf°)

The enthalpy change for a reaction in which exactly **1 mole** of a pure substance is formed from free elements in their **most stable states** under standard-state conditions.

**Key rule**: the standard enthalpy of formation of an element in its most stable form is **zero** under standard conditions.

Examples:
- CO₂(g): C(s, graphite) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)   ΔHf° = −393.5 kJ/mol
- NO₂(g): ½N₂(g) + O₂(g) → NO₂(g)   ΔHf° = +33.2 kJ/mol (½ mol N₂ because ΔHf° always refers to 1 mol of product)
- C₂H₅OH(l): 2C(s, graphite) + 3H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → C₂H₅OH(l)
- Ca₃(PO₄)₂(s): 3Ca(s) + ½P₄(s) + 4O₂(g) → Ca₃(PO₄)₂(s) (carbon's standard state is graphite; phosphorus exists as P₄)

### Hess's Law

If a process can be written as the sum of several stepwise processes, the enthalpy change of the total process equals the sum of the enthalpy changes of the steps. Hess's law is valid because **enthalpy is a state function**: ΔH depends only on the start and end states, not the path.

Direct: C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)   ΔH° = −394 kJ
Two-step:
- C(s) + ½O₂(g) → CO(g)   ΔH° = −111 kJ
- CO(g) + ½O₂(g) → CO₂(g)   ΔH° = −283 kJ
- Sum: −111 + (−283) = −394 kJ ✓

**Two features of ΔH used in Hess's-law manipulations:**
1. **Direct proportionality**: multiply coefficients by n → multiply ΔH by n. (½N₂ + O₂ → NO₂, ΔH = +33.2 kJ; N₂ + 2O₂ → 2NO₂, ΔH = +66.4 kJ.)
2. **Reversibility**: reversing a reaction reverses the sign of ΔH. (H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, ΔH = −184.6 kJ; 2HCl → H₂ + Cl₂, ΔH = +184.6 kJ.)

### Calculating ΔH from Formation Enthalpies

**ΔH°_reaction = Σ n × ΔHf°(products) − Σ n × ΔHf°(reactants)**

where n = stoichiometric coefficients. Rationale: the reaction can be viewed as (1) decomposing reactants into their elements (−ΔHf° of reactants) and (2) recombining elements into products (+ΔHf° of products).

Example — 3NO₂(g) + H₂O(l) → 2HNO₃(aq) + NO(g) (ΔHf°: HNO₃(aq) −207.4; NO₂(g) +33.2; H₂O(l) −285.83; NO(g) +90.25 kJ/mol):
ΔH°_rxn = [2(−207.4) + 1(+90.25)] − [3(+33.2) + 1(−285.83)] = (−324.55) − (−186.23) = **−138.4 kJ**.

### Worked Examples (takeaways)

- **Ex 5.8 — HCl + NaOH neutralization**: 0.0500 mol each → 2.9 kJ released → for 1 mol HCl, ΔH = −2.9 kJ / 0.0500 mol = **−58 kJ**.
- **Ex 5.9 — Sucrose + KClO₃**: 2.67 g sucrose (0.00780 mol), 7.19 g KClO₃ (0.0587 mol) → 43.7 kJ. Ratio 7.52 < 8 ⇒ KClO₃ limiting. ΔH = −43.7 / 0.0587 = −744 kJ/mol KClO₃ → for the balanced equation (8 mol KClO₃): **−5960 kJ**. Lesson: identify the limiting reactant.
- **Ex 5.10 — Gasoline combustion**: 1.00 L isooctane (ρ = 0.692 g/mL, ΔHc° = −5460 kJ/mol) → 1000 × 0.692 / 114 × (−5460) = **−3.31 × 10⁴ kJ** (enough to melt 99.2 kg ice).
- **Ex 5.11 — Ozone formation**: 3O₂(g) → 2O₃(g), ΔH° = +286 kJ → ΔHf°[O₃(g)] = +286 / 2 = **+143 kJ/mol**.
- **Ex 5.13 — FeCl₃ via Hess's law**: Fe + Cl₂ → FeCl₂ (−341.8) + FeCl₂ + ½Cl₂ → FeCl₃ (−57.7) → ΔHf°[FeCl₃(s)] = **−399.5 kJ/mol**.
- **Ex 5.14 — ClF + F₂ → ClF₃**: combine half of (iii), reversed-and-halved (ii), and reversed (iv) → ΔH° = (+107.0) + (+24.7) + (−236.2) = **−104.5 kJ**.
- **Ex 5.15 — HNO₃ reaction via ΔHf°**: confirms **−138.4 kJ** (matches the formula above and the element-decomposition route).
