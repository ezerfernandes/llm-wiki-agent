## 20.0 Introduction

All living organisms on Earth consist primarily of carbon compounds, earning the designation "carbon-based" life. Early chemists believed substances extracted from organisms represented a unique category of matter that could not be created artificially, dubbing them "organic compounds." A prevailing theory called **vitalism** held that only living things possessed the vital force necessary to synthesize organic molecules.

German chemist **Friedrich Wöhler** challenged this belief in 1828 by synthesizing **urea** — a compound found in body fluids — from non-living materials. This breakthrough demonstrated that organic molecules follow the same natural laws as inorganic substances.

Today, organic compounds are recognized as both natural and synthetic substances containing carbon as their principal element, bonded to hydrogen and other carbon atoms. Notably, some carbon-containing compounds — carbonates, cyanides, CO, and CO₂ — are *not* classified as organic.

Modern organic compounds are essential components in plastics, soaps, perfumes, sweeteners, fabrics, pharmaceuticals, and countless everyday products, making organic chemistry a vital scientific discipline.

## 20.1 Hydrocarbons

**Learning objectives:** explain the importance of hydrocarbons and the reason for their diversity; name saturated and unsaturated hydrocarbons and molecules derived from them; describe the reactions characteristic of saturated and unsaturated hydrocarbons; identify structural and geometric isomers of hydrocarbons.

The largest database of organic compounds lists approximately 10 million substances, with an estimated 10⁶⁰ potential organic compounds. This vast number results from carbon's ability to form up to four strong bonds to other carbon atoms, resulting in chains and rings of many different sizes, shapes, and complexities.

The simplest organic compounds contain only carbon and hydrogen and are called **hydrocarbons**. They exhibit wide variety due to varying chain lengths, branched structures, ring formations, and different types of carbon–carbon bonds. Hydrocarbons occur naturally in plants, animals, and fossils, and are synthesized in laboratories. Common uses include fuels (natural gas, acetylene, propane, butane, gasoline, diesel, heating oil) and plastics (polyethylene, polypropylene, polystyrene).

### Alkanes (Saturated Hydrocarbons)

**Alkanes**, or **saturated hydrocarbons**, contain only single covalent bonds between carbon atoms. Each carbon atom has **sp³ hybrid orbitals** and bonds to four other atoms (carbon or hydrogen). Due to sp³ hybridization, bond angles are approximately **109.5°**, creating zigzag shapes in carbon chains.

**Structural representations:** Lewis structures (all atoms and bonds explicit); ball-and-stick models; space-filling models; condensed structural formulas (fewer bond symbols shown); and skeletal (line-angle) structures (carbons at line ends/bends, attached H omitted, other atoms shown by symbol).

**General formula** for noncyclic alkanes: **CₙH₂ₙ₊₂**.

Melting and boiling points change smoothly and predictably as the number of carbon and hydrogen atoms increases, due to stronger intermolecular (dispersion) forces.

#### Isomerism

Hydrocarbons with the same molecular formula can have different structures. Two alkanes exist with formula C₄H₁₀:
- **n-butane** — an unbranched chain (no carbon bonded to more than two other carbons).
- **2-methylpropane** (isobutane) — a branched chain (center carbon bonded to three other carbons).

**Structural isomers** (also **constitutional isomers**) have the same molecular formula but different spatial arrangements of the atoms in their molecules. Lewis structures that look different may represent the same molecule (e.g., three different-looking drawings of n-butane are all identical because each has an unbranched chain of four carbons).

#### IUPAC nomenclature for alkanes

The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) established the nomenclature rules.

**Rule 1 — longest carbon chain:** ethane (2 C), propane (3), butane (4), pentane (5), hexane (6), heptane (7), octane (8), nonane (9), decane (10).

**Rule 2 — substituents:** add prefixes to indicate substituent positions and names. **Substituents** are branches or functional groups that replace hydrogen atoms on a chain. Number carbons from the end nearest the substituents; name multiple substituents alphabetically; use position numbers; use prefixes (di-, tri-, tetra-) for multiple identical substituents. For electronegative substituents the suffix changes from "-ide" (ionic) to "-o" (organic): chloride → chloro, fluoride → fluoro.

**Alkyl groups:** a substituent containing one less hydrogen than the corresponding alkane. Name by dropping "-ane" and adding "-yl": methane → methyl (CH₃–), ethane → ethyl (CH₃CH₂–), propane → n-propyl or isopropyl, butane → n-butyl / sec-butyl / isobutyl / tert-butyl. Hydrogen atoms occupy different environments — **primary** (carbon bonded to one other carbon), **secondary** (two others), **tertiary** (three others). 2-methylpropane has all three types.

#### Alkane reactions

**Combustion** — a highly exothermic oxidation-reduction reaction:

CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(g)

Alkanes burn readily in oxygen, making them excellent fuels. Methane is the principal component of natural gas; butane (C₄H₁₀) is used in camping stoves and lighters; gasoline contains continuous- and branched-chain alkanes with five to nine carbons plus additives. Kerosene, diesel oil, and fuel oil are alkane mixtures of higher molecular mass. Crude oil is separated by **fractional distillation**, which exploits boiling-point differences.

**Substitution reaction** — one or more hydrogen atoms are replaced with a different atom or group. No carbon–carbon bonds break and carbon hybridization is unchanged:

CH₃CH₃ + Cl₂ → CH₃CH₂Cl + HCl  (ethane + chlorine → chloroethane + hydrogen chloride)

The C–Cl portion of chloroethane is a **functional group** — the part or moiety of a molecule that imparts a specific chemical reactivity. Functional groups are major determinants of chemical properties and the classification of organic compounds.

### Alkenes (double bonds)

**Unsaturated** organic compounds contain one or more double or triple bonds between carbon atoms. **Alkenes** are unsaturated hydrocarbons with one or more **C=C double bonds**. The double bond is one **σ (sigma) bond** plus one **π (pi) bond**. Carbon atoms in a double bond are **sp² hybridized**, trigonal planar, with **120°** bond angles.

Examples: **ethene** (ethylene, C₂H₄, simplest alkene); **propene** (propylene); the **butene** isomers (four carbons allow structural and geometric isomerism).

**Nomenclature:** replace "-ane" with "-ene"; the double-bond position is the smaller of the two participating carbon numbers. C₂H₄ ethene; C₃H₆ propene; CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃ 1-butene; CH₃CH=CHCH₃ 2-butene.

**Structural isomers:** 1-butene vs 2-butene differ in atom arrangement.

**Geometric isomers:** the same atoms are attached in the same order, but the geometries differ. Carbon atoms cannot rotate around a double bond (it is rigid), giving two isomers of 2-butene — the **cis-isomer** (both methyl groups on the same side) and the **trans-isomer** (on opposite sides). Different geometries produce different physical properties (e.g., boiling points), enabling separation.

**Reactivity:** alkenes are much more reactive than alkanes because C=C is a reactive functional group and a π bond, being weaker, is disrupted much more easily than a σ bond. The characteristic reaction is the **addition reaction**, in which the π bond is broken and replaced by two σ bonds; carbon hybridization changes from sp² to sp³. Halogens add (rather than substitute):

C₂H₄ + Cl₂ → ClCH₂CH₂Cl  (ethene + chlorine → 1,2-dichloroethane)

**Polymerization:** ethylene polymerizes (with transition-metal catalysts) to **polyethylene**, long chains of –CH₂– units, a commodity packaging plastic. **Polymers** are large molecules made of repeating units called **monomers** — natural (starch from sugars, proteins from amino acids) or synthetic (polyethylene, PVC, polystyrene). Adding functional groups changes properties significantly.

**Plastic recycling** recovers waste plastics and reprocesses them. Plastics are synthetic organic solids that can be molded — typically high-molecular-mass organic polymers. Common monomers (ethylene, propylene, vinyl chloride, styrene, ethylene terephthalate) come from petrochemicals and are not very biodegradable. Recycling codes 1–7: 1 PETE (soda bottles), 2 HDPE (milk bottles), 3 V/PVC (wrap), 4 LDPE (shopping bags), 5 PP (margarine tubs), 6 PS (foam cups), 7 Other (e.g., melamine).

### Alkynes (triple bonds)

Hydrocarbon molecules with one or more **triple bonds** are **alkynes**. The C≡C triple bond is one σ bond plus two π bonds. The carbons are **sp hybridized**, with **180°** bond angles, giving a linear, rod-like shape.

Example: **ethyne** (acetylene, C₂H₂), the simplest alkyne — linear, H–C≡C–H.

**Nomenclature:** like alkenes but with "-yne" for the triple bond. CH₃CH₂C≡CH is 1-butyne.

**Reactivity:** chemically similar to alkenes, but with two π bonds in the C≡C functional group, alkynes typically react even more readily, reacting with twice as much reagent in addition reactions:

HC≡CH + 2 Br₂ → Br₂CHCHBr₂  (ethyne + 2 bromine → 1,1,2,2-tetrabromoethane)

Alkynes burn readily; an acetylene torch exploits acetylene's high heat of combustion.

### Aromatic Hydrocarbons

**Benzene**, C₆H₆, is the simplest member of the **aromatic hydrocarbons** — ring compounds whose bonding is described by **resonance** (valence bond theory) or **delocalization** (molecular orbital theory).

**Structure:** benzene is a hexagonal ring of **sp²-hybridized carbon atoms**, each with an unhybridized p orbital perpendicular to the ring plane. Three sp² electrons per carbon (plus each H's electron) form σ bonds; the fourth electron per carbon is shared with neighbors via the unhybridized p orbitals to make the π system. Benzene does *not* show typical alkene behavior — each of the six C–C bonds is equivalent, with properties intermediate between a single and a double bond. Benzene is drawn with single bonds and a circle inside the ring to represent the delocalized π electrons.

**Derivatives:** toluene (one CH₃ group), xylene (two CH₃ groups), styrene (one CH=CH₂ group). Toluene and xylene are important solvents and raw materials; styrene makes polystyrene.

**Reactivity:** aromatic compounds more readily undergo **substitution** than addition reactions; replacing a hydrogen with another substituent leaves the delocalized double bonds intact — preserving the aromatic structure (contrast with alkenes' addition).

**Isomers of substituted benzenes:** substituents at different ring positions give **ortho**, **meta**, and **para** isomers (e.g., the three chlorotoluenes; the three dibromobenzenes).

## 20.2 Alcohols and Ethers

### Alcohols

**Alcohols** are derivatives of hydrocarbons in which an **–OH (hydroxyl) group** has replaced a hydrogen atom. The hydroxyl group is a *covalent* O–C bond, distinguishing alcohols from ionic hydroxides like NaOH and KOH (which contain OH⁻ ions).

**Ethanol** (CH₃CH₂OH, ethyl alcohol) is produced by yeast fermentation of sugars:

C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) → 2 C₂H₅OH(aq) + 2 CO₂(g)   (yeast)

Industrially, ethanol is made by acid-catalyzed addition of water to ethylene:

C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH   (H₃O⁺ catalyst)

**Polyhydric alcohols** (multiple –OH groups): 1,2-ethanediol (ethylene glycol, antifreeze); 1,2,3-propanetriol (glycerine/glycerol, a cosmetic/medicinal solvent).

**Naming:** replace the final "-e" of the hydrocarbon name with "-ol," and give the –OH carbon position by a number. Examples: 2-pentanol; 2-methyl-2-pentanol.

### Ethers

**Ethers** contain the functional group **–O–**. In the general formula R–O–R, the hydrocarbon groups may be identical or different.

**Naming — two systems:**
- **IUPAC:** name the oxygen plus the smaller carbon branch as an **alkoxy substituent**, with the remainder as the base chain.
- **Common:** name the two branches bonded to oxygen, then "ether."

Examples: CH₃–O–CH₂CH₃ = methoxyethane (IUPAC) / ethylmethyl ether (common); CH₃–O–CH(CH₃)₂ = 2-methoxypropane / isopropylmethyl ether.

**Formation from alcohols:** ethers form by elimination of water from two alcohol molecules. Treating ethanol with sulfuric acid at 140 °C:

2 C₂H₅OH → C₂H₅–O–C₂H₅ + H₂O   (H₂SO₄, Δ) → diethyl ether + water

**Applications:** diethyl ether (colorless, volatile, highly flammable; an anesthetic from 1846; now a solvent for gums, fats, waxes, resins); MTBE (tert-butyl methyl ether, C₄H₉OCH₃, a gasoline additive that raises oxygen content).

**Carbohydrate connection:** carbohydrates (general formula Cₘ(H₂O)ₙ) contain both alcohol (hydroxyl) and ether groups; monosaccharides link via ether bonds to form disaccharides and larger polymers.

## 20.3 Aldehydes, Ketones, Carboxylic Acids, and Esters

Organic molecules in this class contain a **carbonyl group** — a carbon atom connected to an oxygen atom by a double bond (C=O). The trigonal planar carbonyl carbon attaches to two other substituents, giving rise to several subfamilies (aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, esters).

### Aldehydes and Ketones

Both contain a **carbonyl group** (a functional group with a carbon–oxygen double bond).
- In an **aldehyde**, the carbonyl group is bonded to at least one hydrogen atom (group: –CHO; suffix **-al**).
- In a **ketone**, the carbonyl group is bonded to two carbon atoms (group: –C(O)– or –CO–; suffix **-one**).

**Geometry and bonding:** the carbonyl carbon is **trigonal planar, sp² hybridized**. Two sp² orbitals form σ bonds to C or H; the third sp² orbital forms a σ bond to oxygen; the unhybridized p orbital on carbon overlaps a p orbital on oxygen to form the π bond.

**Polarity and reactivity:** the C=O bond is polar (oxygen is more electronegative than carbon, pulling shared electrons toward O and away from C). Reactions typically begin with a Lewis base attacking the carbon at the positive end of the polar C=O bond, yielding an unstable intermediate that rearranges to the final product.

**Oxidation and reduction (organic sense):** oxidation occurs when a C–H bond is replaced by a C–O bond; reduction replaces a C–O bond by a C–H bond. Carbon oxidation numbers across the series: CH₄ (–4) → CH₃OH methanol (–2) → CH₂O aldehyde (0) → HCO₂H carboxylic acid (+2) → CO₂ (+4).

- **Aldehydes from primary alcohols** (–OH on a terminal carbon):
  CH₃CH₂CH₂OH → CH₃CH₂CHO
- **Ketones from secondary alcohols** (–OH on a middle carbon, bonded to two other carbons):
  CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ → CH₃COCH₃

An alcohol whose –OH carbon is bonded to no or one other carbon forms an aldehyde; one bonded to two other carbons forms a ketone. If three carbons are attached to the –OH carbon, there is no C–H bond to replace, so it is not susceptible to oxidation.

**Examples:**
- **Formaldehyde (HCHO):** colorless gas, pungent/irritating odor; sold as **formalin** (~37% by weight in water). Coagulates proteins, killing bacteria and stopping decay — used to preserve tissue specimens, embalm bodies, sterilize soil, and make Bakelite.
- **Acetone (dimethyl ketone, CH₃COCH₃):** simplest ketone; made by fermenting corn/molasses or by oxidizing 2-propanol; a colorless liquid solvent for lacquer (incl. nail polish), cellulose acetate/nitrate, acetylene, plastics, varnishes; a paint/varnish remover; used in making pharmaceuticals.

### Carboxylic Acids and Esters

Both contain a carbonyl group with a second oxygen single-bonded to the carbonyl carbon.
- In a **carboxylic acid**, that second oxygen also bonds to a hydrogen (group: –COOH or –CO₂H).
- In an **ester**, that second oxygen bonds to another carbon (group: –COO–R or –CO₂R).

**Carboxylic acids as weak acids:** the acidic hydrogen reacts with a base to form an ionic salt:

CH₃CH₂COOH + NaOH → CH₃CH₂COO⁻Na⁺ + H₂O

Carboxylic acids are **weak acids** — not 100% ionized; generally only about 1% of the molecules in water are ionized at any time, the rest undissociated.

**Preparation:** by oxidation of aldehydes, or of alcohols whose –OH is on a terminal carbon, in two steps: alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid.

**Esterification:** esters are produced by reaction of acids with alcohols. Ethyl acetate forms from acetic acid + ethanol:

CH₃COOH + HOCH₂CH₃ → CH₃CO₂CH₂CH₃ + H₂O

**Esters and odor:** the distinctive attractive odors/flavors of many flowers, perfumes, and ripe fruits are due to esters. Because esters lack hydrogen bonds between molecules, they have lower vapor pressures than the alcohols and carboxylic acids from which they derive. Example aromas: raspberry (iso-butyl formate), apple (butyl acetate), pineapple (ethyl butyrate), rum (propyl isobutyrate), peach (benzyl acetate), orange (octyl acetate), wintergreen (methyl salicylate), honey (methyl phenylacetate), strawberry (ethyl methylphenylglycidate).

**Example acids:**
- **Formic acid (HCO₂H):** simplest carboxylic acid, known since 1670; name from Latin *formicus* ("ant"); first isolated by distilling red ants; responsible for ant/wasp-sting irritation and the odor of ants.
- **Acetic acid (CH₃CO₂H):** 3–6% of vinegar. Cider vinegar comes from apple juice fermented without oxygen: yeast converts sugar → ethanol → acetic acid. Pure acetic acid has a penetrating odor and causes painful burns; an excellent solvent; essential for cellulose acetate (used in rayon).

**Natural esters — fats and oils:** among the most important natural esters are **fats** (lard, tallow, butter) and **oils** (linseed, cottonseed, olive), esters of the trihydroxyl alcohol **glycerine, C₃H₅(OH)₃**, with large carboxylic acids such as **palmitic acid CH₃(CH₂)₁₄CO₂H**, **stearic acid CH₃(CH₂)₁₆CO₂H** (both saturated, no double/triple bonds), and **oleic acid CH₃(CH₂)₇CH=CH(CH₂)₇CO₂H** (unsaturated, contains a C=C).

## 20.4 Amines and Amides

### Amines

**Amines** are molecules containing carbon–nitrogen bonds. The nitrogen atom in an amine has a **lone pair of electrons** and three bonds to other atoms (carbon or hydrogen).

**Nomenclature** uses the class-identifying suffix **-ine**: methylamine CH₃NH₂; dimethylamine (CH₃)₂NH; trimethylamine (CH₃)₃N.

**Heterocyclic amines:** in some amines nitrogen replaces a carbon in an aromatic ring. A **heterocyclic compound** contains atoms of two or more different elements in its ring. **Pyridine** is a six-membered ring with one nitrogen, alternating double bonds, and an unshared electron pair on the nitrogen.

**Basicity:** like ammonia, amines are **weak bases** because of the nitrogen lone pair; they undergo protonation:

NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺
CH₃NH₂ + H⁺ → [CH₃NH₃]⁺

The basicity of the nitrogen atom plays an important role in much of an amine's chemistry.

**Biological significance:** amine groups appear in natural and synthetic dyes, polymers, vitamins, and medications (penicillin, codeine), and in amino acids, hormones, neurotransmitters, and DNA.

**Alkaloids** are cyclic molecules with amine functional groups found in plants. They react with dilute acid to form ammonium salts:

R₃N + H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻ → [R₃NH⁺]Cl⁻ + H₂O

and the free compound is recovered with a base:

[R₃NH⁺]Cl⁻ + OH⁻ → R₃N + H₂O + Cl⁻

Examples: nicotine, morphine, codeine, heroin. Small structural changes alter potency — morphine has two hydroxyl groups; converting one to a methyl ether yields codeine; converting both to acetic acid esters produces heroin.

### Amides

**Amides** are molecules with a nitrogen atom connected to the carbon atom of a **carbonyl group**. The amide group has a C=O double bond and an N bonded to the carbonyl carbon, the nitrogen carrying hydrogen atoms or organic groups.

**Nomenclature** uses the class-specific suffix **-amide**: acetamide CH₃CONH₂; hexanamide CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂CONH₂.

**Formation — amidation:** amides form when carboxylic acids react with amines or ammonia; a water molecule is eliminated and the amide is built from the remaining pieces:

CH₃CH₂COOH + CH₃NH₂ → CH₃CH₂CONHCH₃ + H₂O

**Biological importance:** the amine + carboxylic acid → amide reaction is biologically central — it is how **amino acids** (molecules containing both an amine and a carboxylic acid substituent) link in a polymer to form proteins.

**Peptide bonds:** amino acids connect via **peptide bonds** — the covalent bond formed when one amino acid's carboxylic acid group reacts with another's amine group, releasing water. The bond between the carbonyl carbon and amine nitrogen is the peptide link. Because unreacted groups remain, additional peptide bonds extend the chain. A chain of connected amino acids is a **polypeptide**; proteins contain at least one long polypeptide chain.

**Kevlar:** a synthetic polymer from 1,4-phenylene-diamine and terephthaloyl chloride. Its strength comes from hydrogen bonds between polymer chains (carbonyl oxygen partially negative, N–H hydrogen partially positive) plus **aromatic stacking** (interaction between unhybridized p orbitals in the six-membered rings). It has a high tensile-strength-to-weight ratio (about 5× stronger than an equal weight of steel) and is used in body armor, helmets, and protective gear.
