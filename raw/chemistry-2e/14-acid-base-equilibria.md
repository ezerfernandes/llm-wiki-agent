# Chemistry 2e — Chapter 14: Acid-Base Equilibria

> Source: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/14-introduction

Aqueous solutions play a vital role in all living systems, with the characteristic ions H⁺ and OH⁻ being central to chemistry occurring in nature and everyday life. Acid-base chemistry involves the transfer of hydrogen ions from donors (acids) to acceptors (bases). These H⁺ transfer reactions are reversible, and the equilibria established by acid-base systems are essential aspects of phenomena ranging from sinkhole formation to oxygen transport in the human body. This chapter explores acid-base chemistry with an emphasis on the equilibrium aspects of this reaction class.

## 14.1 Brønsted-Lowry Acids and Bases

### Historical Development

Early understanding of acids and bases evolved significantly. Robert Boyle (1680) identified traits of acid solutions including dissolving substances and changing dye colors. By the eighteenth century, acids were recognized as sour-tasting substances that react with limestone to liberate CO₂ and interact with alkalis to form neutral substances. Humphry Davy (1815) demonstrated hydrogen as the essential constituent of acids. Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac concluded that acids and bases could only be defined in terms of each other. Svante Arrhenius (1884) defined an acid as a compound yielding hydrogen cations (hydronium ions) in water and a base as yielding hydroxide anions.

### Brønsted-Lowry Definition

Johannes Brønsted and Thomas Lowry (1923) proposed a more general acid-base description based on hydrogen ion transfer. A compound that donates a proton to another compound is called a **Brønsted-Lowry acid**, and a compound that accepts a proton is called a **Brønsted-Lowry base**. An acid-base reaction is the transfer of a proton from a donor (acid) to an acceptor (base).

Note: Hydrogen ions are often referred to as protons, since this subatomic particle is the only component of cations derived from ¹H.

### Conjugate Acid-Base Pairs

When an acid donates H⁺, the species that remains is called the **conjugate base** of the acid because it reacts as a proton acceptor in the reverse reaction. Likewise, when a base accepts H⁺, it is converted to its **conjugate acid**.

**Water and ammonia:** In the forward direction, water acts as an acid donating a proton to ammonia, becoming hydroxide ion (OH⁻), the conjugate base of water. Ammonia acts as a base, becoming ammonium ion (NH₄⁺), the conjugate acid of ammonia. In the reverse direction, hydroxide acts as a base accepting a proton from ammonium ion, which acts as an acid.

**Hydrogen fluoride and water:**
```
HF(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + F⁻(aq)
```
HF = acid; H₂O = base; H₃O⁺ = (conjugate) acid; F⁻ = (conjugate) base.

### Acid Ionization

The reaction between a Brønsted-Lowry acid and water is called **acid ionization**. When hydrogen fluoride dissolves in water and ionizes, protons transfer from HF molecules to water molecules, yielding hydronium ions and fluoride ions.

### Base Ionization

**Base ionization** occurs when a species accepts protons from water molecules. Example with pyridine:
```
H₂O(l) + C₅NH₅(aq) ⇌ C₅NH₆⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
K = [C₅NH₆⁺][OH⁻] / [C₅NH₅]
```
H₂O = acid; C₅NH₅ = base; C₅NH₆⁺ = conjugate acid; OH⁻ = conjugate base.

### Amphiprotic and Amphoteric Species

Species capable of either donating or accepting protons are called **amphiprotic**, or more generally, **amphoteric** (a term that may be used for acids and bases per definitions other than the Brønsted-Lowry one).

**Bicarbonate ion** as an acid (with water as a base):
```
HCO₃⁻(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ CO₃²⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq)
```
Bicarbonate as a base (with water as an acid):
```
HCO₃⁻(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂CO₃(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
```
When bicarbonate is added to water, both equilibria are established simultaneously and the composition of the resulting solution is determined through equilibrium calculations.

### Autoionization of Water

In the liquid state, molecules of an amphiprotic substance can react with one another. For water:
```
H₂O(l) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
```
The process in which like molecules react to yield ions is called **autoionization**. At 25 °C, approximately two out of every billion water molecules are ionized.

### Ion-Product Constant for Water (Kw)

The extent of water autoionization is reflected in the ion-product constant:
```
Kw = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻]
```
At 25 °C: **Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴**

The slight ionization of pure water is reflected in the small value of the equilibrium constant. The process is endothermic, so the extent of ionization and resulting ion concentrations increase with temperature. At 100 °C, Kw ≈ 5.6 × 10⁻¹³ (roughly 50× larger than at 25 °C).

### Worked Examples (takeaways)

- **Ex 14.1 — Pure water:** [H₃O⁺] = [OH⁻] = x; Kw = x² = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ → x = √(1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M. In pure water at 25 °C the two ions are equal at 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M. (At 80 °C, where Kw = 2.4 × 10⁻¹³, [H₃O⁺] = [OH⁻] = 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ M.)
- **Ex 14.2 — Inverse relation:** [OH⁻] = Kw / [H₃O⁺]. For [H₃O⁺] = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ M, [OH⁻] = (1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴)/(2.0 × 10⁻⁶) = 5.0 × 10⁻⁹ M. An acidic solution has higher [H₃O⁺] and proportionally lower [OH⁻]; explained by Le Châtelier's principle as a left shift in the autoionization equilibrium.
- **Ex 14.3 — Amphoteric behavior of HSO₃⁻:** (a) as acid: HSO₃⁻ + OH⁻ ⇌ SO₃²⁻ + H₂O; (b) as base: HSO₃⁻ + HI ⇌ H₂SO₃ + I⁻. Amphoteric species react as acid or base depending on the other reactant.

## 14.2 pH and pOH

### Solution Classification

A solution is **neutral** if it has equal concentrations of hydronium and hydroxide ions; **acidic** if [H₃O⁺] > [OH⁻]; **basic** if [H₃O⁺] < [OH⁻].

### The p-Function and pH/pOH Scales

A logarithmic p-function expresses quantities spanning many orders of magnitude: **pX = −log X** (base-10 log).

```
pH  = −log[H₃O⁺]      ⇒   [H₃O⁺] = 10^(−pH)
pOH = −log[OH⁻]       ⇒   [OH⁻]  = 10^(−pOH)
```

### Relationship Between pH and pOH

From Kw = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻], taking the negative log of both sides:
```
−log Kw = −log[H₃O⁺] + (−log[OH⁻])
pKw = pH + pOH
```
At 25 °C, Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴, so **pH + pOH = pKw = 14.00**.

### Values at 25 °C

Pure/neutral water: [H₃O⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M → pH = 7.00 and pOH = 7.00.

| Classification | Relative Ion Concentrations | pH at 25 °C |
|---|---|---|
| Acidic | [H₃O⁺] > [OH⁻] | pH < 7 |
| Neutral | [H₃O⁺] = [OH⁻] | pH = 7 |
| Basic | [H₃O⁺] < [OH⁻] | pH > 7 |

### Temperature Dependence

Because Kw is temperature-dependent, these pH correlations change at other temperatures. At 80 °C, pure water has [H₃O⁺] = 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ M → pH = pOH = 6.31; acidic means pH < 6.31, basic means pH > 6.31. Unless noted, pH values refer to 25 °C.

### pH Measurement

Acidity is usually assessed experimentally by measuring pH; pOH is typically calculated from pH rather than measured directly.
- **pH meters**: research-grade resolution 0.001 pH units, accuracy ±0.002; portable meters resolution 0.01, accuracy ±0.2.
- **Colored indicators**: universal indicator dyes produce different colors at different pH values; pH paper covers roughly pH 1–12.

## 14.3 Relative Strengths of Acids and Bases

The relative strength of an acid or base is the extent to which it ionizes when dissolved in water. An acid or base is **strong** if ionization is essentially complete; it is **weak** if relatively little ionization occurs.

### Acid Ionization Constant (Ka)

For `HA(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + A⁻(aq)`:
```
Ka = [H₃O⁺][A⁻] / [HA]
```
The larger the Ka, the greater [H₃O⁺] and [A⁻] relative to nonionized HA, and the stronger the acid. Strong acids have Ka ≈ ∞; weak acids have measurable Ka.

### Percent Ionization

For weak acids:
```
% ionization = ([H₃O⁺]eq / [HA]₀) × 100
```
where [H₃O⁺]eq = [A⁻]. Unlike Ka, percent ionization varies with initial concentration, typically decreasing as concentration increases.

### Base Ionization Constant (Kb)

For `B(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ HB⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)`:
```
Kb = [HB⁺][OH⁻] / [B]
```
Stronger bases have larger Kb. For bases, % ionization = ([OH⁻]eq / [B]₀) × 100.

### Relative Strengths of Conjugate Acid-Base Pairs

For the conjugate pair HA / A⁻:
```
Acid:  HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻     Ka = [H₃O⁺][A⁻]/[HA]
Base:  A⁻ + H₂O ⇌ OH⁻ + HA      Kb = [HA][OH⁻]/[A⁻]
```
Adding these gives the water autoionization equation, so:
```
Ka × Kb = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = Kw
Ka = Kw / Kb     or     Kb = Kw / Ka
```
**Key relationship:** the stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base (and vice versa). The product Ka × Kb for any conjugate pair equals Kw (1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C).

### Leveling Effect

In aqueous solution the strongest acid that can exist is H₃O⁺(aq). Any acid stronger than H₃O⁺ reacts completely with water to generate hydronium ions; this limit on acid strength is the **leveling effect**. Distinguishing among "strong" acids requires a less basic solvent than water. (Analogously, OH⁻ is the strongest base that can exist in water.)

### Equilibrium Calculations for Weak Acid-Base Systems

1. **From equilibrium concentrations:** substitute known equilibrium values into Ka or Kb.
2. **From pH:** convert pH to [H₃O⁺] = 10^(−pH); use an ICE table to find Ka or Kb.
3. **To find equilibrium concentrations:** set up an ICE table and solve for x; when x ≪ initial concentration, simplify to x²/[HA]₀ = Ka.
4. **When the assumption fails:** if x is > 5% of the initial concentration, solve the full quadratic rather than neglecting x.

### Molecular Structure and Acid-Base Strength

**Binary acids/bases:**
- *Down a group:* acid strength increases as H–A bond strength decreases. Group 17: HF < HCl < HBr < HI. Group 16: H₂O < H₂S < H₂Se < H₂Te.
- *Across a period:* acid strength increases with electronegativity of the nonmetal. Period 2: CH₄ < NH₃ < H₂O < HF; Period 3: SiH₄ < PH₃ < H₂S < HCl.

**Ternary acids (OₘE(OH)ₙ):**
- Metallic central atom (low electronegativity): E–O bond ionic, releases OH⁻ → behaves as a **base** (Ca(OH)₂, KOH).
- Nonmetallic central atom (high electronegativity): O–H bond weakened, releases H⁺ → behaves as an **acid** (oxyacid).
- Increasing oxidation number of E increases oxyacid acidity (stronger pull on O electrons, weaker O–H bond): H₂SO₃ (S +4) < H₂SO₄ (S +6); HNO₂ (N +3) < HNO₃ (N +5).

**Amphoteric compounds:** hydroxy compounds of elements near the metal-nonmetal diagonal act as acids with strong bases and as bases with strong acids. Aluminum hydroxide:
```
With strong base: Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃(aq) + OH⁻(aq) ⇌ H₂O(l) + [Al(H₂O)₂(OH)₄]⁻(aq)
With strong acid: 3H₃O⁺(aq) + Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃(aq) ⇌ [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺(aq) + 3H₂O(l)
```

## 14.4 Hydrolysis of Salts

Aqueous salt solutions may be acidic, basic, or neutral, depending on the relative acid-base strengths of the salt's constituent ions.

### Salts with Acidic Ions (cation hydrolysis)

Salts of a weak-base cation and inert anion give acidic solutions. **Ammonium chloride:**
```
NH₄Cl(s) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)
NH₄⁺(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + NH₃(aq)     Ka = Kw / Kb
Cl⁻(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ HCl(aq) + OH⁻(aq)        Kb = Kw / Ka ≈ 0 (negligible)
```
Cl⁻ is the conjugate base of a strong acid, so it does not hydrolyze appreciably; the solution is acidic.

*Worked (anilinium chloride, 0.233 M [C₆H₅NH₃]Cl):* Ka = Kw/Kb = (1.0×10⁻¹⁴)/(4.3×10⁻¹⁰) = 2.3×10⁻⁵; solving 2.3×10⁻⁵ = x²/(0.233−x) with x ≪ 0.233 gives x = 0.0023 M, pH = 2.64. C₆H₅NH₃⁺ is a stronger acid than NH₄⁺.

### Salts with Basic Ions (anion hydrolysis)

Salts of an inert cation and a weak-acid anion give basic solutions. **Sodium acetate:**
```
NaCH₃CO₂(s) ⇌ Na⁺(aq) + CH₃CO₂⁻(aq)
CH₃CO₂⁻(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ CH₃CO₂H(aq) + OH⁻(aq)     Kb = Kw / Ka
```
Na⁺ does not ionize appreciably. Because acetic acid is weak, acetate is a weak base (Kb = (1.0×10⁻¹⁴)/(1.8×10⁻⁵) = 5.6×10⁻¹⁰); the solution is basic. (Worked: pH of 0.083 M NaCN = 11.11.)

### Salts with Acidic and Basic Ions

When both ions react, the pH depends on the relative strengths: compare Ka and Kb of the ions.
- If Ka > Kb → acidic; if Kb > Ka → basic.

Worked classifications:
- **KBr** — K⁺ inert; Br⁻ conjugate base of strong acid → **neutral**.
- **NaHCO₃** — HCO₃⁻ amphiprotic: Ka = 4.7×10⁻¹¹, Kb = (1.0×10⁻¹⁴)/(4.3×10⁻⁷) = 2.3×10⁻⁸; Kb ≫ Ka → **basic**.
- **Na₂HPO₄** — HPO₄²⁻ amphiprotic: Ka = 4.2×10⁻¹³, Kb = (1.0×10⁻¹⁴)/(6.2×10⁻⁸) = 1.6×10⁻⁷; Kb ≫ Ka → **basic**.
- **NH₄F** — NH₄⁺ acidic (Ka = 5.6×10⁻¹⁰), F⁻ basic (Kb = 1.6×10⁻¹¹); Ka > Kb → **acidic**.

### Ionization of Hydrated Metal Ions

Most metal ions (except group 1 and some group 2) act as weak acids via hydrolysis of coordinated water:
```
Al(NO₃)₃(s) + 6H₂O(l) ⇌ [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺(aq) + 3NO₃⁻(aq)
```
Bonded water O–H bonds are more polar than in free water, making them prone to donating H⁺. Sequential ionization (a polyprotic acid):
```
[Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + [Al(H₂O)₅(OH)]²⁺     Ka = 1.4×10⁻⁵
[Al(H₂O)₅(OH)]²⁺ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + [Al(H₂O)₄(OH)₂]⁺
[Al(H₂O)₄(OH)₂]⁺ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃
```
Other metal ions: Fe(III) [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ pKa = 2.74; Cu(II) [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ pKa ≈ 6.3; Zn(II) [Zn(H₂O)₄]²⁺ pKa = 9.6. Acid strength of these complex ions increases with increasing charge and decreasing size of the metal ion.

*Worked (0.10 M Al(NO₃)₃):* 1.4×10⁻⁵ = x²/(0.10−x), x ≪ 0.10 → x = [H₃O⁺] = 1.2×10⁻³ M, pH = 2.92 (acidic).

## 14.5 Polyprotic Acids

### Classification by Ionizable Protons

- **Monoprotic acids**: one ionizable H per molecule (HCl, HNO₃, HCN). Acetic acid CH₃CO₂H is monoprotic despite four H atoms — only the carboxyl (COOH) H reacts.
- **Diprotic acids**: two ionizable H; ionization occurs in two steps. The first ionization always occurs to a greater extent than the second.
- **Triprotic acids**: three ionizable H (e.g., phosphoric acid).

### Stepwise Ionization

Successive equilibrium constants decrease significantly.

**Sulfuric acid H₂SO₄ (strong diprotic):**
```
H₂SO₄ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + HSO₄⁻     Ka1 > 10² (complete)
HSO₄⁻ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + SO₄²⁻      Ka2 = 1.2×10⁻²
```

**Carbonic acid H₂CO₃ (weak diprotic):**
```
H₂CO₃ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + HCO₃⁻      Ka(H₂CO₃) = 4.3×10⁻⁷
HCO₃⁻ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + CO₃²⁻      Ka(HCO₃⁻) = 4.7×10⁻¹¹
```
The first constant exceeds the second by ~10⁴, making H₂CO₃ the dominant source of H₃O⁺.

**Phosphoric acid H₃PO₄ (triprotic):**
```
H₃PO₄ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + H₂PO₄⁻     Ka1 = 7.5×10⁻³
H₂PO₄⁻ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + HPO₄²⁻    Ka2 = 6.2×10⁻⁸
HPO₄²⁻ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + PO₄³⁻     Ka3 = 4.2×10⁻¹³
```

### Key Pattern

Successive ionization constants often differ by a factor of about 10⁵ to 10⁶. This large difference permits treating the first ionization separately (and computing [H₃O⁺] from it alone) when Ka1 exceeds Ka2 by at least a factor of ~20.

*Worked (0.033 M H₂CO₃):* first ionization gives [H₃O⁺] = [HCO₃⁻] = 1.2×10⁻⁴ M; the second ionization gives [CO₃²⁻] = 4.7×10⁻¹¹ M (≈ Ka2, essentially independent of the first step).

### Polyprotic Bases

Polyprotic bases accept multiple protons. Carbonate (CO₃²⁻) is a diprotic base:
```
H₂O + CO₃²⁻ ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻     Kb1 = 2.1×10⁻⁴
H₂O + HCO₃⁻ ⇌ H₂CO₃ + OH⁻     Kb2 = 2.3×10⁻⁸
```
Ionization constants decrease with each step, and calculations follow the same approaches as polyprotic acids.

## 14.6 Buffers

### Definition and Composition

A solution containing appreciable amounts of a weak conjugate acid-base pair is called a **buffer solution** (buffer). Buffers resist pH changes when small amounts of strong acid or strong base are added. Two types:
- **Weak-acid buffer**: weak acid + its salt (e.g., acetic acid + sodium acetate).
- **Weak-base buffer**: weak base + its salt (e.g., ammonia + ammonium chloride).

### How Buffers Work

In an acetic acid / acetate buffer:
```
CH₃CO₂H(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + CH₃CO₂⁻(aq)
```
Adding strong base shifts this right; adding strong acid shifts it left. The buffering action results from the added strong acid/base being converted into the weak acid/base of the conjugate pair.

### Buffer Capacity

**Buffer capacity** is the amount of acid or base that can be added before the pH changes significantly (usually by one unit). It depends on the concentrations of both the weak acid and its conjugate base — a 1.0 M acetic acid/acetate buffer has greater capacity than a 0.10 M one.

Selection rules:
1. A good buffer has roughly equal concentrations of both components; it loses usefulness when one component is less than ~10% of the other.
2. Weak acid + salt works best for pH < 7; weak base + salt works best for pH > 7.

### The Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation

Starting from Ka = [H₃O⁺][A⁻]/[HA] and solving for [H₃O⁺]:
```
[H₃O⁺] = Ka × [HA]/[A⁻]
−log[H₃O⁺] = −log Ka − log([HA]/[A⁻])
```
yields the **Henderson-Hasselbalch equation**:
```
pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA])
```
where pKa = −log Ka. It relates pH, the acid ionization constant, and the concentrations of the conjugate pair. The "x is small" approximation must remain valid to use it. (At equal concentrations, pH = pKa.)

### Buffers in Biology: Blood

Blood maintains pH near 7.4 via the carbonic acid-bicarbonate system:
```
CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂CO₃(aq) ⇌ HCO₃⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq)
Added H₃O⁺: H₃O⁺ + HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃ + H₂O
Added OH⁻:  OH⁻ + H₂CO₃ → HCO₃⁻ + H₂O
```
In blood, [H₂CO₃] ≈ 0.0012 M and [HCO₃⁻] ≈ 0.024 M (higher base concentration buffers acidic metabolic by-products). Normal blood pH variations are < 0.1; changes of 0.4 or greater are likely fatal. Breathing also regulates pH: faster respiration removes CO₂ (raising pH), slower respiration retains CO₂ (lowering pH).

## 14.7 Acid-Base Titrations

### Titration Curves

A **titration curve** plots a solution property versus the amount of added titrant. For acid-base titrations, pH is plotted because it varies predictably with composition; the curve enables monitoring progress and detecting the endpoint.

### Strong Acid / Strong Base

Four stages (25.00 mL of 0.100 M HCl titrated with 0.100 M NaOH):
- **Initial (V = 0):** pH set by the acid. 0.100 M HCl → pH = −log(0.100) = 1.000.
- **Pre-equivalence:** mixture of unreacted acid and product; pH rises gradually. At 12.50 mL added, [H₃O⁺] = 0.0333 M, pH = 1.52.
- **Equivalence point (V = 25 mL):** drastic rise in pH. Solution contains only NaCl and water; neither ion hydrolyzes, so **pH = 7.00 (neutral)**.
- **Post-equivalence:** pH set by excess strong base. At 37.50 mL, [OH⁻] = 0.0200 M → pOH = 1.70 → pH = 12.30.

### Weak Acid / Strong Base

(25.00 mL of 0.100 M CH₃CO₂H titrated with 0.100 M NaOH):
- **Initial:** higher pH than an equally concentrated strong acid (2.87 for acetic acid vs 1.00 for HCl).
- **Half-equivalence point (V = 12.50 mL):** equal moles of weak acid and conjugate base; pH = pKa + log(1) = pKa = −log(1.8×10⁻⁵) = 4.74. **pH = pKa at the half-equivalence point.**
- **Equivalence point (V = 25 mL):** solution contains acetate (0.0500 M) which hydrolyzes: CH₃CO₂⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃CO₂H + OH⁻; Kb = Kw/Ka = (1.0×10⁻¹⁴)/(1.8×10⁻⁵) = 5.6×10⁻¹⁰; [OH⁻] = 5.3×10⁻⁶, pOH = 5.28, **pH = 8.72 (basic, not neutral)**. The equivalence-point pH is significantly greater than 7.
- **Post-equivalence:** pH set by excess strong base.

### Acid-Base Indicators

Indicators are weak organic acids (or bases). For methyl orange (HIn):
```
HIn(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + In⁻(aq)     Ka = 4.0×10⁻⁴
```
HIn form = red; In⁻ form = yellow. The perceived color is set by the ratio [In⁻]/[HIn], described by pH = pKa + log([In⁻]/[HIn]):
- pH > pKa → excess In⁻ (yellow); pH < pKa → excess HIn (red); pH ≈ pKa → mixture (orange).

**Color change interval (pH interval):** the range of pH over which a color change is observed, approximately **pKa ± 1** for most indicators.

**Indicator selection:** the color-change interval must bracket the steep pH rise around the equivalence point.
- Strong acid / strong base: methyl orange, litmus, or phenolphthalein all give reasonably sharp, accurate end points.
- Weak acid / strong base: only phenolphthalein is suitable — its interval brackets the abrupt pH change near the (basic) equivalence point, whereas methyl orange changes well before 25 mL and litmus ends just before equivalence.
