## Chapter Introduction

We encounter transition metals daily in numerous applications. Iron appears in spiral notebooks, kitchenware, vehicles, structures, and blood hemoglobin. Titanium finds use in lightweight, durable products including bicycle frames, prosthetics, and accessories. Chromium serves as a protective coating on plumbing and automotive components.

Beyond elemental forms, transition metal compounds have extensive applications. Silver nitrate creates mirrors, zirconium silicate provides automotive brake friction, and platinum-based compounds like cisplatin fight cancer.

Transition metals display remarkable property diversity due to their intricate valence electron configurations. Unlike main group metals with single typical oxidation states, transition metals commonly exhibit multiple stable oxidation states. Furthermore, electron transitions in these elements can absorb visible-spectrum photons, producing colored compounds. These characteristics make transition metal chemistry particularly rich and complex.

## 19.1 Occurrence, Preparation, and Properties of Transition Metals and Their Compounds

> Source: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/19-1-occurrence-preparation-and-properties-of-transition-metals-and-their-compounds

### Definition of Transition Metals

Transition metals are "those elements that have (or readily form) partially filled d orbitals." The d-block elements in groups 3–11 are transition elements. The f-block elements (lanthanides and actinides) also meet this criterion because the d orbital is partially occupied before the f orbitals fill. Group 12 elements are technically not transition metals but are commonly included in discussions due to shared chemical properties.

### Classification of Transition Series

The d-block elements divide into:
- **First transition series**: Sc through Cu
- **Second transition series**: Y through Ag
- **Third transition series**: La and Hf through Au
- **Fourth transition series**: Ac and Rf through Rg

The f-block contains:
- **Lanthanide series**: Ce through Lu (also called lanthanoid series)
- **Actinide series**: Th through Lr (also called actinoid series)

### Electron Configuration and Valence Electrons

For transition and inner transition metals, "it is necessary to remove the s electrons before the d or f electrons" when determining ion configurations.

**Example 19.1 — Valence Electrons in Transition Metals.** For ions, the s-valence electrons are lost before d or f electrons:
- **Ce³⁺**: [Xe]4f¹ (lanthanide series)
- **Pb²⁺**: [Xe]6s²5d¹⁰4f¹⁴ (main group element)
- **Ti²⁺**: [Ar]3d² (first transition series)
- **Am³⁺**: [Rn]5f⁶ (actinide)
- **Pd²⁺**: [Kr]4d⁸ (second transition series)

A first transition series ion with no d electrons: V⁵⁺, Sc³⁺, Ti⁴⁺, Cr⁶⁺, or Mn⁷⁺.

### Properties of Transition Elements

**General characteristics.** Transition elements are "almost all hard, high-melting solids that conduct heat and electricity well. They readily form alloys and lose electrons to form stable cations." They form "a wide variety of stable coordination compounds, in which the central metal atom or ion acts as a Lewis acid."

**Reactivity and reduction potentials.** Transition metals demonstrate "a wide range of chemical behaviors." Some are strong reducing agents; others have very low reactivity. For example:
- Lanthanides all form stable 3+ aqueous cations.
- Platinum and gold have much higher reduction potentials and resist oxidation.
- Lighter d-block ions (Cr³⁺, Fe³⁺, Co²⁺) form colorful hydrated ions stable in water.
- Heavier d-block ions (Mo³⁺, Ru³⁺, Ir²⁺) are unstable and react readily with oxygen.

**Platinum metals** (Ru, Os, Rh, Ir, Pd, Pt): Form simple cations with difficulty in water and do not form stable oxyanions.

**Chemical reactions.** Both d- and f-block elements react with nonmetals to form binary compounds; heating is often required. Key reactions:
- With halogens: Form halides with oxidation states ranging from 1+ to 6+.
- With oxygen: React with all transition elements except Pd, Pt, Ag, and Au (whose oxides decompose upon heating).
- With aqueous acids: The f-block elements, group 3 elements, and first transition series elements (except Cu) form hydrogen gas and salt solutions.

**Oxidation state trends.** "For the elements scandium through manganese (the first half of the first transition series), the highest oxidation state corresponds to the loss of all of the electrons in both the s and d orbitals of their valence shells." Example: Ti⁴⁺ forms when titanium loses its two 3d and two 4s electrons. Iron forms oxidation states from 2+ to 6+, with Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ most common.

Elements of the second and third transition series "generally are more stable in higher oxidation states than are the elements of the first series." Larger atomic radii in lower periods make electron removal easier. Example: Mo and W favor the 6+ oxidation state, while Cr favors 3+.

**Example 19.2 — Activity of Transition Metals.** To determine the strongest oxidizing agent among dichromate (Cr(VI)), permanganate (Mn(VII)), and titanium dioxide (Ti(IV)), examine reduction potentials:
- Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O   E° = +1.33 V
- MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O   E° = +1.51 V
- TiO₂ + 4H⁺ + 2e⁻ → Ti²⁺ + 2H₂O   E° = −0.50 V

Larger reduction potential indicates easier reduction. Permanganate is the strongest oxidizer, followed by dichromate, then titanium dioxide. (Check Your Learning: Co(s) + 2HCl → H₂ + CoCl₂(aq); Pt(s) shows no reaction with H⁺.)

### Preparation of Transition Elements

**General extraction — three principal steps:**
1. **Preliminary treatment**: Crushing/grinding ore, concentrating metal-bearing components, and chemically converting them into more easily reducible compounds.
2. **Smelting**: Extracting the metal in molten state, including reduction and removal of impurities via slag formation.
3. **Refining**: Final purification by distillation (low boiling metals like Zn, Hg), gravity separation (low melting metals like Sn), or electrolysis.

**Isolation of Iron.** Iron occurs "almost exclusively in oxidized forms, such as rust (Fe₂O₃)." Process: (1) roast ore (heating in air) to remove water, decompose carbonates into oxides, and convert sulfides into oxides; (2) reduce in a blast furnace (80–100 ft high, 25 ft diameter) using roasted ore, coke, and limestone fed continuously at the top.

Blast furnace reactions (top to bottom):
- 230 °C (75 ft): 3Fe₂O₃ + CO → 2Fe₃O₄ + CO₂
- 410 °C (65 ft): Fe₃O₄ + CO → 3FeO + CO₂
- 525 °C (55 ft): FeO + CO → Fe + CO₂
- 945 °C (35 ft): C + CO₂ → 2CO ; CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
- 1125 °C (25 ft): C + CO₂ → 2CO ; CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃
- 1300 °C (15 ft): C + O₂ → CO₂
- 1510 °C (5 ft): molten metal and slag collection zone

Near the bottom, preheated air oxidizes coke: CO₂(g) + C(s) → 2CO(g). Carbon monoxide is the reducing agent in upper regions. In the middle region, limestone decomposes and combines with silica: CaO(s) + SiO₂(s) → CaSiO₃(l), forming slag (mostly calcium silicate). Less-dense slag floats on the molten iron, protecting it from oxidation; both are withdrawn several times daily.

**Steel production.** Iron is refined and converted to steel by removing impurities and adding substances such as Mn, Cr, Ni, W, Mo, and V to produce alloys with specific properties. Most steels contain 0.04%–2.5% carbon; excess carbon makes iron brittle.

**Isolation of Copper.** Most important ores contain copper sulfides (covellite, CuS), though copper oxides (tenorite, CuO) and hydroxycarbonates [malachite, Cu₂(OH)₂CO₃] occur. Process: (1) roast concentrated sulfide ore to remove part of sulfur as SO₂; (2) mix remaining material (Cu₂S, FeS, FeO, SiO₂) with limestone flux and heat; molten slag forms via Lewis acid–base reactions:
- CaCO₃(s) + SiO₂(s) → CaSiO₃(l) + CO₂(g)
- FeO(s) + SiO₂(s) → FeSiO₃(l)

Silicon dioxide acts as Lewis acid accepting electron pairs from oxide ions (Lewis bases). (4) Reduce remaining Cu₂S by blowing air through the molten material:
- 2Cu₂S(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2Cu₂O(l) + 2SO₂(g)
- 2Cu₂O(l) + Cu₂S(l) → 6Cu(l) + SO₂(g)

**Blister copper** (containing air blisters) is cast into large plates used as anodes in electrolytic refining.

**Isolation of Silver.** Silver occurs in nuggets or veins. Hydrometallurgy converts it to soluble ions, then extracts and reduces to precipitate pure metal. In the presence of air, alkali metal cyanides form soluble dicyanoargentate(I) ions, [Ag(CN)₂]⁻:
- 4Ag(s) + 8CN⁻(aq) + O₂(g) + 2H₂O(l) → 4[Ag(CN)₂]⁻(aq) + 4OH⁻(aq)
- 2Ag₂S(s) + 8CN⁻(aq) + O₂(g) + 2H₂O(l) → 4[Ag(CN)₂]⁻(aq) + 2S(s) + 4OH⁻(aq)
- AgCl(s) + 2CN⁻(aq) → [Ag(CN)₂]⁻(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)

Silver precipitates by adding Zn or Fe²⁺ as reducing agent:
- 2[Ag(CN)₂]⁻(aq) + Zn(s) → 2Ag(s) + [Zn(CN)₄]²⁻(aq)

**Example 19.3 — Refining Redox.** In 4Ag + 8CN⁻ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4[Ag(CN)₂]⁻ + 4OH⁻, oxygen is necessary to balance charge and atoms: silver oxidizes 0 → 1+, and oxygen (a good oxidizing agent) is reduced 0 → 2−. (Check Your Learning: carbon is needed in the blast furnace because it converts to CO, the reducing agent that reduces Fe(III) to Fe(0).)

### Transition Metal Compounds

Bonding ranges from ionic to covalent. Lower oxidation states form ionic compounds; higher oxidation states form covalent compounds or polyatomic ions.

**Halides.** Anhydrous halides by direct metal–halogen reaction: 2Fe(s) + 3Cl₂(g) → 2FeCl₃(s). Heating a metal halide with additional metal forms lower oxidation states: Fe(s) + 2FeCl₃(s) → 3FeCl₂(s). Fluorine generally forms fluorides of metals in their highest oxidation states. Stable aqueous halide solutions form by adding hydrohalic acid to carbonates/hydroxides/oxides:
- NiCO₃(s) + 2HF(aq) → NiF₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
- Co(OH)₂(s) + 2HBr(aq) → CoBr₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l)
- Cr(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CrCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)

Bond polarity varies with electronegativity and oxidation state. Low-oxidation-state halides are more ionic (high melting points): TiCl₂ and TiCl₃ have high melting points; TiCl₄ is a volatile liquid with covalent Ti–Cl bonds. Higher-oxidation-state tetrahalides hydrolyze like SiCl₄: TiCl₄(l) + 2H₂O(l) → TiO₂(s) + 4HCl(aq).

**Oxides.** Low oxidation states tend to be ionic; higher oxidation states more covalent. First transition series oxides from heating metals in air: Sc₂O₃, TiO₂, V₂O₅, Cr₂O₃, Mn₃O₄, Fe₃O₄, Co₃O₄, NiO, CuO. Alternative prep from hydroxides/carbonates/oxalates:
- FeC₂O₄(s) → FeO(s) + CO(g) + CO₂(g)
- Co(OH)₂(s) → CoO(s) + H₂O(g)

Except CrO₃ and Mn₂O₇, transition metal oxides are insoluble in water. Reactivity vs. oxidation state: lowest states are basic (react with acids); intermediate are amphoteric; highest are acidic. Examples:
- Basic: CoO(s) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Co(NO₃)₂(aq) + H₂O(l); Sc₂O₃(s) + 6HCl(aq) → 2ScCl₃(aq) + 3H₂O(l)
- Acidic (V(V), Cr(VI), Mn(VII)): CrO₃(s) + 2OH⁻(aq) → CrO₄²⁻(aq) + H₂O(l); CrO₃ and Mn₂O₇ react with water forming H₂CrO₄ and HMnO₄.

**Hydroxides.** Soluble hydroxide added to a metal salt gives a gelatinous precipitate: Co²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Co(OH)₂(s). Some precipitates are hydrated oxides: 4Fe³⁺(aq) + 6OH⁻(aq) + nH₂O(l) → 2Fe₂O₃·(n+3)H₂O(s). Avoid excess OH⁻ to prevent complex ion formation.

**Carbonates.** Insoluble for the first transition series: Ni²⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → NiCO₃(s). They react with acids (giving salt + CO₂ + water) and decompose on heating to oxides.

**Other salts.** Active metals react with acids: 2Sc(s) + 6HBr(aq) → 2ScBr₃(aq) + 3H₂(g). Substitution makes insoluble salts: Ba²⁺(aq) + CrO₄²⁻(aq) → BaCrO₄(s).

### High-Temperature Superconductors

A superconductor is "a substance that conducts electricity with no resistance." Commercial superconductors (NbTi, Nb₃Sn) require cooling below 23 K (−250 °C) using expensive liquid helium (bp 4 K). High-temperature superconductors discovered in the 1980s superconduct above 90 K; a typical material is **YBa₂Cu₃O₇** (yttrium/rare earth : barium : copper in 1:2:3 ratio), coolable with liquid nitrogen (bp 77 K) at ~1000× lower cost. Bismuth-strontium-copper oxides reach 110 K without rare earths. Applications: lossless power transmission, smaller microchips, medical devices, maglev trains, fusion containment. Challenge: brittleness.

**Lanthanides in devices:** Europium (flat screens), Neodymium (hard drives, crude-oil cracking, hybrid engines, wind-turbine magnets), Holmium (dental/medical), Dysprosium (hybrid engines, wind-turbine magnets). Dysprosium rose from $110/kg (2008) to $470/kg (2014).

## 19.2 Coordination Chemistry of Transition Metals

> Source: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/19-2-coordination-chemistry-of-transition-metals

### Defining Traits of Coordination Compounds

"Ions of the metals, especially the transition metals, are likely to form complexes." A coordination compound contains a central metal ion (or atom) bonded to ligands through coordinate covalent bonds — a Lewis acid–base interaction where both electrons in the bond come from a donor (Lewis base) to an acceptor (Lewis acid). Metal ions with partially filled d subshells typically form colored complex ions; those with empty (d⁰) or filled (d¹⁰) d subshells usually form colorless complexes.

### Coordination Sphere and Coordination Number

"The coordination sphere consists of the central metal ion or atom plus its attached ligands. Brackets in a formula enclose the coordination sphere; species outside the brackets are not part of the coordination sphere." The **coordination number** is "the number of donor atoms bonded to it":
- [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺: coordination number 2
- [CuCl₄]²⁻: coordination number 4
- [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺: coordination number 6

### Ligands

Ligands are Lewis-base donors with one or more electron pairs. **Monodentate ligands** connect through a single donor atom (NH₃, Cl⁻, H₂O). **Polydentate (chelating) ligands** bond through multiple donor atoms; a **chelate** forms when a polydentate ligand coordinates to a metal "rather like a crab's claw." Classification by denticity:
- Bidentate: two donor atoms (ethylenediamine, en, H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂)
- Tridentate: three
- Tetradentate: four (e.g., heme)
- Pentadentate: five
- Hexadentate: six (EDTA: (HO₂CCH₂)₂NCH₂CH₂N(CH₂CO₂H)₂)

### Coordination Geometries

| Coordination Number | Geometry | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Linear | [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ |
| 3 | Trigonal planar | [Cu(CN)₃]²⁻ |
| 4 | Tetrahedral (d⁰ or d¹⁰, low oxidation states) | [Ni(CO)₄] |
| 4 | Square planar (d⁸) | [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻ |
| 5 | Trigonal bipyramidal | [CoCl₅]²⁻ |
| 5 | Square pyramidal | [VO(CN)₄]²⁻ |
| 6 | Octahedral | [CoCl₆]³⁻ |
| 7 | Pentagonal bipyramid | [ZrF₇]³⁻ |
| 8 | Square antiprism | [ReF₈]²⁻ |
| 8 | Dodecahedron | [Mo(CN)₈]⁴⁻ |
| 9+ | More complicated | [ReH₉]²⁻ |

For octahedral complexes, the six donor atoms sit at the corners of an octahedron. Tetrahedral complexes (e.g., [Zn(CN)₄]²⁻) have ligand pairs at 109.5°. In square planar complexes (e.g., [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]), each ligand has two others at 90° (cis positions) and one at 180° (trans position).

### Nomenclature of Coordination Compounds

**Rule 1.** For ionic coordination compounds, name the cation first, then the anion.

**Rule 2.** Name ligands alphabetically before the central metal. Anionic ligands add -o to the stem (F⁻ = fluoro, Cl⁻ = chloro, CN⁻ = cyano, OH⁻ = hydroxo, C₂O₄²⁻ = oxalato, CO₃²⁻ = carbonato). Neutral ligands use the molecule name except four exceptions: aqua (H₂O), ammine (NH₃), carbonyl (CO), nitrosyl (NO).

**Rule 3.** Multiple ligands of the same type use di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, hexa-. When the ligand name itself contains di/tri/tetra or begins with a vowel, use bis-, tris-, tetrakis-.

**Rule 4.** For cationic/neutral complexes, the metal name is spelled like the element, followed by the oxidation state in Roman numerals in parentheses.

**Rule 5.** For anionic complexes, add -ate to the metal stem, then the Roman numeral. Latin names sometimes used: ferrate (iron), plumbate (lead), stannate (tin). The oxidation state is determined from ligand charges and the overall complex charge.

Examples:
- [Co(NH₃)₆]Cl₃ = hexaamminecobalt(III) chloride
- [Pt(NH₃)₄Cl₂]²⁺ = tetraamminedichloroplatinum(IV) ion
- [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ = diamminesilver(I) ion
- [Cr(H₂O)₄Cl₂]Cl = tetraaquadichlorochromium(III) chloride
- [Co(en)₃]₂(SO₄)₃ = tris(ethylenediamine)cobalt(III) sulfate
- [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₄] = diamminetetrachloroplatinum(IV)
- [Ni(en)₂Cl₂] = dichlorobis(ethylenediamine)nickel(II)
- [PtCl₆]²⁻ = hexachloroplatinate(IV) ion
- Na₂[SnCl₆] = sodium hexachlorostannate(IV)

**Example 19.4 — Coordination Numbers and Oxidation States.**
- Na₂[PtCl₆]: [PtCl₆]²⁻ has six chloride ligands; −2 = −6 + x → Pt is 4+. Name: sodium hexachloroplatinate(IV); CN = 6.
- K₃[Fe(C₂O₄)₃]: charge 3−, three oxalate (each −2); −3 = −6 + x → iron(III). Name: potassium trisoxalatoferrate(III); CN = 6 (oxalate is bidentate).
- [Co(NH₃)₅Cl]Cl₂: [Co(NH₃)₅Cl]²⁺; +2 = −1 + x → Co is 3+. Name: pentaamminechlorocobalt(III) chloride; CN = 6.

### Isomerism in Complexes

"Isomers are different chemical species that have the same chemical formula."

**Geometric isomerism.** Same atoms connected by the same bonds but different spatial orientation. For octahedral complexes with two different ligands: **cis** (ligands adjacent) vs **trans** (directly across). Different geometric isomers are different compounds with different properties. Example: [Co(H₂O)₄Cl₂]⁺ exists as cis (violet, polar) and trans (green, nonpolar — dipoles cancel) isomers.

**Example 19.5 — Geometric Isomers.** [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] shown in cis geometry (two Cl adjacent); the trans isomer (each ligand opposite an identical ligand) is trans-diamminedichloroplatinum(II).

**Optical isomerism (enantiomers).** "Optical isomers, or enantiomers...are exact mirror images of each other but cannot be lined up so that all parts match" (nonsuperimposable mirror images). Pairs of optical isomers have identical bp, polarity, solubility — they differ only in how they affect polarized light and react with other optical isomers. [M(en)₃]ⁿ⁺ forms enantiomers; right-handed (natural) DNA binds only one isomer. The cis-[Co(en)₂Cl₂]⁺ ion exists as a pair of optical isomers; the trans isomer does not.

**Linkage isomerism.** A ligand binds through two different atoms. CN⁻: carbon (cyano) or nitrogen (isocyano). SCN⁻: sulfur or nitrogen — [Co(NH₃)₅SCN]²⁺ vs [Co(NH₃)₅NCS]²⁺.

**Ionization isomerism (coordination isomerism).** One anionic ligand in the inner sphere is swapped with the counter ion in the outer sphere. Example: [CoCl₆][Br] and [CoCl₅Br][Cl].

### Natural and Technological Applications

**Hemoglobin.** Heme is "the iron-containing complex in hemoglobin" — a polydentate (tetradentate porphyrin) ligand with four donor (nitrogen) atoms coordinating iron. Oxygen is transported bound to the iron center; hemoglobin transports O₂ only if iron is Fe²⁺ — oxidation to Fe³⁺ prevents transport.

**Chlorophyll.** The green plant pigment, a complex containing magnesium. Plants appear green because chlorophyll absorbs red and purple light; reflected light appears green. The absorbed energy drives photosynthesis.

**Vitamin B-12.** Contains a cobalt coordination complex.

**Cisplatin.** In 1965, Michigan State scientists found a platinum complex that inhibited cell division; cis-diamminedichloroplatinum(II), [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂], was approved in 1978 for cancer treatment. In all cases these are cis isomers, never trans. Related drugs: carboplatin, oxaliplatin, satraplatin.

**Chelation therapy.** EDTA ties up undesirable metal ions (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺). British Anti-Lewisite (BAL, HSCH₂CH(SH)CH₂OH), developed in WWI against the arsenic gas Lewisite, makes a water-soluble metal chelate excreted by the kidneys. Enterobactin (from bacteria) complexes iron to control iron overload in Cooley's anemia. Dimercaptosuccinic acid (DMSA) treats heavy-metal poisoning, usually binding via one sulfur and one oxygen in a five-membered ring.

**Electroplating.** [Ag(CN)₂]⁻ and [Au(CN)₂]⁻ prevent metal clustering, giving smoother coatings.

**Catalysis.** Over 90% of manufactured products use catalysts; vanadium oxide makes ~230,000,000 tons of sulfuric acid yearly. Transition metal catalysts make plastics, detergents, fertilizers, pharmaceuticals.

**Pigments and dyes.** Square planar copper(II) phthalocyanine blue is used in blue ink, blue jeans, and blue paints.

**Example 19.6 — Chelation Therapy.** DMSA has six possible donor atoms (all O and S lone pairs), but geometrically only two coordinate at once — most commonly one S and one O forming a five-membered ring. Biologically important metals that can be disrupted: Ca, Fe, Zn, Cu.

## 19.3 Spectroscopic and Magnetic Properties of Coordination Compounds

> Source: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/19-3-spectroscopic-and-magnetic-properties-of-coordination-compounds

### Crystal Field Theory (CFT)

Crystal field theory explains transition metal complexes through "electrostatic interactions between the electrons from the ligands and the electrons in the unhybridized d orbitals of the central metal atom." Ligands are treated as point charges that repel d-orbital electrons. In pure form, "CFT ignores any covalent bonding between ligands and metal ions."

### Octahedral d-Orbital Splitting

With six ligands along the Cartesian axes, the five d orbitals split into two levels:
- **eₘ (eg) orbitals (higher energy):** dz² and dx²−y² — lobes point directly at ligands → greater repulsion.
- **t₂g orbitals (lower energy):** dxy, dxz, dyz — lobes point between ligands → less repulsion.

The energy difference is the **crystal field splitting energy**, **Δ_oct** ("oct" = octahedral).

### Factors Affecting Δ_oct

Magnitude depends on: the nature of the six ligands; the charge on the metal; and which d orbitals (3d, 4d, 5d) are used.

**Spectrochemical series** (field strength, weak → strong):

**I⁻ < Br⁻ < Cl⁻ < F⁻ < H₂O < NH₃ < en < NO₂⁻ < CN⁻**

Weak-field ligands (left) give small splittings; strong-field ligands (right) give large splittings.

### High-Spin vs. Low-Spin

Electron filling depends on Δ_oct relative to the **pairing energy (P)** — "the energy needed to pair up two electrons in a single orbital."
- **Δ_oct > P (strong field):** electrons pair in t₂g before occupying eg → **low-spin** (fewest unpaired electrons). Example: [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ (Fe²⁺, d⁶) — all six electrons paired in t₂g.
- **Δ_oct < P (weak field):** electrons singly occupy all d orbitals before pairing → **high-spin** (most unpaired electrons). Example: [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ (Fe²⁺, d⁶) — four unpaired electrons.

High/low-spin difference occurs only for d⁴, d⁵, d⁶, d⁷.

**Example 19.7.** Predicting unpaired electrons (octahedral):
- Cr³⁺ (d³): three unpaired regardless of field strength.
- Cu²⁺ (d⁹): one unpaired regardless of field strength.
- Co³⁺ (d⁶) with NO₂⁻ (strong field): low-spin, zero unpaired.

### Tetrahedral Geometry

Four ligands sit between the axes; no orbital points directly at them. The splitting is inverted vs octahedral: **e orbitals (lower)** and **t₂ orbitals (higher)** — magnitude **Δ_tet ≈ (4/9)Δ_oct**. Because Δ_tet is small, "all tetrahedral complexes are high spin." (Note: source text shows minor internal inconsistency on which set is lower; the standard CFT result is e below t₂ for tetrahedral.)

**Example 19.8.** Tetrahedral d⁴: all four electrons unpaired because Δ_tet is small; tetrahedral complexes are always high-spin.

### Square Planar Geometry

Square planar results from removing two trans (z-axis) ligands from an octahedron. The d orbitals order from lowest to highest energy: dxz, dyz < dz² < dxy < dx²−y².

### Magnetic Properties

Complexes with unpaired electrons are **paramagnetic** (attracted to a magnetic field); all-paired complexes are **diamagnetic** (slightly repelled). "The size of the magnetic moment...is related directly to the number of...unpaired electrons: the greater the number of unpaired electrons, the larger the magnetic moment." Low-spin [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ is diamagnetic; high-spin [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ is paramagnetic (four unpaired).

### Colors of Transition Metal Complexes

Coordination compounds absorb visible light when the photon energy matches the d-orbital splitting (a d–d transition). The eye perceives the **complement** of the absorbed colors (color wheel): red absorbed → green observed; violet absorbed → lemon yellow observed. [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺ appears blue because it absorbs orange and red, leaving blue and green.

**Calculating Δ from wavelength (Planck's equation E = hν, ν = c/λ).** For [Ti(H₂O)₆]³⁺, absorbance max at 499 nm:
- ν = (3.00×10⁸ m/s) / (499×10⁻⁹ m) = 6.01×10¹⁴ Hz
- E = (6.63×10⁻³⁴ J·s)(6.01×10¹⁴ Hz) = 3.99×10⁻¹⁹ J/ion = Δ_oct

It absorbs ~450–600 nm (blue through orange), transmitting indigo, violet, and red → appears purple. A green complex absorbs red light (620–800 nm).

**Ligand field strength and color.** Strong-field ligands → large Δ_oct → higher-energy (shorter-wavelength, violet/blue) photons absorbed → complexes appear yellow, orange, or red. Weak-field ligands → small Δ_oct → lower-energy (longer-wavelength, red/orange) photons absorbed → complexes appear blue-green, blue, or indigo.

**d¹⁰ configuration.** Cu⁺ (d¹⁰): all eg orbitals filled; exciting electrons needs UV-energy photons → no visible absorption → colorless. Example: [Cu(CN)₂]⁻ is colorless.

**d⁹ configuration.** Cu²⁺ (d⁹): a vacant eg orbital allows visible-range excitation → Cu²⁺ complexes are almost always colored (blue, blue-green, violet, or yellow).

**Example 19.9.** [Ti(H₂O)₆]³⁺ single d-electron transitions t₂g → eg; max at 499 nm gives Δ_oct = 3.99×10⁻¹⁹ J; absorbs 450–600 nm → appears purple.
