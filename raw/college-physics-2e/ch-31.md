## Chapter Introduction

The chapter examines the internal structure of atomic nuclei and the phenomenon of radioactive decay — why some nuclei are inherently unstable and spontaneously emit high-energy radiation. Objectives across the chapter: understand nuclear composition; analyze decay processes and emitted radiation; apply conservation laws to nuclear transformations; compute decay rates and half-lives; relate binding energy to stability; and explore quantum tunneling.

Applications/context: Earth's retained internal heat from radioactive decay, stellar energy production, medical diagnostics and therapy, archaeological dating, and nuclear power.

---

## 31.1 Nuclear Radioactivity

**Objectives:** explain the nature and origin of nuclear radiation; distinguish the three main emission types; explain ionization of atoms by radiation; define range (penetration depth).

**Definitions/terms:**
- **Nuclear radioactivity** — spontaneous emission of energy from atomic nuclei, with conversion of mass to energy per E = mc².
- **Nuclear radiation** — the rays emitted by decaying nuclei.
- **Radioactive** — describes a substance/object that emits nuclear radiation.
- **Ionizing radiation** — any radiation able to produce ionization (nuclear or otherwise).
- **Range** — distance radiation travels in a material before its energy dissipates.

**Laws/principles:** mass–energy equivalence (E = mc²) explains huge energy from tiny mass loss; conservation of energy and momentum govern collisions; ionization principle — radiation loses energy mainly through ionization events, each removing energy.

**Equations:**
- Classical kinetic energy: KE = ½mv²
- Momentum change in a collision: Δp = FΔt

**Three radiation types:**

| Type | Charge | Identity | Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha (α) | +2|qₑ| | helium nucleus (⁴He) | ~mm in tissue; ~cm in air |
| Beta (β) | −|qₑ| | electron | ~mm in aluminum; tens of cm in tissue |
| Gamma (γ) | neutral | high-energy photon | cm of lead; meters of concrete |

**Factors affecting range:** higher energy → deeper penetration; denser/higher-electron-density material → shorter range; faster/uncharged particles interact less (γ penetrates most); larger charge → more ionization → shorter range.

**Applications:** radium-doped aircraft dials (emitted hazardous radon), gamma tracers for medical imaging (penetrate tissue for external detection, unlike α/β), electroscope-style dosimeters detecting ionization discharge, ionization damage to electronics and cancer-cell destruction.

## 31.2 Radiation Detection and Detectors

**Objectives:** describe how a Geiger tube works; survey radiation-detection technologies.

**Definitions/terms:**
- **Ionizing radiation** — energy affecting tissue without triggering nerve sensation, so external detection is needed.
- **Geiger tube** — gas-filled cylindrical detector with an axial wire; produces electrical pulses when radiation creates ion pairs.
- **Scintillator** — material that converts absorbed radiation energy into light via atomic excitation.
- **Photomultiplier tube** — converts scintillator light into an amplified electrical signal through cascading electron multiplication.

**Mechanisms:**
- **Photographic film** — radiation deposits energy (above a few eV) causing chemical change (darkening); efficiency imperfect.
- **Geiger counter** — radiation ionizes the fill gas, ions migrate to electrodes producing current pulses; counts indicate presence/relative intensity only, not energy/charge/identity.
- **Scintillation** — radiation excites atoms producing light flashes; light output correlates with deposited energy and particle properties; high time resolution.
- **Solid-state (semiconductor) detectors** — convert radiation-induced ionization directly to current; unidirectional conduction; greater stopping power → higher efficiency than gas detectors.

**Applications:** medical/dental radiography (film), radiation safety monitoring, satellite detection of distant-galaxy radiation, body-burden (internal contamination) analysis, accelerator particle-physics research.

## 31.3 Substructure of the Nucleus

**Objectives:** describe the nucleus and its components; explain atomic number and elemental identity; distinguish isotopes; calculate nuclear density; describe nuclear binding forces.

**Definitions/terms:**
- **Nucleons** — protons and neutrons collectively.
- **Atomic number (Z)** — proton count; identifies the element.
- **Mass number (A)** — total nucleon count (protons + neutrons).
- **Isotopes** — same Z, different neutron number N.
- **Nuclide** — a specific nucleus defined by its proton-neutron combination.
- **Unified atomic mass unit (u)** — mass scale where ¹²C = exactly 12 u.
- **Nuclear force** — short-range attractive (strong) force binding nucleons (the weak force also acts in the nucleus).
- **Nuclide notation:** $^{A}_{Z}X_{N}$ (X = chemical symbol).

**Principles/laws:**
- Nuclear radius: r = r₀A^(1/3), r₀ ≈ 1.2 fm.
- Mass–energy conversion: E = mc²; 1 u ↔ 931.5 MeV.
- Volume ∝ A — nucleons are tightly packed (constant nuclear density).
- Nucleon masses nearly equal: m_p ≈ 1836 m_e, m_n ≈ 1839 m_e.

**Equations:**
1. A = N + Z
2. r = r₀A^(1/3), r₀ = 1.2 fm
3. 1 u = 1.6605 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 931.5 MeV/c²
4. Proton: E = mc² = 938.27 MeV (m_p ≈ 938.27 MeV/c²)
5. Density: ρ = m/V = m / [(4/3)πr³]

**Worked example — Iron-56 nuclear density:** illustrates applying the radius formula to a mid-mass nucleus, unit conversion (fm → m), and computing the extreme nuclear density (~10¹⁴ × water).

**Applications/significance:** neutron-to-proton ratio governs stability (even numbers enhance binding); decay energies come from strong-force work; neutron stars have nuclear-density matter; nucleon arrangements explain which isotopes are stable.

## 31.4 Nuclear Decay and Conservation Laws

**Objectives:** characterize decay processes and products; state conservation laws governing nuclear reactions; distinguish parent vs daughter; compute decay energy via E = (Δm)c².

**Definitions/terms:**
- **Radioactive decay** — spontaneous transformation of an unstable nuclide to a more stable product via particle/photon emission.
- **Parent nucleus** — the original unstable nuclide; **daughter nucleus** — the product.
- **Decay series** — successive decays until stability (e.g., ²³⁸U chain → ²⁰⁶Pb).
- **Alpha particle (α)** — ⁴He nucleus (2 p, 2 n).
- **Beta-minus (β⁻)** — electron emission; **positron (β⁺)** — antimatter electron (charge +1, electron-family −1).
- **Neutrino (ν)** — nearly massless, neutral particle carrying energy/momentum in beta decay; interacts only via the weak force.
- **Gamma (γ) decay** — photon emission from an excited nucleus.
- **Electron capture (EC)** — inner-shell electron absorbed by the nucleus.

**Conservation laws:** (1) charge; (2) nucleon number A; (3) linear and angular momentum; (4) mass–energy, E = (Δm)c²; (5) electron family number.

**Decay equations (nuclide notation):**
- **Alpha:** $^{A}_{Z}X_{N} \rightarrow\, ^{A-4}_{Z-2}Y_{N-2} +\, ^{4}_{2}He_{2}$
  - Examples: ²³⁸U → ²³⁴Th + ⁴He; ²³⁹Pu → ²³⁵U + ⁴He
- **Beta-minus:** $^{A}_{Z}X_{N} \rightarrow\, ^{A}_{Z+1}Y_{N-1} + \beta^{-} + \bar{\nu}_e$ (a neutron → proton + electron + antineutrino)
  - Example: ⁶⁰Co → ⁶⁰Ni + β⁻ + ν̄ₑ
- **Beta-plus:** $^{A}_{Z}X_{N} \rightarrow\, ^{A}_{Z-1}Y_{N+1} + \beta^{+} + \nu_e$ (a proton → neutron + positron + neutrino); positron later annihilates: e⁺ + e⁻ → γ + γ
  - Example: ²²Na → ²²Ne + β⁺ + νₑ
- **Electron capture:** $^{A}_{Z}X_{N} + e^{-} \rightarrow\, ^{A}_{Z-1}Y_{N+1} + \nu_e$
- **Gamma:** $^{A}_{Z}X^{*}_{N} \rightarrow\, ^{A}_{Z}X_{N} + \gamma_1 + \gamma_2 + \ldots$ (asterisk = excited state; same element/isotope, only energy changes)
  - Example: ⁶⁰Ni* → ⁶⁰Ni + γ₁ + γ₂ (gammas used in cancer therapy)

**Decay-energy concept:** energy released = mass deficit × c². Using neutral-atom masses, β⁻ uses Δm = m(parent) − m(daughter) (daughter atomic mass already includes the emitted electron); β⁺ requires Δm = m(parent) − [m(daughter) + 2mₑ].

**Historical context:** Wolfgang Pauli proposed the neutrino (1930) to save conservation of energy/momentum/angular momentum in beta decay; detected 1953. Chien-Shiung Wu experimentally confirmed Fermi's weak-force theory of beta decay.

**Stability rule:** neutron-rich nuclei undergo β⁻ (lowering n/p ratio); proton-rich nuclei undergo β⁺ or EC (raising n/p), both moving toward the line of stability.

## 31.5 Half-Life and Activity

**Objectives:** define half-life and relate it quantitatively to decay; perform radioactive dating; define and compute activity.

**Definitions/terms:**
- **Half-life (t₁/₂)** — time for half the original nuclei to decay; each nucleus has a 50% chance of surviving any one half-life, independent of history.
- **Activity (R)** — number of decays per unit time in a sample.
- **Decay constant (λ)** — characterizes how fast a nuclide decays.
- **Radioactive dating** — estimating a specimen's age from the remaining abundance of a naturally occurring unstable isotope.

**Principles:** decay is purely statistical (random); a nucleus's decay probability per interval is constant. Activity is proportional to the number of nuclei present and inversely proportional to half-life.

**Equations:**
1. Exponential decay: N = N₀e^(−λt)
2. λ = ln(2)/t₁/₂ ≈ 0.693/t₁/₂
3. Activity definition: R = ΔN/Δt
4. Activity (proportional form): R = 0.693N/t₁/₂  (= λN)
5. Activity decay: R = R₀e^(−λt)

**Units of activity:**
- **Becquerel (Bq)** — 1 decay/second (SI).
- **Curie (Ci)** — 1 Ci = 3.70 × 10¹⁰ Bq (≈ activity of 1 g of ²²⁶Ra).

**Carbon-14 dating:** atmospheric ¹⁴C is taken up by living organisms; at death uptake stops while existing ¹⁴C keeps decaying. Comparing current ¹⁴C/C ratio (living tissue ≈ 1.3 × 10⁻¹²) to expected gives age; reliable to ~50,000–60,000 yr. Older materials use longer-lived isotopes (²³⁸U, t₁/₂ = 4.5 × 10⁹ yr, for rocks).

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- *Shroud of Turin* — apply the decay equation when 92% of expected ¹⁴C remains to find age.
- *¹⁴C activity in living tissue* — compute decay rate from half-life and nuclei count; convert decays/yr ↔ Bq ↔ Ci.
- *Chernobyl ¹³⁷Cs release* — work backward from measured activity to escaped radioactive mass using the half-life relation.

**Applications:** archaeological/biological dating, medical therapy and imaging, environmental contamination tracking, geological (uranium) dating.

## 31.6 Binding Energy

**Objectives:** understand binding energy and its role in stability; compute and interpret binding energy per nucleon.

**Definitions/terms:**
- **Binding energy (BE)** — total energy needed to disassemble a nucleus into its separate nucleons; a bound nucleus has less mass than its separated parts.
- **Mass defect (Δm)** — difference between the summed nucleon masses and the actual nuclear mass; this "missing" mass equals BE/c².
- **Binding energy per nucleon (BE/A)** — measures how tightly each nucleon is bound on average; enables comparison across nuclei.

**Principles:** the strong nuclear force is ~100× stronger than Coulomb repulsion but far shorter range. Light nuclei: BE/A rises with A; mid-mass (A ≈ 60): maximum binding/stability; heavy nuclei: Coulomb proton repulsion increasingly overwhelms attraction.

**Equations:**
- Mass defect: Δm = [Z·m(¹H) + N·m_n] − m(ᴬX)
- Binding energy (atomic masses): BE = {[Z·m(¹H) + N·m_n] − m(ᴬX)}c²
- Mass–energy: E = (Δm)c²
- Conversion: 1 u = 931.5 MeV/c²

**Worked example — ⁴He (alpha) binding energy:** shows that closed-shell nuclei with Z = N have unusually high BE/A (~7.07 MeV/nucleon vs ~3 for neighboring light nuclei), explaining why alpha particles form and why alpha decay of heavy nuclei is energetically favorable.

**Applications:**
- **BE/A curve** (BE/A vs A) peaks near **iron-56**, marking the most stable nuclei; spikes mark "magic numbers" (closed shells) and Z = N nuclei.
- Cosmic abundance: common elements (H, ⁴He, ¹²C) correspond to BE/A peaks — stability drives prevalence.
- Energy production: the curve's downward slope at high A explains fission (heavy → mid-mass) and fusion (light → mid-mass), both releasing net energy; basis of stellar nucleosynthesis and reactors.
- Radioactive heating: long-lived primordial isotopes (²³⁸U, ⁴⁰K) and chains keep Earth's interior molten.

## 31.7 Tunneling

**Objectives:** define tunneling; explain a potential barrier; describe quantum-mechanical tunneling.

**Definitions/terms:**
- **Tunneling (barrier penetration)** — a quantum process where a particle crosses an energy barrier it classically lacks the energy to enter, appearing on the far side without occupying the intermediate region.
- **Potential barrier** — an energy threshold confining a particle; classically impassable without enough KE, quantum-mechanically passable with some probability.
- **Wave function** — varies smoothly across a barrier, decaying exponentially inside it rather than dropping abruptly to zero.

**Principles:** probability of finding a particle ∝ |ψ|²; the wave function is nonzero beyond the barrier, allowing detection there. (Classical analogy: a marble in a bowl can never escape the rim; a nucleon can.)

**Historical context:** George Gamow (1928) explained alpha emission via tunneling; J. Robert Oppenheimer contributed theory, enabling prediction of alpha-decay half-lives spanning ~17 orders of magnitude.

**Applications:**
- **Alpha decay** — an alpha particle inside the nucleus is ~8 MeV short of the energy needed to escape the nuclear attraction, yet tunnels through the repulsive Coulomb barrier and is emitted spontaneously.
- **Energy–half-life relation** — more energetic alpha decay implies a thinner effective barrier and shorter half-life (less exponential suppression).
- **Scanning tunneling microscope (STM)** — electrons tunnel across a thin gap between probe tip and surface; monitoring tunneling current images individual atoms.
