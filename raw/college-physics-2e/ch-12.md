## Introduction

Fluid dynamics is the physics of fluids in motion. It lets us analyze phenomena such as rising smoke, water discharged from a dam, and the circulation of blood. The chapter develops flow rate and continuity, energy conservation for moving fluids (Bernoulli), internal friction (viscosity) and its laminar-flow law (Poiseuille), the onset of turbulence (Reynolds number), drag on objects in viscous fluids (Stokes' law, terminal speed), and molecular-scale transport (diffusion, osmosis, dialysis, active transport). Many applications are biological and medical.

## 12.1 Flow Rate and Its Relation to Velocity

**Objectives:** compute volumetric flow rate; recognize volume units; understand incompressible-fluid behavior; interpret the equation of continuity.

**Definitions:**
- **Flow rate (Q):** volume of fluid passing a location per unit time. SI unit m³/s.
- **Incompressible fluid:** liquid of (essentially) constant density; volume unchanged by pressure.
- **Average velocity (v̄):** mean speed of fluid through a cross-section.
- **Cross-sectional area (A):** area perpendicular to flow.

**Equations:**
- Q = V/t  (volume per time)
- Q = A·v̄  (flow rate from area and average velocity)
- Equation of continuity (single tube): Q₁ = Q₂  →  A₁·v̄₁ = A₂·v̄₂
- Branching systems: n₁·A₁·v̄₁ = n₂·A₂·v̄₂

**Principle (continuity):** For incompressible fluid in a closed system, the same volume passes any point per unit time. Narrowing the conduit raises velocity; widening it lowers velocity (velocity inversely proportional to area, hence to radius squared for circular tubes).

**Worked examples (concept):**
- Cardiac output: convert flow-rate units and apply Q = V/t to get total volume over long times.
- Nozzle effect: continuity explains why a narrow opening produces a much faster jet (v ∝ 1/r²).
- Capillary network: branching conserves total flow while blood velocity falls sharply because total cross-sectional area of all capillaries is enormous.

**Applications:** garden-hose nozzles; cardiovascular flow (blood slows in capillaries despite constant cardiac output); water treatment/hydroelectric velocity control; river flow vs. channel geometry.

## 12.2 Bernoulli's Equation

**Objectives:** interpret terms of Bernoulli's equation; connect to energy conservation; derive Bernoulli's principle; apply it; identify applications.

**Definitions:**
- **Bernoulli's principle:** pressure drops in a rapidly moving fluid (at constant height).
- **Entrainment:** a fast fluid stream's low pressure draws other fluid into it.

**Principle:** Bernoulli's equation is conservation of mechanical energy per unit volume for an incompressible, frictionless (nonviscous) fluid; total energy per unit volume is constant along a streamline.

**Equations:**
- General: P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant
- Between two points: P₁ + ½ρv₁² + ρgh₁ = P₂ + ½ρv₂² + ρgh₂
- Static fluid (v=0): P₁ + ρgh₁ = P₂ + ρgh₂
- Bernoulli's principle (constant height): P₁ + ½ρv₁² = P₂ + ½ρv₂²
- KE per unit volume = ½ρv²; PE_grav per unit volume = ρgh

**Worked example (concept):** Example 12.4 — pressure drop in a hose nozzle: with constant elevation, the rise in ½ρv² as fluid accelerates is matched by an equal drop in pressure.

**Applications:** shower curtain pulled inward; passing vehicles pushed together; entrainment devices (Bunsen burner, atomizer/perfume sprayer, aspirator, chimney draft); airplane-wing lift (faster flow over the curved top gives lower pressure); sail propulsion; velocity measurement with Pitot tubes and manometers.

## 12.3 The Most General Applications of Bernoulli's Equation

**Objectives:** compute exit speed via Torricelli's theorem; compute power in fluid flow.

**Definitions:**
- **Torricelli's theorem:** exit speed from an opening depends only on the vertical fall height, not on the opening's size or initial direction.
- **Gauge pressure:** pressure relative to atmospheric.

**Equations:**
- Torricelli: v² = v₁² + 2g(h₁ − h₂)  (≈ v² = 2gh when starting at rest)
- Power in fluid flow: Power = (P + ½ρv² + ρgh)·Q, splitting into PQ (pressure power), ½ρv²Q (kinetic), ρghQ (gravitational)
- Continuity: Q = A·v

**Worked examples (concept):**
- Example 12.5 (fire-hose nozzle): all three Bernoulli terms vary — pressure falls as water accelerates and rises in elevation.
- Example 12.6 (pump power): a pump supplies power by raising fluid pressure; required power scales with pressure increase times flow rate.

**Applications:** discharge from dams/reservoirs; fire-hose nozzle optimization; pump power sizing; non-constant-depth flow.

## 12.4 Viscosity and Laminar Flow; Poiseuille's Law

**Objectives:** distinguish laminar vs. turbulent flow; define viscosity; apply Poiseuille's law to flow rate and resistance; explain pressure drop.

**Definitions:**
- **Viscosity (η):** internal friction of a fluid; resistance to shear. Juice = low η, syrup = high η. SI unit Pa·s.
- **Laminar flow:** smooth flow in parallel layers; continuous streamlines.
- **Turbulent flow:** chaotic flow with eddies that mix layers; broken streamlines.
- **Streamlines:** paths of small fluid volumes.
- **Resistance (R):** everything other than pressure that impedes flow (viscosity, tube length, radius).

**Equations:**
- Viscosity (parallel-plate): F = ηvA/L; rearranged η = FL/(vA)
- Poiseuille resistance: R = 8ηl/(πr⁴)  (resistance ∝ 1/r⁴; doubling r cuts R by 16×)
- Flow vs. pressure & resistance: Q = (P₂ − P₁)/R
- Poiseuille's law (flow): Q = (P₂ − P₁)πr⁴/(8ηl)

**Worked examples (concept):**
- Plaque deposits: a small radius reduction (≈16%) halves blood flow; the heart must raise pressure to compensate.
- IV infusion: find needle-entrance pressure needed to deliver saline at a set rate against venous back-pressure.

**Applications:** vasodilation/constriction regulating organ perfusion; plaque reducing flow and causing turbulence; municipal-water pressure drops at peak use; cold engine oil (η rises as temperature falls); faucet constrictions creating turbulence.

## 12.5 The Onset of Turbulence

**Objectives:** compute Reynolds number; classify flow regime.

**Definitions:**
- **Reynolds number (N_R):** dimensionless number predicting laminar-to-turbulent transition.
- **Korotkoff sounds:** sounds from turbulent arterial flow during blood-pressure measurement.
- **Chaotic behavior:** deterministic but so sensitive to initial conditions that prediction is impractical.

**Principle / classification (flow in tubes):**
- N_R < 2000: laminar (stable)
- 2000 < N_R < 3000: unstable/transitional
- N_R > 3000: turbulent

**Equation:** N_R = 2ρvr/η  (ρ density, v velocity, r tube radius, η viscosity)

**Worked example (concept):** saline through an infusion needle gives N_R ≈ 523, confirming laminar flow (well below 2000).

**Applications:** detecting arterial blockage by stethoscope (turbulence); heart-valve murmurs; ultrasound detection of turbulence from aneurysms; normal circulation is laminar except in the aorta where high speed can induce turbulence.

## 12.6 Motion of an Object in a Viscous Fluid

**Objectives:** compute Reynolds number for a moving object; classify the flow; identify terminal speed.

**Definitions:**
- **Reynolds number (object), N_R′:** dimensionless number predicting the flow pattern around a moving body.
- **Turbulent wake:** chaotic trailing flow behind a fast-moving object.
- **Terminal speed:** constant velocity when drag balances the driving force (zero net force).
- **Viscous drag:** velocity-dependent resistance proportional to viscosity.

**Principle / classification:**
- N_R′ < 1: laminar
- 1 < N_R′ < 10: transition
- 10 < N_R′ < 10⁶: mixed/oscillating
- N_R′ > 10⁶: fully turbulent

**Equations:**
- Reynolds number (object): N_R′ = ρvL/η  (L = characteristic length, e.g. sphere diameter)
- Stokes' law (small sphere, laminar drag): F_S = 6πRηv

**Worked example (concept):** a thrown ball (7.40 cm, 40 m/s in air) gives N_R′ ≈ 2.11×10⁵, so its wake is turbulent — large fast objects make turbulence, limiting where Bernoulli's principle applies.

**Applications:** sedimentation of cells/dust/particles; centrifugation to speed separation; drag on cyclists and skydivers; measuring viscosity from terminal speeds.

## 12.7 Molecular Transport Phenomena: Diffusion, Osmosis, and Related Processes

**Objectives:** characterize diffusion, osmosis, dialysis, active transport; compute diffusion rates.

**Definitions:**
- **Diffusion:** movement of a substance due to random thermal molecular motion, even without bulk flow.
- **Random walk:** the multi-directional path of a thermally agitated molecule.
- **Root-mean-square distance (x_rms):** statistical average distance diffused.
- **Diffusion constant (D):** substance/medium-specific transport parameter, units m²/s.
- **Osmosis:** water transport across a semipermeable membrane from high to low water concentration.
- **Dialysis:** solute transport across a semipermeable membrane down a concentration gradient.
- **Semipermeable membrane:** selectively permeable barrier (pores or solubility-based).
- **Back pressure:** hydrostatic pressure that builds during osmosis and eventually stops net flow.
- **Relative osmotic pressure:** the back pressure that stops osmosis (with pure water on one side it is the osmotic pressure).
- **Active transport:** energy-using membrane process moving substances against a gradient.

**Principles:**
- Net diffusion goes from high to low concentration; rate is proportional to the concentration difference; net movement stops when concentrations equalize.
- At fixed temperature, average molecular speed is inversely related to molecular mass, so heavier molecules diffuse more slowly.
- D increases with temperature because mean kinetic energy (½mv²) rises with absolute temperature.

**Equations:**
- x_rms = √(2Dt)
- Back/osmotic pressure (column): ρgh = relative osmotic pressure

**Worked example (concept):** time for glucose to diffuse ~1.0 cm in water is ~21 hours — diffusion is extremely slow over macroscopic distances, so mechanical mixing is needed in practice.

**Applications:** corneal oxygen via diffusion across tear film; Epsom-salt soaks drawing fluid from swollen tissue (osmosis); kidneys (osmosis + dialysis + active transport); evolution of circulatory systems beyond diffusion's reach; plant turgor (osmotic pressure); reverse-osmosis desalination; ~25% of body energy powering active transport.
