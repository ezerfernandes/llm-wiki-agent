## 22.0 Introduction to Magnetism

Magnetism is a fundamental force arising from moving electric charges and intrinsic properties of matter. It accounts for phenomena from compass needles to auroras. Magnetic and electric forces are two aspects of a single electromagnetic interaction, tightly coupled through moving charges. Magnetic materials were known in ancient Magnesia (modern Turkey) before systematic study began.

Applications surveyed: aurora (solar particles deflected by Earth's field), refrigerator/permanent magnets, navigation compasses, electric motors (torque on current coils), generators, MRI imaging, magnetic data storage (giant magnetoresistance), and ferrous-particle separation.

---

## 22.1 Magnets

**Objectives:** distinguish magnetic north/south poles and their directional properties; explain pole-pole forces.

**Terms:**
- Magnetic poles — every magnet has two; suspended freely, the north-seeking pole points toward geographic north, south-seeking pole toward geographic south.
- Monopole — a hypothetical isolated single pole; not observed in nature. Poles always occur in matched pairs.

**Principles:**
- Like poles repel, unlike poles attract (analogous to electric charges).
- Inseparability of poles: cutting a magnet yields two complete magnets, each with both poles — true down to atomic/subatomic scales.

**Applications/Phenomena:**
- Compasses work because Earth behaves as a giant bar magnet whose south-seeking pole sits near the geographic North Pole.
- Magnetic dipoles appear universally: sunspot pairs, electrons, protons, neutrons.
- Magnets attract ferromagnetic materials (iron).

## 22.2 Ferromagnets and Electromagnets

**Objectives:** characterize ferromagnetic materials; explain magnetic domains; identify Curie temperature; link current to magnetism.

**Terms:**
- Ferromagnetic materials — iron, cobalt, nickel, gadolinium, rare-earth alloys (e.g., neodymium); strongly magnetic, can be permanently magnetized.
- Magnetic domains — microscopic regions where atomic magnetic moments align uniformly, each acting as a tiny bar magnet.
- Curie temperature — temperature above which a ferromagnet loses magnetization; for iron, 1043 K (770 °C).
- Electromagnets — temporary magnets created by electric current.
- Electromagnetism — principle by which currents produce magnetic effects.

**Principles:**
- An external field aligns and grows favorably oriented domains, magnetizing the material.
- Thermal energy or mechanical shock randomizes domains, demagnetizing a magnet.
- Electric current is the source of all magnetism, including ferromagnetism.
- Currents always produce dipole fields; isolated monopoles remain unobserved.

**Concepts illustrated:** domain growth/reorientation under an external field; permanent magnetization by heating in a field then cooling; iron filings show identical field geometry around a current coil and a bar magnet.

**Applications:** scrap-metal lifting electromagnets with ferromagnetic cores; superconducting electromagnets in MRI; beam-steering electromagnets in particle accelerators; digital data storage via reversible domain magnetization.

## 22.3 Magnetic Fields and Magnetic Field Lines

**Objectives:** build a conceptual model of magnetic fields; draw/interpret field lines for various configurations.

**Terms:**
- Magnetic field (B) — vector quantity representing the influence that lets magnetic forces act at a distance.
- Magnetic field lines — visual representation of direction and strength; align with compass needles, run from north to south poles externally.
- Areal density — lines per unit perpendicular area, proportional to field magnitude.

**Four properties of magnetic field lines:**
1. Field direction is tangent to the line at every point (compass needles align with it).
2. Field strength is proportional to line density (closer lines = stronger field).
3. Lines never cross (field is single-valued).
4. Lines are continuous closed loops with no endpoints — they pass through the magnet's interior, from north to south externally.

**Distinguishing feature:** Unlike electric field lines, which begin/end on charges, magnetic field lines form closed loops because magnetic monopoles do not exist.

**Configurations:** bar magnet (out of N, into S); current loop (same dipolar pattern); straight current-carrying wire (concentric circles; right-hand rule: thumb along current, fingers curl with field).

## 22.4 Magnetic Field Strength: Force on a Moving Charge

**Objectives:** describe how fields affect charged-particle motion; apply RHR-1; compute force magnitude.

**Terms:**
- Lorentz force — electromagnetic force on a charged particle moving through a magnetic field.
- Tesla (T) — SI unit of field strength; field exerting 1 N on 1 C moving at 1 m/s perpendicular to it.
- Gauss (G) — 1 G = 10⁻⁴ T (Earth surface ≈ 0.5 G; strong permanent magnets ≈ 2 T).

**Principles:**
- Magnetic forces act only on moving charges; stationary charges feel no magnetic force.
- All magnetism originates from charge motion; fields exert forces on other moving charges.

**Equations:**
- Force magnitude: F = qvB sin θ
- Field strength (rearranged): B = F / (qv sin θ)
- Unit equivalence: 1 T = 1 N / (1 A·m) = 1 N / (1 C·m/s)

**RHR-1:** thumb = velocity, fingers = field, palm pushes in force direction (positive charge; reverse for negative). Force is perpendicular to both v and B.

**Worked example (concept):** glass rod, 20 nC moving west at 10 m/s through Earth's northward field (5×10⁻⁵ T) gives F ≈ 10⁻¹¹ N — shows why magnetic force on macroscopic objects is negligible while still aligning submicroscopic particles.

**Applications:** Earth's field strongly affects electrons/subatomic particles; compass operation.

## 22.5 Force on a Moving Charge: Examples and Applications

**Objectives:** characterize charged-particle motion in fields; find the radius of curvature.

**Terms:**
- Magnetic force is perpendicular to velocity, deflecting trajectory without changing speed.
- Centripetal supply — the magnetic force provides centripetal force for circular motion.
- Spiral/helical motion — when v has parallel and perpendicular components to B, particles trace helices along field lines.

**Principles:**
1. Perpendicular force does no work; kinetic energy is conserved.
2. Perpendicular entry into a uniform field gives uniform circular motion.
3. Particles spiral along (are trapped by) field lines where field strength varies.

**Equations:**
- Force = centripetal force: qvB = mv²/r
- Radius of curvature: r = mv / (qB)

**Worked example (concept):** electron at 6.00×10⁷ m/s in B = 0.500 T gives r ≈ 0.683 mm — shows how permanent magnets badly distort CRT images via tight circular motion.

**Applications:** auroras (cosmic rays follow Earth's field lines, ionizing air near poles); Van Allen radiation belts; particle-accelerator beam containment; tokamak plasma confinement; microwave amplifiers; mass spectrometry.

## 22.6 The Hall Effect

**Objectives:** understand the Hall effect mechanism; calculate the voltage across a current-carrying conductor in a field.

**Terms:**
- Hall effect — voltage appearing perpendicular to both current and applied field in a conductor (Edwin Hall, 1879).
- Hall emf (ε) — emf across the conductor's width from magnetically-induced charge separation.
- Charge-carrier identification — Hall-voltage polarity reveals sign of the current carriers.

**Mechanism:** magnetic force pushes mobile charges to one edge; the accumulated charge sets up an electric field opposing further deflection; equilibrium balances electric and magnetic forces, producing a measurable transverse voltage. Demonstrates that electrons carry current in metals while positive carriers dominate some semiconductors.

**Equations:**
- Force balance: qE = qvB
- Field relation: E = vB
- Hall voltage (B, v, l mutually perpendicular): ε = Blv

**Worked example (concept):** Hall probe on an artery, B = 0.100 T, diameter 4.00 mm, v = 20.0 cm/s gives ε = 80.0 μV — small signal needing AC amplification; converts physical flow parameters into a measurable voltage.

**Applications:** calibrated Hall probes for field measurement; conducting-fluid flow detection (blood, seawater); material characterization (carrier type, density, drift velocity); the quantum Hall effect (1980).

## 22.7 Magnetic Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor

**Objectives:** explain why fields exert force on current-carrying wires; compute force magnitude and direction.

**Terms:**
- Drift velocity (v_d) — average carrier speed under the driving electric field.
- Current relation: I = nqAv_d (n = carrier density, q = charge, A = cross-section).

**Principle:** confined charges cannot escape, so the magnetic force on each moving carrier is transmitted to the conductor itself; total force is the sum over all carriers in the field.

**Equations:**
- Force on one carrier: F = qv_d B sin θ
- Force on wire segment length l: F = IlB sin θ
- Force per unit length: F/l = IB sin θ (θ = angle between current and field)

**Direction:** RHR-1 (thumb = current, fingers = field, palm pushes = force).

**Worked example (concept):** 5.00 cm wire, B = 1.50 T, I = 20.0 A, θ = 90° gives F = 1.50 N — substantial force even in a short wire in a strong field.

**Applications:** electric motors; magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) pumping of corrosive/hot liquids (liquid sodium in reactors, experimental artificial hearts); proposed MHD submarine propulsion.

## 22.8 Torque on a Current Loop: Motors and Meters

**Objectives:** explain motors and meters via magnetic torque on current loops; calculate the torque.

**Terms:**
- Torque (τ) — rotational force at a distance from a pivot; τ = rF sin θ.
- Current loop — rectangular coil carrying current in a uniform field.
- Brushes — automatic switches that reverse current to sustain continuous rotation.

**Equations:**
- τ = wF sin θ (w = loop width)
- τ = wIlB sin θ (l = side length)
- Multi-turn loop: τ = NIAB sin θ (N turns, A = area, θ = angle between loop normal and B)

**Principles:** forces on segments parallel to the axis give no torque; opposite vertical segments give equal-and-opposite forces creating rotation. Max torque at θ = 90°, zero at θ = 0°; torque reverses past θ = 0° unless brushes reverse the current.

**Worked example (concept):** 100-turn square loop (10.0 cm sides), I = 15.0 A, B = 2.00 T gives τ_max = NIAB = 30.0 N·m — practical motor torques can be large.

**Applications:** electric motors (electrical → mechanical energy); analog meters (fuel gauges, ammeters using shaped magnets for proportional deflection); brushes for unidirectional rotation.

## 22.9 Magnetic Fields Produced by Currents: Ampère's Law

**Objectives:** find the current needed for a given field strength; apply RHR-2.

**Terms:**
- RHR-2 — thumb along current, fingers curl in the direction of the field loops.
- Permeability of free space μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A.
- Solenoid — tightly wound coil with a uniform interior field and minimal external field.

**Laws:**
- Biot–Savart law — each current segment contributes a field like a long straight wire; total field is the vector sum.
- Ampère's law — general relation between field distribution and current; one of Maxwell's equations.

**Equations:**
- Long straight wire: B = μ₀I / (2πr)
- Center of circular loop: B = μ₀I / (2R); N loops: B = Nμ₀I / (2R)
- Solenoid interior: B = μ₀nI (n = N/l, turns per unit length)

**Worked examples (concepts):** ~25 A produces a field comparable to Earth's at a measurable distance (ordinary currents have significant magnetic effects); a solenoid reaching 2.01 T (MRI-grade) requires very large currents — highlighting electromagnet design constraints.

**Applications:** power-line compass interference; MRI solenoids with superconducting coils; toroidal coils for tokamak plasma confinement; ferromagnetic magnetic shielding.

## 22.10 Magnetic Force Between Two Parallel Conductors

**Objectives:** explain forces between current-carrying wires; compute the force between parallel conductors.

**Terms:**
- Pinch effect — mutual attraction of parallel currents that concentrates current into a narrow region (arcs, plasmas, solar phenomena).
- Operational definition — a definition grounded in a measurable physical phenomenon.

**Laws:**
- Ampere (official definition): two infinite parallel conductors 1 m apart in vacuum, each carrying 1 A, exert exactly 2×10⁻⁷ N/m on each other.
- Same-direction currents attract; opposite-direction currents repel.

**Equations:**
- Field from wire 1: B₁ = μ₀I₁ / (2πr)
- Force on wire 2: F₂ = I₂lB₁
- Force per unit length: F/l = μ₀I₁I₂ / (2πr)

**Worked example (concept):** compute the field from one wire, apply the force equation for the second wire's current in that field, derive force-per-length.

**Applications:** circuit-breaker arc behavior; solar jets/flares in plasma; most accurate practical method for defining/measuring the ampere and coulomb.

## 22.11 More Applications of Magnetism

**Objectives:** describe practical applications of magnetism.

**Terms:**
- Mass spectrometer — measures ion masses from their magnetic-field trajectories.
- Velocity selector — crossed (perpendicular) E and B fields pass only one specific velocity.
- Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) — nuclei in a field absorb/reemit specific radio frequencies set by nucleus type and chemical environment.
- MRI — non-invasive imaging based on NMR.
- Cathode ray tube (CRT) — accelerates electrons, steers the beam with magnetic coils.
- MCG / MEG — non-contact recordings of heart and brain magnetic fields.
- SQUID — superconducting quantum interference device; detects fields thousands of times weaker than Earth's.

**Principles:** charged particles move in circles when perpendicular to a uniform field; velocity-selector force balance qE = qvB; nuclear-spin alignment into quantized energy states; resonance absorption/reemission at characteristic frequencies.

**Equations:**
- Radius of circular path: r = mv / (qB)
- Velocity selector: v = E / B

**Worked examples (concepts):** mass spectrometry — with v, q, B fixed, radius is proportional to mass, enabling mass-to-charge identification; velocity selector — straight-line motion only when E and B forces balance; MRI — spatially varying field makes resonant frequency position-dependent for tissue mapping.

**Applications:** chemical/biological identification by mass-to-charge ratio (proteins, viruses); isotope concentration measurement; organ imaging, tumor/stroke/joint detection; functional MRI (fMRI) brain mapping; MCG/MEG diagnostics; CRTs in TVs, monitors, x-ray machines, accelerators.
