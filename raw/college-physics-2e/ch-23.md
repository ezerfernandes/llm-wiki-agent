## Chapter Introduction

Electromagnetic induction is the process whereby a changing magnetic field gives rise to an electric current — the mirror image of Oersted's discovery that currents create magnetic fields. This symmetry between electricity and magnetism underpins generators, transformers, and most AC electrical technology. Wind turbines, guitar pickups, security gates, and instrument damping all rely on induction. The chapter develops Faraday's and Lenz's laws, motional emf, AC generators, back emf, transformers, electrical-safety devices, inductance, RL circuits, reactance, and RLC series circuits with resonance.

## 23.1 Induced Emf and Magnetic Flux

**Objectives:** compute magnetic flux through a loop at any orientation; describe how to generate emf with magnetic fields and coils.

**Definitions**
- *Electromagnetic induction*: a changing magnetic flux generates an emf, whether or not a closed conducting path exists.
- *Magnetic flux (Φ)*: quantifies the magnetic field passing through a surface; depends on field strength, area, and orientation.
- *Galvanometer*: instrument that detects/measures induced current.

**Principles**
- Faraday's core finding: any *change* in magnetic flux induces an emf. Static fields produce nothing.
- Only *relative* motion between magnet and coil matters; faster relative motion → larger emf.

**Equations**
- `Φ = B A cos θ` (units T·m²), with θ the angle between B and the surface normal.
- `Φ = B⊥ A` (perpendicular field component form).

**Worked-example concepts**
- Faraday's iron-ring experiment: opening/closing a primary-coil switch changes B and induces opposite-direction currents in a secondary coil — shows that flux change is required.
- Magnet moved through a coil: opposite motions produce opposite currents; a stationary magnet induces nothing.
- Rotating coil: continuous rotation varies θ, changing flux smoothly and producing alternating emf.

**Applications:** generators, motors (same structure, reverse function), EM field detection.

## 23.2 Faraday's Law of Induction: Lenz's Law

**Objectives:** apply Faraday's law to find induced emf/current/field; explain Lenz's law.

**Principles**
- *Faraday's law*: induced emf is proportional to the rate of change of flux, the number of turns N, and inversely to the time interval.
- *Lenz's law*: the induced current/field opposes the flux change producing it; the minus sign expresses energy conservation (no perpetual free energy).

**Equations**
- `emf = −N (ΔΦ/Δt)` (with direction)
- `emf = N (ΔΦ/Δt)` (magnitude only)
- `ΔΦ = Δ(B A cos θ) = A Δ(B cos θ)`

**Lenz's-law problem strategy:** sketch directions; find external B; decide if flux is rising or falling; the induced field opposes that change; use RHR-2 for induced-current direction; mark emf polarity to match.

**Worked-example concept**
- Example 23.1 (induced emf): rapidly changing fields induce measurable voltage even in simple coils; the ~22.6 mV result shows real applications need stronger magnets, more turns, or faster changes.

**Applications:** magnetic tape/audio-video recording and playback; hard-drive storage (modern reads via giant magnetoresistance); credit-card magnetic stripes; cochlear implants; transcranial magnetic stimulation; infant sleep-apnea monitors (breathing changes coil area → changing induced current).

## 23.3 Motional Emf

**Objectives:** compute emf, force, field, and work from motion of a conductor in a field.

**Definitions**
- *Motional emf*: voltage induced when a conductor moves through a (stationary) magnetic field.
- *Hall effect*: charge separation from magnetic force on moving charges, giving a transverse voltage.

**Principles:** Faraday's law applies whether field or conductor moves (relative motion). Lenz's law fixes direction. RHR-1 gives polarity of separated charge; RHR-2 gives current direction.

**Equations**
- `emf = N (ΔΦ/Δt)` (magnitude)
- `emf = B ℓ v` (rod of length ℓ moving at v perpendicular to B)
- `F = q v B sin θ` (force on moving charge)
- `F = I ℓ B sin θ` (force on current-carrying conductor)

**Worked-example concept**
- Example 23.2 (tethered satellite): same `B ℓ v` principle scales from microvolts at the surface to kilovolts in orbit because v is enormous.

**Applications:** Hall-effect field/velocity measurement; Tethered Satellite experiments (1992, 1996) converting orbital KE to electrical energy via Earth's field; demonstrates a moving magnetic field produces an electric field (electromagnetic unification).

## 23.4 Eddy Currents and Magnetic Damping

**Objectives:** find magnitude/direction of eddy currents and their effects; describe magnetic-damping applications.

**Definitions**
- *Eddy current*: circulating current loop induced in a conductor by relative motion of conductor and field.
- *Magnetic damping*: drag force from eddy currents that opposes the motion creating them; proportional to velocity and zero at rest.

**Principles:** Faraday's law (changing flux induces current) plus Lenz's law (eddy currents oppose the flux change → retarding force). RHR-1 gives the force direction on the induced current.

**Equations:** none given; relationships described qualitatively.

**Illustrated concepts**
- Solid plate entering/leaving a field: counterclockwise loop on entry, clockwise on exit; both forces oppose motion.
- Slotted conductor: slots shrink loops; adjacent loops' opposing forces cancel, sharply reducing damping.
- Insulators: negligible eddy currents, essentially no damping.

**Applications:** damped laboratory balances; magnetic metal separation in recycling; metal detectors (eddy currents alter pickup-coil induction); eddy-current braking on trains/coasters (copper fins through magnets; needs supplementary friction brake to fully stop); induction cooktops heat ferromagnetic cookware.

## 23.5 Electric Generators

**Objectives:** compute generator emf and peak emf for a configuration.

**Definitions**
- *Electric generator*: produces emf by rotating a conducting coil in a magnetic field.
- *Commutator*: split-ring that reverses output connections to produce pulsed DC.

**Equations**
- `emf = −N (ΔΦ/Δt)` (average, Faraday's law)
- `ΔΦ = A B Δ(cos θ)`
- `emf(t) = N A B ω sin(ωt)` (instantaneous, sinusoidal)
- `emf₀ = N A B ω` (peak emf)
- `ω = Δθ/Δt`; `f = ω/(2π)`; `T = 1/f = 2π/ω`

**Worked-example concepts**
- Example 23.3 (average emf): a quarter-turn rotation gives household-scale voltage (~131 V average).
- Example 23.4 (peak emf): peak exceeds average; output is proportional to rotation speed.

**Applications:** AC power generation (sinusoidal output); bicycle generators (faster pedaling → brighter light); hydroelectric/steam turbines; motor↔generator equivalence; split-ring commutator for pulsed DC.

## 23.6 Back Emf

**Objectives:** explain back emf and how it arises in rotating motor coils.

**Definitions**
- *Back emf*: self-generated emf from a motor's rotating coil; opposes the applied driving voltage (Lenz's law).
- *Motor–generator equivalence*: identical construction; differ only in energy-conversion direction.

**Equations**
- `ε_back ∝ ω`
- `V_coil = V_applied − ε_back`
- `I = V_coil / R = (V_applied − ε_back)/R`
- `P = I² R`

**Worked-example concept**
- Example (48.0 V motor, 0.400 Ω): at startup ε_back = 0 → huge current (~120 A) and heat (~5.76 kW); at running speed ε_back = 40 V → modest current (~20 A) and heat (~160 W).

**Applications:** lights dim when large motors start (inrush current); motors slowing under load lose back emf and gain torque/current; sustained low-speed operation can overheat coils; unloaded motors speed up until ε_back ≈ supply, minimizing draw.

## 23.7 Transformers

**Objectives:** explain transformer operation; relate voltage, current, and turns.

**Definitions:** primary coil (input), secondary coil (output), step-up (raises V, lowers I), step-down (lowers V, raises I), ferromagnetic core (concentrates flux between coils).

**Principles:** Faraday's law (changing flux in secondary induces output voltage); Lenz's law; power conservation in an ideal (lossless) transformer.

**Equations**
- `Vs/Vp = Ns/Np`
- `Vs = −Ns (ΔΦ/Δt)`, `Vp = −Np (ΔΦ/Δt)`
- `Pp = Ip Vp = Is Vs = Ps` (ideal)
- `Is/Ip = Np/Ns`

**Worked-example concepts**
- X-ray unit (step-up): large voltage gain needs many more secondary turns; current drops inversely.
- Battery charger (step-down): fewer secondary turns lower the voltage and raise current; verifies power conservation.

**Applications:** plug-in adapters; multi-stage power distribution (generation >10 kV → transmission 200–700 kV → delivery 120–480 V); high-current industrial windings; safety devices.

**Limitation:** transformers cannot change DC voltage — they require continuously changing flux, which AC provides and DC does not.

## 23.8 Electrical Safety: Systems and Devices

**Objectives:** explain modern circuit-safety features, emphasizing induction-based protection.

**Definitions:** thermal hazard (overheating); shock hazard (current through the body); circuit breaker/fuse (interrupts overcurrent); three-wire system (live/hot, neutral, ground); earth/ground (low-resistance 0 V path); live/hot wire (supplies V and I); neutral wire (grounded return); ground/earth wire (bonds appliance case to 0 V); leakage current (induced on a case, not through the load); ground-fault interrupter (GFI); isolation transformer; doubly insulated (non-conductive case, safe with two-prong plug).

**Mechanisms**
- *Three-wire system*: grounds keep the case at 0 V and provide alternate return paths; protects against shock and thermal hazards.
- *Induction problem*: AC in an appliance induces emf on a metal case; grounding holds the case near 0 V.
- *GFI operation*: induction compares live vs. neutral currents through a sensing coil; equal currents cancel, unequal currents (leakage) trigger interruption. Standard threshold ~5 mA.
- *Isolation transformer*: equal-ratio transformer inserts high-resistance insulation between source and load, blocking a complete circuit through a user.

**Applications:** grounded household wiring with breakers; GFIs in kitchens/bathrooms (wet areas); medical microshock protection (~0.1 mA); doubly insulated power tools (asymmetric two-prong plug).

## 23.9 Inductance

**Objectives:** find an inductor's inductance from geometry; find stored magnetic energy; find induced voltage from changing current.

**Definitions:** inductance L (opposition to current change, henries H); mutual inductance M (one coil induces voltage in another, e.g., transformer); self-inductance (a coil's own changing current opposes itself, Lenz's law); inductor (device with significant self-inductance, coil symbol).

**Equations**
- `emf₂ = −M (ΔI₁/Δt)` (mutual)
- `emf = −L (ΔI/Δt)` (self)
- `L = N (ΔΦ/ΔI)` (definition)
- `L = μ₀ N² A / ℓ` (solenoid)
- `E_ind = ½ L I²` (stored energy)

**Worked-example concepts**
- Example 23.7 (solenoid L): inductance depends only on geometry/core, not current (~0.632 mH for 200-turn, 10 cm solenoid).
- Example 23.8 (stored energy): parallels capacitor energy; explains dangerous voltage spikes when current is interrupted suddenly (~0.284 J at 30 A).

**Applications:** roadway vehicle-detection loops (metal raises L); airport metal detectors; camera-flash voltage boosting via switched transformer; counterwound coils minimize mutual inductance to protect casings.

## 23.10 RL Circuits

**Objectives:** find current after a given time; find the time constant; sketch current vs. time.

**Definitions:** RL circuit (resistor + inductor in series); time constant τ (time to reach ~63.2% of final current on turn-on; current can't change instantaneously because the inductor's back emf opposes change).

**Equations**
- Turn-on: `I = I₀ (1 − e^(−t/τ))`
- Turn-off: `I = I₀ e^(−t/τ)`
- `τ = L/R` (seconds; 1 H = 1 Ω·s)
- `I₀ = V/R`

**Worked-example concept**
- Example 23.9: compute τ from L and R, then use exponential decay; each successive τ reduces the current to ~36.8% of the prior value.

**Applications:** switching transients, relay design, power-supply transient response, energy dissipation in inductive loads.

## 23.11 Reactance, Inductive and Capacitive

**Objectives:** graph V and I vs. time for inductive/capacitive/resistive circuits; compute reactances; find current/voltage.

**Definitions:** inductive reactance X_L (opposition from resisting current change); capacitive reactance X_C (opposition from charge storage/release); phase angle (time offset between V and I).

**Principles**
- Inductor: voltage *leads* current by 90° (current lags).
- Capacitor: voltage *lags* current by 90° (current leads).
- Resistor: V and I in phase (0°); resistance is frequency-independent.

**Equations**
- `X_L = 2π f L`; `I = V/X_L`
- `X_C = 1/(2π f C)`; `I = V/X_C`

**Worked-example concepts**
- Example 23.10 (X_L): a 3.00 mH inductor — reactance rises with frequency (~1.13 Ω at 60 Hz vs. ~188 Ω at 10 kHz); inductors impede high frequencies.
- Example 23.11 (X_C): a 5.00 μF capacitor — inverse with frequency (~531 Ω at 60 Hz vs. ~3.18 Ω at 10 kHz); capacitors impede low frequencies.

**Applications:** inductors block high-frequency noise; capacitors remove 60 Hz hum; frequency-selective filtering.

## 23.12 RLC Series AC Circuits

**Objectives:** find impedance, phase angle, resonant frequency, power, power factor, voltage, and current; sketch RLC diagrams; explain why resonance matters.

**Definitions:** impedance Z (total opposition; AC analog of resistance); reactance (frequency-dependent X_L, X_C); resonant frequency f₀ (X_L and X_C cancel → minimum Z, maximum I); power factor (cos φ; real-to-apparent power); phase angle φ.

**Principles:** Kirchhoff's loop rule; series current is the same throughout; resistor voltage in phase with I, inductor leads 90°, capacitor lags 90°. Mechanical-resonance analogy (forced oscillation at a natural frequency).

**Equations**
- `I₀ = V₀/Z`; `I_rms = V_rms/Z`
- `V₀ = √[V₀R² + (V₀L − V₀C)²]`
- `Z = √[R² + (X_L − X_C)²]`
- `f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC))`
- `cos φ = R/Z`
- `P_ave = I_rms V_rms cos φ`

**Worked-example concepts**
- Example 23.12: impedance varies with frequency — X_C dominates low, X_L dominates high.
- Example 23.13: at f₀ the reactances cancel, Z = R, and current peaks.
- Example 23.14: phase angle controls real power; far more power transfers at resonance (power factor = 1).

**Applications:** radio tuning via variable capacitor; LC frequency standards in digital watches; high-power-factor (efficient) motor operation near resonance; selective filter circuits.
