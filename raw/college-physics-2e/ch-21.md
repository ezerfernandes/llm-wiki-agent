## 21.0 Introduction to Circuits and DC Instruments

The chapter extends circuit analysis beyond the simplest single-resistor cases. It covers how resistors combine in series and parallel, how real voltage sources behave (emf vs. terminal voltage and internal resistance), systematic analysis of multi-loop networks via Kirchhoff's rules, how measuring instruments (voltmeters, ammeters, galvanometers) are built and connected, precision null-measurement techniques, and the transient behavior of resistor–capacitor (RC) circuits driven by DC.

Resistive results apply to both DC and AC. Capacitive behavior under DC is treated here as a transient (charging/discharging); AC behavior of capacitors is deferred to later chapters. Applications range from a flashlight to the wiring inside complex computers.

## 21.1 Resistors in Series and Parallel

### Objectives
- Draw circuit diagrams for series and parallel resistor arrangements.
- Apply Ohm's law to find voltage drops across resistors.
- Compute total (equivalent) resistance differently for series vs. parallel.
- Justify why parallel resistance is always less than the smallest resistor in the combination.
- Reduce mixed (combination) circuits to a single equivalent resistance.

### Terms
- **Series connection** — components arranged so the same current flows sequentially through each.
- **Parallel connection** — components each wired directly across the source, so each receives the full source voltage.
- **Voltage drop** — the loss of electrical potential energy (in volts) as current passes through a resistor.
- **Equivalent resistance** — a single resistance replacing a combination while keeping the circuit's overall behavior unchanged.

### Principles
- Conservation of charge and energy: charge entering equals charge leaving; energy supplied by the source equals energy dissipated in the resistors.
- In **series**, current is identical through every resistor; voltages add.
- In **parallel**, voltage is identical across every resistor; currents add.

### Equations
- Series total: `Rs = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...`
- Series voltage division: `V = V₁ + V₂ + V₃`, with `Vₙ = I Rₙ`
- Parallel total: `1/Rp = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + ...`
- Parallel current division: `I = I₁ + I₂ + I₃`, with `Iₙ = V/Rₙ`
- Power: `P = IV = I²R = V²/R`
- Power conservation: `P_source = P₁ + P₂ + P₃`

### Worked Examples (concept illustrated)
- **Ex 21.1 (series):** resistances add; voltage divides across components; total power dissipated equals power supplied (energy conservation check).
- **Ex 21.2 (parallel):** equivalent resistance from reciprocals is smaller than the smallest branch; current divides inversely with branch resistance.
- **Ex 21.3 (combination):** wire resistance reduces voltage delivered to a load when a high-current device switches on; solve by stepwise reduction of series/parallel sub-blocks.

### Applications
- Household wiring uses parallel branches so appliances run independently at full voltage.
- Long extension cords drop noticeable voltage at high current due to wire resistance.
- Car headlights dim when the starter motor engages (voltage drop across connecting wires).
- Series resistance can be added to limit dangerous currents.

## 21.2 Electromotive Force: Terminal Voltage

### Objectives
- Distinguish a source's emf from its terminal voltage.
- Analyze how internal resistance affects terminal voltage, delivered current, and power under load.
- Explain why parallel sources increase current capacity.

### Terms
- **Electromotive force (emf)** — the potential difference a source produces when delivering no current; measured in volts; despite the name it is not a force.
- **Internal resistance (r)** — opposition to current within the source itself, from its construction and chemistry.
- **Terminal voltage (V)** — the voltage measured across the source's terminals while current flows; below emf when loaded.

### Principles
A real source behaves as an ideal emf in series with internal resistance r. As batteries deplete or are heavily loaded, r grows, lowering terminal voltage and available current (a weak battery dims a flashlight).

### Equations
- Terminal voltage: `V = emf − I r`
- Loaded current: `I = emf / (R_load + r)`
- Power: `P = I² R`

### Worked Examples (concept illustrated)
- **Ex 21.4(a):** light load (large R relative to r) → terminal voltage stays near emf, so internal resistance has little effect.
- **Ex 21.4(b):** heavy load (small R) → large drop across r, so terminal voltage falls well below emf.
- **Ex 21.4(c):** power delivered computed from terminal voltage and current.
- **Ex 21.4(d):** degraded source where r equals the load → severe drop in output voltage and large internal power dissipation.

### Applications
- Battery testers apply a load and watch terminal voltage decline to gauge internal resistance.
- Series sources: emfs add but internal resistances also add (flashlight cells).
- Parallel identical sources: same emf, lower combined internal resistance, more current (paralleled starter batteries).
- Electric eels stack electroplaques (~0.15 V each) in series/parallel for ~600 V at ~1 A.
- Solar arrays combine ~0.5 V cells in series/parallel for desired voltage and current.

## 21.3 Kirchhoff's Rules

### Objectives
- Analyze complex multi-loop circuits that cannot be reduced by simple series/parallel combination.

### Terms
- **Junction (node) rule** — application of charge conservation at a junction.
- **Loop rule** — application of energy conservation around a closed path.

### Principles
- **Junction rule (conservation of charge):** the sum of currents into a junction equals the sum of currents out.
- **Loop rule (conservation of energy):** the algebraic sum of potential changes around any closed loop is zero.
- Current directions may be assigned arbitrarily; a wrong guess just yields a negative value, not a wrong magnitude.

### Equations
- Junction: `I₁ = I₂ + I₃`
- Loop: `Σ V = 0` around a closed loop.
- Sign conventions when traversing:
  - Resistor traversed with the current: `ΔV = −IR`
  - Resistor traversed against the current: `ΔV = +IR`
  - emf traversed − to +: `ΔV = +emf`
  - emf traversed + to −: `ΔV = −emf`
- Example loop equations: `−I₂(R₂ + r₁) + emf₁ − I₁R₁ = 0` and `I₁R₁ + I₃(R₃ + r₂) − emf₂ = 0`

### Worked Example (concept illustrated)
- **Ex 21.5:** a two-loop circuit with two sources. Apply the junction rule at one node and the loop rule to independent loops to obtain enough independent equations to solve for all unknown currents; sign of results validates the chosen current directions.

### Applications
Complex multi-source networks, mixed series/parallel topologies, and any circuit with multiple independent loops needing simultaneous equations.

## 21.4 DC Voltmeters and Ammeters

### Objectives
- Explain why voltmeters connect in parallel and ammeters in series.
- Convert a galvanometer into a voltmeter or ammeter and size the added resistor.
- Recognize that connecting a meter perturbs the circuit being measured.

### Terms
- **Voltmeter** — measures potential difference; connected in parallel with the element so it sees the same voltage.
- **Ammeter** — measures current; connected in series so the same current flows through it and the element.
- **Galvanometer** — needle instrument whose deflection is proportional to current via magnetic force.
- **Current sensitivity** — the current giving full-scale deflection of the galvanometer.
- **Shunt resistance** — a small resistor placed in parallel with the galvanometer so most current bypasses it (used to make an ammeter).

### Principles
- Parallel elements share the same voltage → parallel connection suits voltage measurement.
- Series elements share the same current → series connection suits current measurement.
- A voltmeter is a galvanometer plus a large series resistor; an ammeter is a galvanometer plus a small shunt.

### Equations
- Voltmeter total resistance: `R_tot = R + r = V / I`
- Series resistor for voltmeter: `R = R_tot − r`
- Shunt for ammeter: `R = r · (I_G / I)`
  - where r = galvanometer resistance, I_G = galvanometer full-scale current, I = desired full-scale current.

### Worked Examples (concept illustrated)
- **Galvanometer → voltmeter:** a 25 Ω, 50 µA galvanometer needs ~200 kΩ in series for 10 V full scale; the large series resistor sets the voltage range.
- **Galvanometer → ammeter:** the same galvanometer needs a ~1.25×10⁻³ Ω shunt for 1.0 A full scale; the tiny shunt diverts most current.

### Applications & considerations
- Connecting a meter always alters the circuit: a parallel voltmeter lowers effective resistance; a series ammeter adds resistance.
- Ideal voltmeter: very high resistance; ideal ammeter: near-zero resistance. Higher galvanometer sensitivity makes better meters.
- Car dashboard gauges act as voltmeters reading variable-resistance senders.
- Digital meters draw less current than analog, perturbing the circuit less.

## 21.5 Null Measurements

### Objectives
- Explain why null methods are more accurate than ordinary voltmeters/ammeters.
- Describe how a Wheatstone bridge finds an unknown resistance.

### Terms
- **Null measurement** — balancing voltages so zero current flows through the detector, avoiding disturbance of the circuit.
- **Potentiometer** — null instrument that measures emf by comparison against a calibrated standard voltage.
- **Wheatstone bridge** — null circuit that finds an unknown resistance by balancing four resistive arms.

### Principles
Ordinary meters introduce error: voltmeters draw current, ammeters drop voltage. Null methods adjust the circuit until the detector reads zero current, removing meter-induced disturbance; ratio comparisons cancel unknowns (like circuit current).

### Equations
- Terminal voltage context: `V = emf − I r`
- Potentiometer comparison: `emf_x / emf_s = R_x / R_s`, so `emf_x = emf_s · (R_x / R_s)`
- Wheatstone balance: `I₁R₁ = I₂R₃` and `I₁R₂ = I₂R_x`
- Unknown resistance: `R_x = R₃ R₂ / R₁`

### Worked Examples (concept illustrated)
- **Potentiometer:** at the contact point where the galvanometer reads zero, the ratio of wire resistances gives the unknown emf with no need to know the current — ratios eliminate unknowns.
- **Wheatstone bridge:** adjust the variable resistor until the detector current is zero (equipotential balance); the unknown resistance follows from the three known resistances.

### Applications & limitations
- Measure battery emf without knowing internal resistance; find resistance to ~4 significant figures.
- Limits: detector can't reach exactly zero; uncertainty in standard resistances; wire/contact resistance; temperature drift; imprecision in wire-length ratios.

## 21.6 DC Circuits Containing Resistors and Capacitors (RC Circuits)

### Objectives
- Define the time constant and compute it from circuit values.
- Explain gradual battery fade and capacitor charging behavior.
- Understand timing circuits and compute strobe flash rates.

### Terms
- **RC circuit** — a circuit with a resistor and a charge-storing capacitor.
- **Time constant (τ)** — `τ = RC`, the characteristic charge/discharge time.
- **Capacitor voltage** — `V_C = Q/C`.

### Principles
On closing the switch, current charges an initially empty capacitor; as charge builds, repulsion of like charges opposes further current. The loop rule (potential changes sum to zero) governs the transient. Initial current `I₀ = emf/R`; smaller R charges faster. Growing battery internal resistance with age slows charging (camera-flash delay).

### Equations
- Charging: `V = emf · (1 − e^(−t/RC))`
- Time constant: `τ = RC`
- At `t = τ`: `V = 0.632 · emf` (63.2% of final)
- Discharging: `V = V₀ · e^(−t/RC)`
- At `t = τ` (discharge): `V = 0.368 · V₀` (36.8% of initial)

### Worked Examples (concept illustrated)
- **Strobe photography:** size the capacitor so the flash duration (τ) is short enough to freeze a fast object; relate distance and speed via `t = x/v`, then set `t = τ`.
- **Heart defibrillator:** compute τ and track exponential voltage decay over successive intervals — exponential decay in a medical device.

### Applications
- Flash photography (brief, intense pulses freeze motion).
- Intermittent windshield wipers (adjustable R sets timing).
- Battery-powered flashing novelty lights.
- Artificial pacemakers (timed pulses via RC time constant).
- Laser pumping by capacitor-discharge flash lamps.
