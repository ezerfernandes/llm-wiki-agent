## Chapter Introduction

The chapter examines the relationship between electric voltage and stored electrical energy, stressing that they are connected but distinct. Two batteries can share a voltage rating yet hold very different amounts of energy (a motorcycle battery vs. a car battery at the same 12 V). Applications span automated external defibrillators (AEDs), high-voltage power transmission (hundreds of thousands of volts), household 120 V outlets, and ion transport across cell membranes for biological signaling.

## 19.1 Electric Potential Energy: Potential Difference

**Objectives:** distinguish electric potential from electric potential energy; relate potential difference to changes in electric PE; use the electron volt; compute energy from charge and voltage.

**Terms**
- Electric potential energy (PE): energy a charge has due to its position in an electric field (joules).
- Electric potential (V): potential energy per unit charge — a field property independent of the test charge.
- Potential difference (ΔV, "voltage"): change in potential energy per unit charge between two points; always between two locations.
- Electron volt (eV): energy gained by one fundamental charge accelerated through 1 V.

**Principles**
- The electrostatic force is conservative, so work is path-independent and a potential energy can be defined (analogous to gravity).
- Work done by a conservative force equals the negative of the potential energy change.

**Equations**
- V = PE / q
- ΔV = V_B − V_A = ΔPE / q
- ΔPE = q ΔV
- 1 V = 1 J/C
- 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
- Energy conservation: KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f
- Speed from acceleration through V: v = √(2qV/m)

**Worked examples (concepts)**
- Battery energy delivery: voltage alone doesn't fix energy output; total charge moved matters (12 V motorcycle vs. car battery).
- Electron flow in a circuit: convert power and voltage into charge, then into a particle count.
- Kinetic energy gain: electric PE converts fully to KE as a free electron accelerates between plates.

**Applications:** electron guns (displays, analysis); particle acceleration to set energies; assessing whether a particle has enough eV to break molecular bonds; battery capacity vs. voltage.

## 19.2 Electric Potential in a Uniform Electric Field

**Objectives:** relate voltage to electric field strength; derive the relationship for uniform fields; find field strength from separation and potential difference.

**Terms**
- Voltage / potential difference: scalar, energy per unit charge (ΔV).
- Electric field E: vector, force per unit charge.
- Uniform field: constant field between parallel conducting plates.
- Gradient: rate of change of potential with distance.

**Principles:** voltage (energy view) and field (force view) are two faces of the same physics; in uniform fields the relationship is calculus-free. The field points toward decreasing potential.

**Equations**
- V_AB = E d  (uniform field, d = plate separation)
- E = V_AB / d
- General: E = −ΔV / Δs
- Unit equivalence: 1 N/C = 1 V/m
- F = qE

**Worked examples (concepts)**
- Max voltage between parallel plates: limited by air dielectric breakdown (~3.0 × 10⁶ V/m in dry air; lower in humid air).
- Electron gun field and force: compute field from energy and separation; force on a charge is uniform throughout the region.

**Applications:** transmission line design (avoid arcing); spark chambers; CRTs/electron guns; static hazard assessment.

## 19.3 Electrical Potential Due to a Point Charge

**Objectives:** describe point charges and write the potential expression; contrast potential and field; compute potential from charge and distance.

**Terms**
- Point charge: a fundamental particle or a spherical charge distribution that looks like a point source externally.
- Electric potential (V): scalar; work per unit charge to bring a test charge from infinity. No direction.
- Electric field (E): vector; combine multiple fields vectorially.

**Principles**
- Work–potential: W = −q ΔV.
- Potentials from multiple charges add algebraically (scalar). Fields add as vectors.
- Reference (zero) potential is conventionally at infinity (or Earth/ground).

**Equations**
- V = kQ / r  (point charge), k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²
- E = F/q = kQ / r²  (point charge)
- Potential falls off as 1/r; field as 1/r².

**Worked examples (concepts)**
- Metal sphere voltage (Ex. 19.6): uniform charge on a conducting sphere gives the same external potential as a point charge at its center; negative charge gives negative potential (attracts positive charges).
- Van de Graaff generator (Ex. 19.7): reverse the formula — find excess charge from a measured surface voltage, showing the point-charge model works for real conductors.

**Applications:** static electricity (nC–µC); voltages near conducting spheres; electrostatic generators; choosing the potential reference.

## 19.4 Equipotential Lines

**Objectives:** define equipotential lines/surfaces and relate them geometrically to E fields; explain grounding; distinguish field lines from equipotentials.

**Terms**
- Equipotential line/surface: locus of constant potential (lines in 2D, surfaces in 3D).
- Grounding: connecting a conductor to Earth, fixing it at 0 V relative to ground.

**Principles**
- Equipotential lines are always perpendicular to electric field lines.
- A conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is an equipotential surface (any internal potential difference would drive charge flow).
- No work is done moving a charge along an equipotential.

**Equations**
- V = kQ / r
- Work along equipotential: W = −ΔPE = −q ΔV = 0
- W = F d cos θ = q E d cos θ = 0 → perpendicularity follows from cos 90° = 0

**Illustrative cases**
- Single point charge: equipotentials are concentric spheres, perpendicular to radial field lines.
- Two opposite charges: equipotentials form closed contours, perpendicular to curved field lines.
- Parallel plates: equipotentials are uniformly spaced and parallel (constant field).

**Applications:** electrical safety via grounding metal housings; ECG/cardiac equipotential mapping to track heart rhythm.

## 19.5 Capacitors and Dielectrics

**Objectives:** describe capacitors and define capacitance; derive parallel-plate capacitance; explain how dielectrics increase capacitance; compute capacitance from V and Q.

**Terms**
- Capacitor: two conductors separated by an insulator; net neutral overall but stores separated +Q and −Q.
- Capacitance (C): stored charge per unit voltage. Unit: farad (F) = C/V.
- Dielectric: insulator between plates that raises capacitance and withstands higher fields than air.
- Dielectric constant (κ): factor by which polarization raises capacitance vs. vacuum.
- Dielectric strength: maximum field before the insulator breaks down and conducts.

**Principles**
- Field ∝ charge; voltage ∝ field; therefore charge ∝ voltage (Q = CV).
- Polarization: an external field separates molecular charge, creating an opposing internal field, lowering net field and increasing storage.
- Larger plate area and smaller separation both increase capacitance.

**Equations**
- Q = CV ; C = Q/V
- Parallel plate (vacuum/air): C = ε₀ A / d
- Parallel plate (dielectric): C = κ ε₀ A / d
- ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F/m
- E = V/d ; max voltage V = E·d

**Worked example (concept)**
- Ex. 19.8: large capacitance needs huge area or tiny separation; a 1 m² / 1 mm air capacitor is only ~9 nF, explaining why real capacitors use thin foils and dielectrics.

**Applications:** defibrillators; radio filtering/noise reduction; cell membranes (~70 mV across 7–10 nm ≈ 9 × 10⁶ V/m, near air breakdown); Teflon-filled capacitors tolerate ~60,000 V vs. ~3,000 V for air at the same gap, storing ~42× more charge.

## 19.6 Capacitors in Series and Parallel

**Objectives:** write total-capacitance expressions for series and parallel; identify series vs. parallel sub-networks; compute effective capacitance.

**Terms**
- Series: capacitors in a chain; same charge magnitude on all, voltage splits; behaves like a larger plate separation.
- Parallel: capacitors across the same voltage; same V on each; behaves like a larger plate area.

**Principles**
- Series total is always less than the smallest individual capacitor.
- Parallel total is always larger than any individual capacitor.
- Charge conservation: series plates carry equal-magnitude charge.

**Equations**
- Series: 1/C_S = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + 1/C₃ + …
- Parallel: C_P = C₁ + C₂ + C₃ + …
- C = Q/V

**Worked examples (concepts)**
- Ex. 19.9 (series): 1.0, 5.0, 8.0 µF in series → 0.755 µF, less than the smallest.
- Ex. 19.10 (mixed): reduce series sub-groups first, then add parallel elements.

**Applications:** building non-stock capacitance values; series for high-voltage division; parallel for greater charge storage.

## 19.7 Energy Stored in Capacitors

**Objectives:** name capacitor uses; relate stored energy to charge and voltage; explain defibrillators.

**Terms / concepts**
- Stored energy accumulates as charge builds up under voltage; capacitor voltage rises from 0 to its final value during charging.
- The average voltage seen by the total charge is half the final voltage — the source of the ½ factor.

**Equations**
- E_cap = QV/2 = CV²/2 = Q²/(2C)

**Worked example (concept)**
- Defibrillator capacitance: given stored energy and voltage, solve for the capacitance needed to deliver a therapeutic energy dose.

**Applications:** defibrillators (precise energy bursts); calculator memory backup during battery changes; camera flash energy storage.
