## 18.0 Introduction

Electrostatics studies the forces and fields produced by stationary (or slowly moving) electric charges. The chapter links large-scale electrical observations to the microscopic behavior of charge. Electromagnetic forces underlie nearly all everyday phenomena — friction, cohesion, adhesion, and most sensory experience — with gravity being the notable exception. Historical milestones include Franklin's kite experiment (connecting lightning to laboratory static electricity) and the Galvani–Volta work on electrical stimulation of tissue (leading to the battery and to understanding muscle contraction).

Core ideas of the chapter: two types of charge (positive/negative), conservation of charge, Coulomb's law, the electric field as force per unit charge, electric field lines, the superposition principle, conductor behavior in static equilibrium, and applications.

## 18.1 Static Electricity and Charge: Conservation of Charge

**Objectives:** define electric charge and the two charge types; describe the three common ways static charge arises; state conservation of charge.

**Terms:**
- **Electric charge** — a fundamental property of matter, in two types conventionally labeled positive and negative.
- **Coulomb (C)** — SI unit of charge.
- **Electron / proton** — primary charge carriers; the electron is negative, the proton positive, with equal magnitude.
- **Elementary charge** |q_e| — the smallest free charge observed.

**Charge interaction rules:** like charges repel; unlike charges attract; the force weakens with distance.

**Law of Conservation of Charge:** total charge is constant in any process — charge is neither created nor destroyed, only transferred/redistributed. In particle creation, oppositely-charged particles always appear together so net charge change is zero.

**Equations:**
- |q_e| = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
- Number of charges per coulomb: 1.00 C / (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) = 6.25 × 10¹⁸
- Mass-energy for pair creation: Δm = E / c²

**Worked-example concepts:**
- Charge separation by friction: rubbing transfers electrons from one material to another, leaving equal and opposite net charges — no charge created.
- Van de Graaff hair demo: like-charged hair strands repel and stand up.
- Pair creation: an electron and positron appear together, conserving total charge (zero).

**Applications:** plastic-wrap cling, dryer static cling, lightning, gasoline-pump grounding to prevent sparks, conductive booties/strips in operating rooms to prevent ignition of anesthetic gases.

## 18.2 Conductors and Insulators

**Objectives:** distinguish conductors and insulators; describe the three charging mechanisms; describe how electrostatic force changes with distance; explain charge separation in neutral materials.

**Terms:**
- **Conductor** — material whose charge carriers (electrons or ions) move freely (metals, salt water).
- **Free electrons** — electrons not bound to particular atoms; move through a conductor.
- **Ion** — atom/molecule with net charge.
- **Insulator** — material that resists charge movement; charges are bound and flow ~10²³ times slower than in conductors (glass, dry salt, pure water).
- **Polarization** — separation of charge within an object that remains overall neutral.

**Three charging mechanisms:**
1. **By contact (conduction):** touching a charged object to a neutral conductor transfers charge; in metals only electrons move.
2. **By induction (separation):** a nearby charged object rearranges charge in a neutral conductor without touching; if the conductor is split into pieces while the inducer is present, each piece keeps an opposite net charge and the inducer loses none.
3. **By induction with grounding:** the inducer polarizes the conductor; a ground wire (Earth = huge charge reservoir) lets charge flow in/out; break the ground connection before removing the inducer to leave a net charge opposite to the inducer.

**Principle — attraction of neutral objects:** both positive and negative charged objects attract neutral matter. The induced opposite charge is pulled nearer the inducer while the like charge is pushed away; because electrostatic force weakens with distance, the (closer) attraction beats the (farther) repulsion → net attraction. **Polar molecules** (e.g., water) have built-in charge separation and respond strongly to external charge.

## 18.3 Coulomb's Law

**Objectives:** state how electrostatic force varies with distance; compute the force between two point charges; compare electrostatic to gravitational force at subatomic and macroscopic scales.

**Terms:**
- **Electrostatic force** — attractive/repulsive force between charges; like repel, unlike attract; weakens with distance.
- **Point charges** — idealized charges of negligible size.

**Coulomb's Law (magnitude):**
- F = k |q₁ q₂| / r²
- k = Coulomb constant = 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²
- Force is directed along the line joining the charges (repulsive for like, attractive for unlike).
- Inverse-square dependence F ∝ 1/r², experimentally verified to ~1 part in 10¹⁶.

**Worked-example concept — hydrogen atom:** the electron–proton electrostatic attraction (r = 0.530 × 10⁻¹⁰ m) exceeds their gravitational attraction by ~2.27 × 10³⁹, showing gravity is negligible at subatomic scale.

**Applications:** electrostatic force dominates subatomic interactions; at macroscopic scale gravity governs because bulk matter is nearly neutral and Coulomb forces cancel.

## 18.4 Electric Field: Concept of a Field Revisited

**Objectives:** describe a force field and compute the field of a point charge; find the force on a test charge in a field; relate force on a test charge to field strength.

**Terms:**
- **Force field** — model of forces surrounding an object that act on distant objects (action at a distance).
- **Electric field** — a property of the source charge only; the force per unit positive test charge at a point. Independent of the test charge that measures it.
- **Test charge** — a charge small enough not to disturb the source distribution.

**Equations:**
- Field definition: E = F / q
- Field of a point charge: E = k |Q| / r²
- Force on a charge in a field: F = q E
- Units: E in N/C; k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C².

**Worked-example concepts:**
- Field strength of a point charge depends only on the source charge Q and distance r, not on the test charge.
- Force on a charge equals qE; sign of q sets direction (along E for positive, opposite for negative).

**Applications:** comb attracting paper, static cling; foundation for macroscopic charge interactions.

## 18.5 Electric Field Lines: Multiple Charges

**Objectives:** find net force/field on a test charge from several charges; draw field diagrams for single positive and (larger) negative charges; sketch lines between like and unlike charges.

**Terms:**
- **Electric field lines** — continuous curves mapping field direction (tangent) and relative strength (line density).
- Line closeness ∝ field strength.

**Rules for drawing field lines:**
1. Lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges (or go to/from infinity for an isolated charge).
2. Number of lines ∝ magnitude of charge.
3. Field strength ∝ line density (lines per unit perpendicular area).
4. Field direction = tangent to the line at a point.
5. Lines never cross (field is unique at each point).

**Superposition principle:** the net field of several charges is the vector sum of the individual fields.

**Equations:**
- E = k |Q| / r² (per charge)
- Resultant of perpendicular components: E_tot = √(E₁² + E₂²)
- Direction: θ = tan⁻¹(E₁ / E₂)

**Worked-example concept (Ex. 18.4):** add the field vectors of two charges via components and Pythagoras for a non-collinear arrangement.

**Observations:** between like charges the fields partly cancel (weaker between them; far field looks like one larger charge); between unlike charges the fields reinforce between them (far field looks like a single smaller charge / dipole).

## 18.6 Electric Forces in Biology

**Objectives:** describe water as a polar molecule; explain electrostatic screening in cells.

**Terms:**
- **Polar molecule** — unequal electron sharing creates separated + and − charge centers; water's oxygen pulls electrons from hydrogen, forming a dipole.
- **Dipole / dipole moment** — two equal and opposite charge centers; the moment measures the separation strength.
- **Electrostatic screening** — surrounding charges reduce field strength, turning the Coulomb force from long-range to effectively short-range.
- **DNA charge density** — about 2 q_e per 0.3 × 10⁻⁹ m along the strands.

**Principles:** Coulomb's law F ∝ 1/r² means DNA base pairs must stay close for attraction to hold strands. Water and cellular ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻) screen Coulomb interactions between biomolecules.

**Equation:** F ∝ 1/r².

**Applications:** electrostatic forces maintain the DNA double helix; ion movement across membranes drives nerve impulses; calcium-ion release after cell damage starts repair, and low-level electrical stimulation aids wound healing; microtubules may project electric fields despite screening.

## 18.7 Conductors and Electric Fields in Static Equilibrium

**Objectives:** list the three equilibrium properties of conductors; explain why the internal field is zero; describe Earth's atmospheric field; analyze fields around irregular conductors; explain lightning rods and the car-as-shield effect.

**Terms:**
- **Electrostatic equilibrium** — steady state after free charges redistribute so net force on them is zero.
- **Free charges** — mobile carriers (electrons in metals).
- **Polarization** — charge redistribution under an external field.
- **Ionosphere** — charged layer ~100 km up that maintains Earth's fair-weather field.

**Three properties of a conductor in static equilibrium:**
1. The electric field is zero inside the conductor.
2. The field just outside is perpendicular to the surface (any parallel component would push charges until it vanishes).
3. All excess charge resides on the outer surface.

External field of a charged conductor (far away) equals that of a point charge q at its center.

**Concepts illustrated:**
- Parallel-plate system: closely spaced oppositely charged plates give uniform surface charge and a uniform field between them.
- Field concentration at sharp points: charge density is highest where curvature is greatest; on flatter regions repulsion spreads charge more effectively.
- Earth's atmospheric field: fair weather ≈ 150 N/C pointing downward (positive ionosphere, negative surface); storms reverse and intensify it; air ionizes (breaks down) above ~3 × 10⁶ N/C.

**Applications:** lightning rods (pointed tips bleed off induced charge); smooth, large-radius high-voltage conductors minimize leakage; **Faraday cage** (metal enclosure → zero internal field, shields equipment); a metal car body shields occupants from lightning/downed lines.

## 18.8 Applications of Electrostatics

**Objectives:** describe practical electrostatic technologies.

**Terms:**
- **Photoconductor** — insulator in the dark, conductor in light (e.g., selenium).
- **Toner** — fine negatively charged powder for marking.
- **Xerography** — "dry writing" dry-copying via electrostatic attraction/repulsion.
- **Electrostatic precipitator** — uses opposite charges to capture airborne particles.

**Principles:** Coulomb force F = qE; excess charge moves to outer surfaces; strong fields ionize surrounding material; like repel / unlike attract.

**Applications in detail:**
- **Van de Graaff generator:** a pointed conductor sprays charge onto a moving insulating belt; an upper pointed conductor removes it onto a large sphere where it spreads to the outer surface; reaches ~15 MV before air ionization limits buildup. Used in nuclear-physics research.
- **Xerography (photocopying):** (1) corotron uniformly charges a grounded selenium-coated aluminum drum positive; (2) light reflected from the image makes lit areas of selenium conduct and lose charge while dark areas keep it (latent charge image); (3) negative toner sticks to the remaining positive regions; (4) paper charged more positively than the drum pulls toner off, then heat/pressure rollers fuse it. Selenium's light-dependent photoconductivity is the key.
- **Laser printer:** a scanning laser writes the charge image on the photoconducting drum for high resolution; later steps match xerography; onboard computing can rival the host.
- **Ink-jet printer:** nozzles emit fine droplets, droplets are charged, then deflected by charged plates onto exact spots; cyan/magenta/yellow/black jets make full color (additive), avoiding multiple drums/toners.
- **Electrostatic painting:** charged paint particles repel each other and are attracted to an oppositely charged surface; perpendicular surface fields give even coats, with extra buildup at corners/edges.
- **Electrostatic precipitator / air cleaner:** a first grid charges particles (smoke, dust, pollen); an oppositely charged grid collects them. Industrial units remove >99% of coal/oil-combustion particulates; home HVAC units cut pollutants/irritants.

**Problem-solving strategy:** (1) confirm static electricity applies; (2) identify charges, magnitudes, positions; (3) name the unknowns; (4) list givens, distinguishing force vs. field; (5) solve the equation or sketch field lines; (6) check units/magnitudes for reasonableness.
