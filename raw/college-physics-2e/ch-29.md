## 29.0 Introduction

Quantum mechanics is the branch of physics that handles objects too small to observe directly. Its central, classically alien idea is **quantization**: physical properties such as energy, electric charge, and angular momentum occur only in discrete, specific values rather than over a continuous range. The **correspondence principle** states that quantum descriptions reduce to classical predictions when applied to large, slow-moving bodies — quantum steps are simply too tiny to notice macroscopically. Key terms introduced: atom (nucleus + electrons in discrete shells), photon (a discrete packet of electromagnetic energy), and wave-particle duality (matter and radiation both show wave-like and particle-like behavior). Applications include electron microscopy and explaining atomic/molecular structure.

## 29.1 Quantization of Energy

**Objectives:** appreciate Planck's role in founding quantum mechanics; recognize that atomic spectra reveal quantized energy.

**Definitions:**
- *Quantization of energy* — a system can possess only certain discrete energy values; intermediate energies are forbidden.
- *Blackbody radiation* — EM emission from an ideal radiator (emissivity = 1) whose spectrum is fixed entirely by absolute temperature.
- *Atomic spectra / line spectrum* — discrete emission/absorption wavelengths from transitions between quantized levels, not a continuous band.

**Principles:**
- Planck (c. 1900) resolved the blackbody spectrum (the classical theory's "ultraviolet catastrophe") by assuming oscillating atoms can only absorb/emit radiation in discrete energy increments.
- Stefan-Boltzmann behavior: total radiated intensity ∝ T⁴; the spectral peak shifts to shorter wavelength as T rises.
- Correspondence principle: quantum granularity vanishes at macroscopic scale.

**Equations:**
- Oscillator energy: E = (n + 1/2)hf, with n = 0, 1, 2, 3, …
- Planck's constant: h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s
- Energy-level spacing: ΔE = hf

**Worked example (infrared):** For f = 10¹⁴ Hz, the energy step is ~0.4 eV — significant atomically, negligible macroscopically. Illustrates why quantization is hidden at everyday scales.

**Applications:** spectroscopy (material ID, temperature), quantum devices, the historical trigger of quantum mechanics.

## 29.2 The Photoelectric Effect

**Objectives:** describe a photoelectric experiment; compute max kinetic energy of ejected electrons from photon energy/wavelength.

**Definitions:**
- *Photoelectric effect* — EM radiation striking a material ejects electrons.
- *Binding energy (work function, BE)* — minimum energy to free an electron from a surface.
- *Threshold frequency (f₀)* — minimum frequency for emission; below it no electrons eject regardless of intensity.
- *Photoelectron* — an ejected electron.

**Principles (Einstein 1905, EM radiation is itself quantized):**
1. A threshold frequency exists.
2. Ejection is essentially instantaneous (no energy accumulation needed).
3. Intensity (more photons/area) raises the *number* of ejected electrons, not their energy.
4. Max KE is independent of intensity.
5. Energy conservation: photon energy splits between freeing the electron (BE) and its KE.

**Equations:**
- E = hf
- E = hc/λ
- KE_e = hf − BE  (max kinetic energy)
- BE = hf₀  (at threshold)

**Worked example (violet light on calcium):** A 420 nm photon (~2.96 eV) on calcium (BE 2.71 eV) yields photoelectrons of ~0.25 eV. Illustrates wavelength→photon-energy conversion and energy conservation; photon energy just exceeds threshold ⇒ low-energy electrons.

**Applications:** light meters / camera iris, solar cells, foundation of quantized EM radiation.

## 29.3 Photon Energies and the Electromagnetic Spectrum

**Objectives:** relate photon energy (J or eV) to frequency/wavelength; find photons/second from a monochromatic source of given power.

**Definitions:**
- *Photon* — quantum of EM radiation, energy set by frequency.
- *Ionizing radiation* — photon energy high enough to remove electrons (biologically damaging).
- *Bremsstrahlung* — "braking radiation" from decelerating charged particles.
- *Characteristic X-rays* — sharp peaks from anode-material atomic excitations.

**Principles:** photon energy ∝ frequency; energy conservation can equate initial and final energy forms; quantized levels mean absorption/emission only when photon energy matches a level gap.

**Equations:**
- E = hf;  E = hc/λ
- h = 4.14 × 10⁻¹⁵ eV·s;  hc = 1240 eV·nm (handy constants)
- X-ray production: hf = qV (max photon energy = electron kinetic energy)

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- X-ray photon energy: electrons through 50 kV → max 50 keV photons (electrical PE → photon energy).
- UV photon effects: ~100 nm UV (~12.4 eV) can ionize atoms / break bonds.
- Photon emission rate: a 100 W bulb emits ~10¹⁹ visible photons/s — huge photon counts in ordinary light.

**Applications by band:** gamma/X-ray (keV–MeV, penetrating, ionizing, imaging but hazardous); UV (4–300 eV, vitamin D, sterilization, DNA/dye damage); visible (1.63–3.26 eV, matches atomic transitions, single photons trigger retinal cells, color); IR/microwave (non-ionizing, absorbed by water, thermal imaging, food heating).

## 29.4 Photon Momentum

**Objectives:** relate photon momentum to energy and wavelength; apply momentum conservation to emission/absorption/reflection; describe wavelength shift in scattering and the Compton wavelength.

**Definitions:**
- *Photon* — massless quantum that nonetheless carries momentum and acts as a particle in collisions.
- *Compton effect / Compton scattering* — X-ray photons scatter off electrons and lose energy (wavelength increases); observed by Arthur H. Compton (~1923), confirming photons carry momentum.

**Principles:** momentum is conserved in classical, relativistic, and quantum domains, even for massless photons. Photons show both particle (momentum, collisions) and wave (λ, f) traits.

**Equations:**
- Photon momentum: p = h/λ
- Relativistic energy-momentum: E² = (pc)² + (mc²)²
- For m = 0: E = pc, hence p = E/c
- E = hc/λ = hf
- KE_e = ½mv² (nonrelativistic electron)

**Worked examples:**
- 29.5 Electron vs photon momentum: a 500 nm photon has p = 1.33 × 10⁻²⁷ kg·m/s; an electron with the same momentum moves ~1460 m/s yet the photon's energy (2.48 eV) dwarfs the electron's KE (6.02 × 10⁻⁶ eV) — ~5 orders of magnitude. Shows photon momentum is tiny by macroscopic standards.
- 29.6 Energy/momentum verification: computing p from E via p = E/c matches the wavelength formula, validating the relativistic relation.

**Applications:** comet tails (dust pushed away from Sun by reflected photons); solar sails (accumulated sunlight momentum); photon detectors (eyes, film, photomultipliers, CCDs) relying on particle-like interactions.

## 29.5 The Particle-Wave Duality

**Objectives:** define particle-wave duality; explain why it applies to EM radiation.

**Definition:** *Particle-wave duality* — EM radiation has both particle-like and wave-like properties, but a given observation reveals one set, never both at once.

**Principles:** wave nature shows interference/diffraction (definite λ, f); particle nature shows discrete localized interactions (quantized energy, momentum). Quote (paraphrased): observations yield either particle *or* wavelike properties, never both simultaneously. The symmetry question — does matter also have wave properties? — is answered yes (next sections), with profound implications.

**No equations.** Applications: double-slit interference, quantum measurement effects, groundwork for matter waves.

## 29.6 The Wave Nature of Matter

**Objectives:** explain the Davisson-Germer experiment; describe de Broglie's hypothesis linking particle and wave.

**Definitions:**
- *De Broglie wavelength* — wavelength associated with any particle.
- *Bragg reflection* — particles scatter from crystal planes at specific angles, producing diffraction-grating-like interference.
- *Davisson-Germer experiment* (1925–26) — electrons scattered from a crystal produced diffraction patterns, empirical proof of matter waves.

**Principles:** by nature's symmetry, if EM radiation has dual character, matter must too — de Broglie's 1923 thesis. Matter interferes when interacting with objects comparable to its (usually extremely small) wavelength; macroscopic objects show negligible interference.

**Equations:**
- de Broglie wavelength: λ = h/p
- Nonrelativistic: v = h/(mλ)
- Bragg condition: nλ = 2d sin θ (n = 1, 2, 3, …)
- KE = ½mv²

**Worked example:** an electron with λ = 0.167 nm — solve for velocity and KE (54.0 eV). Shows sub-nanometer electrons come from modest accelerating voltages, linking quantum wavelength to measurable quantities.

**Applications:** electron microscopes — TEM (electrons through thin sample, ~0.1 nm resolution, ~100 million× magnification) and SEM (surface scan, 3D, ~10× lower resolution, no thin specimen needed); crystal-structure analysis via Bragg reflection.

## 29.7 Probability: The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

**Objectives:** apply both forms of the uncertainty principle; analyze how measurement unavoidably disturbs a quantum system.

**Definitions/concepts:**
- *Probability distribution* — because matter is wavelike, single positions aren't predictable; repeated measurements form a statistical (diffraction-like) distribution showing where a particle is *likely* to be.
- *Measurement affects the system* — a position probe (photon/electron) transfers momentum unpredictably, degrading momentum knowledge.
- Trade-off: short probe wavelength → good position but worse momentum; long wavelength → reverse.

**Laws:**
- Position-momentum: Δx Δp ≥ h/(4π) (approximate intuition: Δx ≈ λ, Δp ≈ h/λ, ΔxΔp ≈ h)
- Energy-time: ΔE Δt ≥ h/(4π)
- Neither uncertainty can go to zero without the conjugate growing without bound.

**Worked examples:**
- 29.8 (position-momentum): localizing an electron to 0.01 nm forces velocity uncertainty ~5.79 × 10⁶ m/s; resulting KE (95.5 eV) exceeds atomic level spacings — orbits can't be tracked.
- 29.9 (energy-time): a 10⁻¹⁰ s excited state has tiny energy uncertainty (~3.3 µeV); a 10⁻²⁵ s particle has GeV-scale energy spread, affecting mass measurement.

**Applications:** atomic structure (electron clouds/orbitals, not orbits); particle physics (decay-energy spread, lifetime from energy width); virtual particles (brief ΔE for duration Δt); negligible for macroscopic bodies (Jupiter's momentum stays classical).

## 29.8 The Particle-Wave Duality Reviewed

**Objectives:** state particle-wave duality and its universal applicability.

**Definitions:** *Particle-wave duality* — all particles show both particle and wave character, manifesting differently by measurement context. *de Broglie relationship* — links wavelength to momentum for all particles regardless of mass.

**Principles:**
1. Universal duality: every particle, massless (photon) or massive (electron, atom, molecule), has wave properties.
2. Mass-dependent observability: massive macroscopic particles look like particles (wave part negligible); large numbers of massless quanta look like waves (particle part hard to see).
3. Scale: quantum effects dominate subatomically, classical behavior macroscopically.

**Equations:** λ = h/p;  p = h/λ.

**Worked example (photon recoil on dust):** a 550 nm photon (p ≈ 1.21 × 10⁻²⁷ kg·m/s) hits a 1.00 µg dust grain at rest → recoil ~1.21 × 10⁻¹⁸ m/s. Shows momentum conservation between a massless photon and a massive body.

**Applications:** comet tails (cumulative photon absorption), integrated-concept links to momentum/collisions, cross-disciplinary ties to dynamics, energy, fields, and wave optics.
