## Chapter Introduction: Further Applications of Newton's Laws

This chapter extends Newton's foundational principles to realistic force interactions that go beyond idealized scenarios.

### Learning Objectives
- Examine contact forces and resistance phenomena in mechanical systems.
- Analyze how objects move through fluids and gases.
- Understand material deformation under applied loads.
- Apply Newton's laws to complex, practical situations.

### Scope
Three primary phenomena get detailed treatment: friction and surface interactions; fluid resistance and terminal-velocity behavior; and material properties under deformation.

### Application Context
Hip-replacement design exemplifies how understanding force interactions improves prosthetic function — an artificial ball-and-socket joint must balance mobility with structural integrity using material-science principles. The chapter frames how engineering applies force principles to biological systems with specific geometric and material constraints.

## 5.1 Friction

### Learning Objectives
- Explain the general characteristics of friction and how it operates.
- Distinguish between the different types of friction.
- Compute magnitudes of both static and kinetic friction forces.

### Definitions & Terms
- **Friction**: a resistive force acting parallel to surfaces in contact, always opposing relative motion or attempted motion between those surfaces.
- **Kinetic friction**: friction occurring when two surfaces slide relative to one another.
- **Static friction**: friction present when objects remain stationary relative to each other; for identical surfaces it typically exceeds kinetic friction.

### Key Principles
- Friction acts parallel to the contact surfaces, perpendicular to the normal force.
- Static friction adapts responsively to the applied force until it reaches a maximum threshold.
- Once motion begins, kinetic friction requires less force than the maximum static friction.
- Friction magnitude depends on material properties and the normal force, not on contact area.
- At low nonzero speeds, kinetic friction is nearly independent of velocity.

### Equations
- Static friction magnitude: `f_s ≤ μ_s N`
- Maximum static friction: `f_s(max) = μ_s N`
- Kinetic friction magnitude: `f_k = μ_k N`
- On an inclined plane: `f_k = μ_k m g cos θ`
- Constant-velocity sliding on an incline: `μ_k = tan θ`

Where `μ_s` = static friction coefficient, `μ_k` = kinetic friction coefficient, `N` = normal-force magnitude.

### Worked Example Concepts
- **Skiing exercise**: shows how to compute friction coefficients from a known friction force by resolving the weight components perpendicular to the slope.

### Applications
- **Joint lubrication**: synovial fluid in knee and hip joints lowers friction coefficients to ~0.015, enabling smooth movement and supporting artificial replacements.
- **Medical imaging**: coupling gels in ultrasound reduce transducer-skin friction.
- **Biological lubrication**: saliva and mucus between organs ease movement during respiration and circulation.
- **Inclined-plane measurement**: objects sliding at constant velocity reveal the kinetic friction coefficient through the slope angle.

## 5.2 Drag Forces

### Learning Objectives
- Formulate drag force mathematically.
- Examine real-world applications of drag resistance.
- Characterize terminal velocity conceptually.
- Compute terminal velocity from a given mass.

### Definitions & Terms
- **Drag force**: a resistance experienced by objects moving through fluids (gases or liquids); always opposes the direction of motion.
- **Terminal velocity**: the constant speed reached when net force becomes zero — gravitational pull equals drag resistance, so acceleration ceases.
- **Drag coefficient (C)**: a dimensionless empirical value (often found via wind-tunnel testing) reflecting an object's aerodynamic properties.

### Laws & Principles
- For large objects at moderate-to-high velocities, drag is proportional to the square of velocity and depends on the object's shape, size, velocity, and fluid properties.
- At terminal velocity, gravitational force balances drag force, producing zero net acceleration.

### Equations
- Standard drag-force formula: `F_D = (1/2) C ρ A v²`
- Alternative form: `F_D = b v²`, where `b = 0.5 C ρ A`
- Terminal velocity: `v_t = sqrt( 2 m g / (ρ C A) )`
- Stokes' law (small particles, low velocity): `F_s = 6 π r η v`, where `r` = particle radius, `η` = fluid viscosity

Where `F_D` = drag force, `C` = drag coefficient, `ρ` = fluid density, `A` = cross-sectional area facing the fluid, `v` = velocity.

### Worked Example Concepts
- **Headfirst skydiver (75 kg)**: minimizing cross-sectional area through body orientation increases terminal velocity to ~98 m/s (~350 km/h).
- **Spread-eagle skydiver (85 kg)**: expanding surface area reduces terminal velocity to ~44 m/s despite greater mass, illustrating the dominance of the area term.

### Applications
- **Vehicle design**: aerodynamic shaping reduces fuel consumption; highway efficiency peaks around 45–50 mi/h.
- **Athletic equipment**: body suits, dimple redesigns, and hair removal collectively trim milliseconds in competition.
- **Animal biology**: terminal velocity explains why small animals survive falls unharmed (surface area scales more favorably than mass).
- **Microorganism movement**: bacteria use flagella to overcome viscous drag; sediment settling follows Stokes' law.
- **Evolutionary streamlining**: fish, dolphins, birds, and migratory species evolved streamlined forms to minimize drag.

### Take-Home Experiment Concept
- Nested coffee filters explore the relationship between mass and terminal velocity, testing empirically whether `v ∝ sqrt(m)` or `v² ∝ m` holds.

## 5.3 Elasticity: Stress and Strain

### Learning Objectives
- State and explain Hooke's law, including via graphical representations.
- Categorize three deformation types: longitudinal (tension/compression), transverse (shear), and volumetric (bulk).
- Apply Young's modulus, shear modulus, and bulk modulus with examples.
- Calculate length changes from given material parameters.

### Definitions & Terms
- **Deformation**: shape alteration resulting from an applied force.
- **Elastic deformation**: reversible shape change; the object returns to its original form when the force is removed.
- **Stress**: normalized force intensity, measured as force per unit area (N/m²).
- **Strain**: relative deformation; a dimensionless ratio of the change to the original dimension.
- **Tensile strength**: the breaking-stress threshold causing permanent deformation or fracture.
- **Elastic moduli**: material-dependent proportionality constants measuring resistance to deformation (Young's modulus Y, shear modulus S, bulk modulus B).

### Fundamental Laws & Principles
- **Hooke's law (linear region)**: applied force is proportional to the resulting deformation for small displacements — `F = k ΔL`, where `k` depends on material composition, shape, and force direction.
- **Stress-strain relationship**: `stress = Y × strain`, emphasizing material behavior independent of geometry.

### Equations
- Hooke's law: `F = k ΔL`; rearranged `ΔL = F / k`
- Longitudinal deformation (tension/compression): `ΔL = (1/Y)(F/A) L₀`; rearranged `F/A = Y (ΔL/L₀)`; and `k = Y A / L₀`
- Shear deformation: `Δx = (1/S)(F/A) L₀`
- Volumetric deformation (bulk compression): `ΔV = (1/B)(F/A) V₀`; rearranged `ΔV/V₀ = (1/B)(F/A)`

### Worked Example Concepts
- **Example 5.3 (suspension cable)**: applies the longitudinal-deformation equation to predict elastic elongation under maximum tension over a long span.
- **Example 5.4 (femur compression)**: computes compressive strain in a biological weight-bearing structure, showing bones change length minimally despite large loads.
- **Example 5.5 (nail deflection)**: an inverse problem — find the force magnitude from a measured transverse displacement using the shear-modulus relationship.
- **Example 5.6 (ocean pressure)**: volumetric-compression calculation showing liquids reduce volume measurably only under extreme pressure.

### Applications
- **Biological structures**: tendons show nonlinear stress-strain behavior with three phases (toe region of fiber alignment, linear fibril stretching, failure region of breakage); bones resist shear forces exceptionally well; arteries maintain blood flow through elastic recoil.
- **Engineering**: steel-reinforced concrete compensates for concrete's poor shear performance; guitar-string elongation varies by material and diameter.
- **Geophysical**: deep ocean water shows measurable compression; subsurface carbon undergoes crystalline restructuring under extreme confining pressure.
- **Medical**: spinal-disc shear forces grow with postural curvature, raising back-injury risk; elastic properties of tissues diminish with age.
