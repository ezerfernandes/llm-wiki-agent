## Introduction to One-Dimensional Kinematics

Kinematics is the study of motion without considering its causes. This chapter focuses on motion along a straight line (one dimension), building foundations before extending to two- and three-dimensional motion.

**Learning objectives:** describe and quantify one-dimensional motion; distinguish scalar vs. vector quantities; relate displacement, velocity, and acceleration; build mathematical models for constant-acceleration motion; apply kinematics to real situations such as falling objects.

**Key terms:** kinematics (from Greek "kinema," motion), displacement, velocity, speed, acceleration, one-dimensional motion, vectors vs. scalars.

**Principles:** coordinate systems are essential for quantitative description; the constant-acceleration model predicts future motion from initial conditions; position, velocity, and acceleration can be interpreted graphically.

**Applications:** straight-line bird flight (kestrels), space-probe travel time, predicting where a thrown football lands, blood circulation, molecular vibration.

## 2.1 Displacement

**Learning objectives:** define and distinguish position, displacement, distance, and distance traveled; explain how position and displacement relate within a reference frame; calculate displacement and distance.

**Definitions:**
- **Position:** an object's location relative to a chosen reference frame (Earth, building, airplane, etc.).
- **Displacement:** the change in position, a vector with magnitude and direction. Δx = x_f − x₀.
- **Distance:** the magnitude of displacement; scalar, no direction.
- **Distance traveled:** total path length covered, which can exceed displacement magnitude if direction changes.
- **Reference frame:** the coordinate system used to describe position.

**Principles:** the symbol Δ ("delta") means "change in." Displacement depends only on initial and final positions, not the path. Direction in 1D is shown with positive/negative signs; the choice of positive direction is arbitrary but must stay fixed.

**Equations:**
- Δx = x_f − x₀

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- Professor walking right while lecturing: displacement uses only initial and final positions (+2.0 m); positive sign = rightward.
- Airplane passenger moving toward the rear: negative displacement (−4.0 m); displacement magnitude can differ from distance traveled.
- Cyclist 3 km west then 2 km east: displacement (−1 km), distance traveled (5 km), and displacement magnitude (1 km) are distinct quantities.

**Applications:** rocket position relative to Earth; tracking movement within structures; passenger movement within vehicles; navigation.

## 2.2 Vectors, Scalars, and Coordinate Systems

**Learning objectives:** distinguish scalar and vector quantities with examples; set up coordinate systems for 1D motion.

**Definitions:**
- **Vector quantity:** has both magnitude and direction (e.g., displacement, velocity 90 km/h east, force 500 N downward). Drawn as arrows whose length is proportional to magnitude.
- **Scalar quantity:** magnitude only, no direction (temperature, energy, speed, height, distance). A scalar can be negative (e.g., −20 °C), where the minus sign marks a point on a scale, not a direction.

**Displacement vs. distance:** displacement is a vector (direction + magnitude); distance is a scalar (magnitude only).

**Coordinate-system conventions:**
- Horizontal: right = positive, left = negative.
- Vertical: up = positive, down = negative.
- Conventions are flexible, but once chosen they must be applied consistently throughout a problem.

**Worked example (concept):** Speed as a scalar — a person rounding a corner at constant speed has the same scalar speed even though direction changes, showing speed has magnitude only.

**Applications:** aircraft motion (direction vs. distance traveled); falling objects (sometimes downward chosen as positive); race analysis (choosing a positive direction matching runner motion).

## 2.3 Time, Velocity, and Speed

**Learning objectives:** relate instantaneous/average velocity, instantaneous/average speed, displacement, and time; compute these from position–time data; derive and interpret velocity–time graphs.

**Definitions:**
- **Time:** the interval over which change occurs; measured by observing a physical quantity change. SI unit: second (s).
- **Elapsed time (Δt):** Δt = t_f − t₀.
- **Velocity:** vector; displacement per unit time (has magnitude and direction).
- **Average velocity:** displacement over elapsed time.
- **Instantaneous velocity:** velocity at a specific instant (over an infinitesimal interval).
- **Speed:** scalar; distance traveled per unit time.
- **Average speed:** distance traveled / elapsed time (uses distance, not displacement).
- **Instantaneous speed:** magnitude of instantaneous velocity.

**Principles:** velocity is a vector because displacement is; speed has no direction. Average velocity hides intermediate detail. Average speed can exceed the magnitude of average velocity when the path reverses direction. Smaller time intervals reveal more motion detail.

**Equations:**
- Δt = t_f − t₀
- v̄ = Δx/Δt = (x_f − x₀)/(t_f − t₀)
- average speed = distance / time

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- Airplane passenger: average velocity with negative displacement (−4 m over 5 s = −0.8 m/s), showing direction.
- Round trip to a store: average velocity = 0 despite nonzero distance traveled; average speed = 12 km/h — distinguishing the two.

**Applications:** police use instantaneous velocity; trip planning uses average velocity; speedometers show instantaneous speed magnitude; motion graphs model real journeys.

## 2.4 Acceleration

**Learning objectives:** define and distinguish instantaneous, average, and negative acceleration; compute acceleration from initial/final velocities and time.

**Definitions:**
- **Average acceleration:** rate of change of velocity over time. SI unit: m/s².
- **Instantaneous acceleration:** acceleration at a specific instant (infinitesimal interval).
- **Deceleration:** acceleration directed opposite to velocity, which reduces speed.

**Critical distinction:** negative acceleration (pointing in the negative coordinate direction) is NOT the same as deceleration. An object moving left while accelerating right has positive acceleration but is decelerating; an object moving left while accelerating left has negative acceleration but is speeding up.

**Principles:** acceleration is a vector pointing in the direction of the *change* in velocity, not necessarily the direction of motion. Velocity can change in magnitude, direction, or both. Sign rule: when acceleration and velocity share the same sign, the object speeds up; opposite signs → it slows down.

**Equations:**
- a̅ = Δv/Δt = (v_f − v₀)/(t_f − t₀)

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- Racehorse from rest to final velocity: average acceleration using sign conventions.
- Subway train displacement: relating positive/negative displacement to directional choice.
- Train speeding up: unit conversion (km/h → m/s) before computing acceleration.
- Train slowing down: negative acceleration opposing positive velocity.
- Leftward motion decelerating: positive acceleration can decelerate leftward motion.

**Applications:** aircraft landing; train arrivals; general vehicle motion.

## 2.5 Motion Equations for Constant Acceleration in One Dimension

**Learning objectives:** find displacement for non-accelerating objects; find final velocity of accelerating objects; find displacement and final position with given initial conditions.

**Notation (taking t₀ = 0):** Δt = t; Δx = x − x₀; Δv = v − v₀; x₀, v₀ = initial position/velocity; x, v = final position/velocity.

**Central assumption:** acceleration is constant, so average and instantaneous acceleration are equal, and average velocity is the arithmetic mean of initial and final velocities.

**Core equations:**
- v̄ = (v₀ + v)/2
- x = x₀ + v̄t
- v = v₀ + at
- x = x₀ + v₀t + ½at²
- v² = v₀² + 2a(x − x₀)

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- Jogger at constant 4.00 m/s for 2 min: linear displacement–velocity–time relation.
- Landing airplane decelerating at 1.50 m/s²: negative acceleration reduces final velocity.
- Dragster from rest at 26.0 m/s²: displacement depends on the square of elapsed time.
- Braking distance, dry vs. wet concrete: lower deceleration greatly increases stopping distance; reaction time adds substantial distance.
- Merging car on a 200-m ramp: solving a quadratic for time, discarding the negative root.

**Applications:** vehicle safety (reaction time, wet vs. dry surfaces); aerospace (ICBM vs. Space Shuttle burn profiles); drag racing (quarter-mile); freeway on-ramp design.

## 2.6 Problem-Solving Basics for One-Dimensional Kinematics

**Learning objectives:** apply a systematic method to 1D kinematics problems; judge whether results are physically reasonable and find error sources.

**Key principle:** analytical and problem-solving methods transfer to new situations better than memorized facts.

**Six-step framework:**
1. **Identify physical principles** — sketch the scenario, set a positive direction, connect to underlying laws.
2. **List knowns** — including implicit knowns ("stopped" → v = 0; initial conditions often 0).
3. **Identify unknowns** — clarify what to find and the solution sequence.
4. **Select equations** — prefer those with a single unknown; use multiple if needed.
5. **Substitute and solve** — carry units; unit analysis catches errors (but correct units don't guarantee a correct number).
6. **Check reasonableness** — verify magnitude, sign, and units.

**Evaluating unreasonable results:** solve with the method, convert to intuitive units, then examine premises for inconsistency or unrealistic assumptions. Example: accelerating at 0.40 m/s² for 100 s gives 40 m/s (~89 mph) — unreasonable because sustaining that acceleration for 100 s is implausible.

## 2.7 Falling Objects

**Learning objectives:** characterize gravity's effect on moving objects; explain free-fall motion; find position and velocity in free fall.

**Definitions:**
- **Free fall:** motion under gravity alone, with air resistance and friction negligible. In a given location all objects then fall toward Earth's center with the same constant acceleration, independent of mass.
- **Acceleration due to gravity (g):** the constant downward gravitational acceleration, about 9.80 m/s² on Earth (varying 9.78–9.83 by location).

**Principles:** all objects fall with identical constant acceleration regardless of mass when air resistance is negligible (demonstrated by astronaut David Scott on the Moon, g ≈ 1.67 m/s²). Sign convention: with up positive, a = −g; with down positive, a = +g.

**Equations (up positive, a = −g):**
- v = v₀ − gt
- y = y₀ + v₀t − ½gt²
- v² = v₀² − 2g(y − y₀)

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- Rock thrown upward from a cliff: velocity reverses while acceleration stays constant.
- Rock thrown downward: same final speed at a given height as the upward case — speed depends only on initial speed and vertical displacement.
- Measuring local g from a measured fall distance and time (≈ 9.80 m/s²).

**Applications:** estimating mine-shaft depth by timing a dropped rock; reaction-time measurement with a falling ruler; precise local gravity determination; astronaut free-fall training ("Vomit Comet").

## 2.8 Graphical Analysis of One-Dimensional Motion

**Learning objectives:** interpret straight-line graphs via slope and intercept; get velocity from position–time graphs; get acceleration from velocity–time graphs; construct v–t graphs from x–t data and a–t graphs from v–t data.

**Key relationships:**
- Linear graph: y = mx + b, where m is slope (rise/run) and b is the y-intercept.
- Position–time: x = x₀ + v̄t → slope = average velocity, intercept = initial position x₀.
- Velocity–time: v = v₀ + at → slope = acceleration, intercept = initial velocity v₀.

**Equations:**
- y = mx + b
- x = x₀ + v̄t
- v = v₀ + at
- slope of x–t = Δx/Δt = v
- slope of v–t = Δv/Δt = a

**Interpretation:** tangent-line slope gives instantaneous velocity (from x–t curve) or instantaneous acceleration (from v–t curve). Zero acceleration → straight x–t line (constant velocity). Curved x–t → changing velocity; curved v–t → changing acceleration.

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- Jet car average velocity from widely separated x–t points.
- Instantaneous velocity from a tangent line at a specific time.
- Acceleration from the slope of a v–t graph.

**Applications:** jet-powered vehicle speed records (Bonneville Salt Flats); ship harbor navigation with varying deceleration phases.
