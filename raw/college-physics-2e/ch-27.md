## 27.0 Introduction to Wave Optics

Wave optics (also called physical optics) studies light behaviors that ray optics cannot explain — phenomena that arise specifically because light is a wave. These appear most strongly when light interacts with structures comparable in size to its wavelength: interference (bright/dark regions from superposed waves), diffraction (bending around edges and through openings), thin-film color (soap bubbles, oil slicks), polarization, and the resolution limits of optical instruments. Applications include non-reflective optical coatings, surface and thin-film analysis, enhanced microscopy, and resolution improvement in telescopes and microscopes.

## 27.1 The Wave Aspect of Light: Interference

**Terms:** electromagnetic waves (oscillating E and B fields; visible light is part of the spectrum); interference (waves combine constructively/destructively, most prominent when interacting with objects near their wavelength); index of refraction n; wavelength in a medium λₙ.

**Principles:**
- Wave-particle duality: light behaves ray-like with large objects but shows pronounced wave behavior (interference) with small structures.
- When light passes between media, frequency stays constant; speed and wavelength change. Color is set by frequency (unchanged across media), not wavelength.

**Equations:**
- `c = f λ` (vacuum), c = 3×10⁸ m/s
- `λₙ = λ / n`
- `v = c / n`

**Applications:** designing slits/gratings for spectroscopy; understanding color perception; laser/observatory work showing both ray and wave behavior.

## 27.2 Huygens's Principle: Diffraction

**Terms:** wavefront (leading edge of a wave, moves perpendicular to rays); wavelets (secondary spherical/semicircular waves from each wavefront point); diffraction (bending of waves around edges or through openings); ray (line perpendicular to wavefronts).

**Huygens's Principle:** Every point on a wavefront is a source of wavelets spreading forward at the wave speed; the new wavefront is the line tangent to all wavelets.

**Equation:** `s = v t` (distance traveled by wavelets).

**Concepts illustrated:**
- Reflection: wavelet construction yields reflected wavefront with angle of reflection = angle of incidence.
- Refraction: wavelets travel shorter distances in the slower (higher-n) medium, bending the wavefront toward the normal (basis of Snell's law).
- Diffraction through openings: bending is significant when the aperture is comparable to the wavelength.

**Applications:** explains why sound spreads through doorways while light casts sharp shadows (wavelength dependence); slit diffraction patterns confirm light's wave nature.

## 27.3 Young's Double Slit Experiment

**Terms:** coherent light (definite phase relationship); incoherent light (random phases); monochromatic (single wavelength); interference fringes (alternating bright/dark bands); order m (integer labeling a fringe).

**Principles:** superposition (amplitudes add algebraically depending on phase); path-length difference from the two slits determines constructive vs. destructive interference; diffraction spreads light into semicircular wavefronts after the slits.

**Equations:**
- Constructive (bright fringes): `d sin θ = m λ`, m = 0, ±1, ±2, …
- Destructive (dark fringes): `d sin θ = (m + ½) λ`, m = 0, ±1, ±2, …
- d = slit separation, θ = angle from incident beam, λ = wavelength, m = order.

**Worked examples:**
- Ex 27.1 — Wavelength determination: use measured fringe angle to compute an unknown wavelength (interference patterns measure wavelength).
- Ex 27.2 — Maximum order: sin θ ≤ 1 limits the number of observable fringes; depends on λ/d.

**Applications:** spectroscopy (intensity vs. wavelength); wavelength measurement; verification of light's wave nature.

## 27.4 Multiple Slit Diffraction (Diffraction Gratings)

**Terms:** diffraction grating (many equally spaced parallel slits, transmissive or reflective); iridescence (structural color from periodic microstructures acting as reflection gratings); order m.

**Principles:** constructive interference when path differences equal integer wavelengths; gratings produce narrower, brighter, better-separated maxima than a double slit — sharpness/resolution improves with more slits.

**Equations:**
- Grating equation: `d sin θ = m λ`, m = 0, ±1, ±2, …
- Screen position: `y = x tan θ` (x = grating-to-screen distance).

**Worked example:** "Typical Diffraction Grating Effects" — apply the grating equation to find diffraction angles for different visible wavelengths, then compute physical separation on a screen (spectral dispersion).

**Applications:** spectroscopy/monochromators; wavelength-optimized fiber optics; natural structural color (bird feathers).

## 27.5 Single Slit Diffraction

**Terms:** Huygens's principle (every point in the slit emits wavelets, initially in phase); central maximum (brightest, widest band at center); secondary/satellite maxima (dimmer, narrower bands either side); path-length difference (across the slit aperture).

**Principle:** rays from different points across the slit interfere; phase relationships at each angle produce maxima/minima. Narrow slits (near a wavelength) produce pronounced diffraction.

**Equation (destructive interference / minima):**
- `D sin θ = m λ`, m = ±1, ±2, ±3, … (D = slit width). Note: this gives the dark fringes; the central maximum is between the m = ±1 minima.

**Worked example:** Ex 27.4 — invert the minima equation: from a minimum's position and known wavelength, determine slit width; higher-order minima occupy progressively wider angular intervals.

**Applications:** measuring slit dimensions; understanding resolution limits; instrument and sensor design.

## 27.6 Limits of Resolution: The Rayleigh Criterion

**Terms:** resolution (smallest separation at which two sources appear distinct); Rayleigh criterion (two sources are just resolvable when the center of one diffraction pattern falls on the first minimum of the other); diffraction limit; numerical aperture NA = n sin α.

**Principles:** diffraction is an unavoidable consequence of light's wave nature and limits all optics; larger aperture diameter narrows diffraction spread and improves resolution; higher NA → finer resolvable detail and brighter images.

**Equations:**
- Angular resolution (circular aperture): `θ = 1.22 λ / D` (θ in radians, D = aperture diameter)
- Linear resolution: `x = 1.22 λ d / D` (d = object-to-lens distance)
- Microscope form: `x = 0.61 λ / (n · NA)`

**Worked example:** Hubble Space Telescope — a 2.40 m mirror at λ = 550 nm gives θ ≈ 2.80×10⁻⁷ rad, resolving sources ~0.56 light-years apart at the Andromeda distance; shows huge aperture/wavelength ratio yet diffraction persists.

**Applications:** telescope and microscope resolution limits; eye acuity (pupil aperture); laser/microwave beam divergence θ = 1.22 λ/D (larger beam → less spread); ties to the Heisenberg uncertainty principle as a fundamental limit.

## 27.7 Thin Film Interference

**Terms:** thin film (thickness t less than a few wavelengths); Newton's rings (concentric interference rings where two lens surfaces touch — used in precision optical grinding, one ring per wavelength of separation); non-reflective coating (film engineered for destructive interference of reflections).

**Principles:**
- Phase change on reflection: reflecting off a higher-index medium causes a 180° (λ/2) phase shift; reflecting off a lower-index medium causes none. The asymmetry between the two reflected rays determines constructive vs. destructive interference.
- The ray reflected from the film's bottom travels ~2t farther than the ray reflected from the top.

**Equations:**
- `λₙ = λ / n`
- Constructive: `2t = λₙ, 2λₙ, 3λₙ, …`
- Destructive: `2t = λₙ/2, 3λₙ/2, 5λₙ/2, …`
- (Conditions can swap with constructive/destructive depending on the phase-shift situation at each surface.)

**Worked examples:**
- Ex 27.6 — Non-reflective lens coating: thinnest MgF₂ (n = 1.38) film for destructive interference at 550 nm; t = (λ/n)/4 ≈ 99.6 nm.
- Ex 27.7 — Soap bubble thicknesses: for red 650 nm in soap (n = 1.333), several thicknesses give constructive (~122, 366, 610 nm) or destructive (~0, 244, 488 nm) interference; thickness variation creates bright/dark bands.

**Applications:** anti-reflective camera/lens coatings; Newton's rings for surface-precision testing; angle-dependent paints; anti-forgery features on cards/currency; iridescent insect wings.

## 27.8 Polarization

**Terms:** polarization (definite directional orientation of a wave's oscillation; for EM waves, along the E field); polarized vs. unpolarized light; polarizing filter (passes only the E-field component along its transmission axis); Brewster's angle θ_b (reflection angle giving complete polarization); optical activity (rotation of the polarization plane); birefringent crystals (split light into ordinary and extraordinary rays).

**Laws:**
- Malus's law: `I = I₀ cos²θ` (θ = angle between polarization and filter axis)
- Brewster's law: `tan θ_b = n₂ / n₁`

**Mechanisms:** reflection preferentially leaves horizontally polarized reflected light (vertical components preferentially refract); scattering produces partially polarized light perpendicular to the incident direction.

**Worked examples:**
- Ex 1 — Intensity reduction: angle (≈71.6°) needed to reduce transmitted intensity to 10% via Malus's law.
- Ex 2 — Brewster's angle: water (≈53.1°) and crown glass (≈56.7°); explains why polarizing sunglasses cut glare.

**Applications:** polarized sunglasses (block horizontally polarized glare); LCDs (voltage-controlled polarization rotation); photoelastic stress analysis through crossed polarizers; analytical chemistry (sugar/protein solutions); polarizing filters in sky photography; birefringent/dichroic materials.

## 27.9 Extended Topic: Microscopy Enhanced by the Wave Characteristics of Light

**Terms:** resolution limit (~one wavelength, from diffraction); contrast (object-vs-background intensity); coherent/monochromatic light; birefringent and optically active materials.

**Principles:** light's wave nature both limits resolution (cannot resolve below ~λ, tied to Heisenberg uncertainty) and enables contrast enhancement via interference, phase shifts, and polarization.

**Techniques:**
- UV microscopy — shorter wavelength → finer detail (but tissue hazard, special optics/detectors).
- Interference microscope — split into object and reference beams; recombination reveals phase-difference contrast.
- Phase-contrast microscopy — a phase plate makes object/background light interfere for contrast (Frits Zernike, Nobel 1953).
- Polarization microscopy — polarized light through birefringent/optically active specimens viewed through a crossed polarizer; gives color and high contrast.
- Confocal microscopy — pinholes reject out-of-focus light, yielding 3D images; uses laser-excited fluorescent labels.
- Spectral/scattering microscopy — frequency-shifted scattered light acts as a molecular fingerprint for chemical composition.

**Key concept:** the wave nature of light simultaneously caps resolution near one wavelength yet, through interference, provides the tools to enhance contrast beyond plain illumination.
