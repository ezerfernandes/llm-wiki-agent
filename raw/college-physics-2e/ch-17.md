## Chapter Introduction

Sound is examined as both a physical phenomenon and a perceptual experience. Sound exists as a wave disturbance in matter whether or not anyone perceives it; hearing is the biological interpretation of that physical stimulus. The chapter connects acoustic physics to practical applications, especially medical diagnostics and therapy.

Topics: sound fundamentals, propagation (speed/frequency/wavelength), intensity and perceived loudness, Doppler phenomena, wave interference and resonance in air columns, biological hearing, and ultrasonic applications.

---

## 17.1 Sound

**Objectives:** define sound and distinguish it from hearing; characterize sound as a longitudinal wave.

**Definitions:**
- **Sound** — a disturbance propagating outward from a source through matter. At atomic scale it is organized disruption beyond thermal motion, often periodic, with atoms executing simple harmonic motion.
- **Hearing** — sensory perception of sound waves (analogous to vision interpreting light).
- **Longitudinal wave** — sound consists of compressions (high-pressure zones) and rarefactions (low-pressure zones) traveling outward at the source's vibration frequency. Fluids support primarily longitudinal sound due to minimal shear strength.
- **Compressions / rarefactions** — alternating regions of increased/decreased local pressure created as vibrating matter displaces surrounding fluid.

**Principles:** Vibrating objects (e.g., strings) transfer energy to air. Most energy becomes heat via turbulence; a portion creates propagating pressure fluctuations. Energy dissipates by absorption (e.g., eardrums), air viscosity, heat exchange during compression-rarefaction cycles (entropy increase), and amplitude reduction as the wave spreads over expanding area.

**Applications:** ultrasound imaging/treatment; acoustic resonance shattering glass; eardrum vibration from pressure differences initiating neural conversion.

## 17.2 Speed of Sound, Frequency, and Wavelength

**Objectives:** explain pitch as the perceptual correlate of frequency; relate sound speed to frequency and wavelength; analyze medium and temperature effects.

**Definitions:**
- **Pitch** — perception corresponding to frequency.
- **Wavelength** — distance between adjacent identical wave portions (e.g., consecutive compressions).
- **Frequency** — wave cycles passing a point per unit time; set by the source.

**Principles:**
- Sound speed depends on the medium: rigidity (incompressibility) and density. More rigid, lower-density materials transmit faster. Air's compressibility makes it slow.
- Frequency independence: across the audible range (20–20,000 Hz), sound speed in a given medium is nearly constant regardless of pitch.

**Equations:**
- Wave relationship: `v_w = f·λ` (Eq. 17.1)
- Sound speed in air vs. temperature: `v_w = 331 m/s · √(T / 273 K)` (Eq. 17.2), T in kelvin
- RMS particle speed in a gas: `v_rms = √(3kT / m)` (Eq. 17.3); k = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K (Boltzmann constant), m = particle mass

**Worked example (17.1 — Audible Wavelengths):** computes min/max wavelengths for human hearing (20 Hz, 20,000 Hz) at 30°C. Illustrates the inverse frequency–wavelength relationship at constant speed — explains why large instruments give low pitches and small ones high pitches.

**Applications:** fireworks (light faster than sound); earthquake P-/S-wave timing to locate epicenters; bat echolocation; ultrasound imaging; instrument size vs. wavelength.

## 17.3 Sound Intensity and Sound Level

**Objectives:** define intensity, sound intensity, and sound pressure level; compute sound intensity level in decibels.

**Definitions:**
- **Intensity** — power per unit area carried by a wave (rate of energy transfer), W/m².
- **Sound intensity level (β)** — logarithmic comparison of intensity to a reference, not direct intensity.
- **Reference intensity (I₀)** — 10⁻¹² W/m², threshold of human hearing at 1000 Hz.
- **Pressure amplitude (Δp)** — half the max–min pressure difference of a sound wave, in pascals.
- **Decibel (dB)** — unit derived from the bel (named after Alexander Graham Bell); the intensity-ratio log × 10.

**Principles:** Perception is logarithmic — the ear responds to the log of intensity. Each tenfold intensity increase corresponds to +10 dB.

**Equations:**
- Intensity: `I = P / A` (P = power, A = area)
- Intensity from pressure amplitude: `I = (Δp)² / (2·ρ·v_w)` (ρ = medium density, v_w = sound speed)
- Sound intensity level: `β(dB) = 10·log₁₀(I / I₀)`
- Intensity-ratio relation: `β₂ − β₁ = 10·log₁₀(I₂ / I₁)`

**Worked examples:**
- **17.2 — Decibels from pressure data:** 0.656 Pa amplitude in 0°C air → ~87 dB. Shows moderate sounds involve tiny physical pressures.
- **17.3 — Doubling intensity:** doubling intensity changes level by ~3 dB (3.01 dB). Demonstrates logarithmic compression of large intensity ratios.

**Applications:** occupational safety (85 dB / 8-hour limit; 120+ dB immediate damage); auditory research over the ~10¹² intensity range; environmental noise monitoring; acoustic engineering by ratios.

## 17.4 Doppler Effect and Sonic Booms

**Objectives:** characterize Doppler effect, Doppler shift, sonic boom; apply formulas for observed frequency under relative motion; explain phenomena from supersonic motion.

**Definitions:**
- **Doppler effect** — change in observed frequency when source or observer moves relative to the other.
- **Doppler shift** — the actual frequency change from relative motion.
- **Sonic boom** — constructive-interference shock wave when an object exceeds sound speed.
- **Bow wake** — V/cone-shaped disturbance when source speed exceeds wave speed in the medium.

**Biographical (Christian Doppler):** Christian Johann Doppler (1803–1853), Austrian physicist and mathematician, experimented with stationary and moving musical sources/observers (including musicians on moving trains) to measure frequency changes.

**Principles:** A moving source bunches compressions ahead (shorter λ, higher f) and spreads them behind (longer λ, lower f). An observer moving toward a source intercepts crests more often; moving away, less often. Because sound travels at constant speed in a medium regardless of source motion, `v_w = f·λ` governs the shifts.

**Equations:**
- Moving source, stationary observer: `f_obs = f_s · v_w / (v_w ± v_s)` (minus = approach, plus = recession)
- Moving observer, stationary source: `f_obs = f_s · (v_w ± v_obs) / v_w` (plus = approach, minus = recession)
- Both moving: `f_obs = [f_s · (v_w ± v_obs)/v_w] · v_w/(v_w ± v_s)`
- Symbols: f_s source frequency, f_obs observed, v_w sound speed, v_s source speed along line, v_obs observer speed along line.

**Worked example (train horn):** 150 Hz horn on a 35 m/s train in 340 m/s air → 167 Hz approaching, 136 Hz receding (asymmetric shifts). With source and observer moving together (engineer on train), relative velocity is zero so f_obs = f_s.

**Sonic boom physics:** As v_s → v_w, the formula denominator → 0 and f_obs → ∞ (compressions superimpose). Beyond sound speed, no sound reaches an observer until the source passes; waves interfere constructively on cone-shaped surfaces forming shock waves. Aircraft generate two booms (nose and tail).

**Applications:** ultrasound Doppler blood-flow; radar speed guns; Doppler weather radar; astronomical redshift (cosmic recession, universe age ~14 billion years); Cerenkov radiation (particles exceeding light speed in water, ~0.75c).

## 17.5 Sound Interference and Resonance: Standing Waves in Air Columns

**Objectives:** define/distinguish antinodes, nodes, fundamental, overtones, harmonics; recognize interference in real contexts; explain open/closed tube resonance and instrument design; calculate tube dimensions from wave parameters.

**Definitions:**
- **Node** — point of zero air displacement (e.g., closed tube end).
- **Antinode** — point of maximum air displacement (e.g., open tube end).
- **Fundamental frequency** — lowest natural resonant frequency of an air column.
- **Overtones** — resonant frequencies above the fundamental.
- **Harmonics** — all resonant frequencies; fundamental = first harmonic, overtones = second, third, etc.
- **Standing waves** — patterns from constructive interference of incoming and reflected waves.

**Principles:** Interference (constructive/destructive) is experimental proof sound is a wave.
- Closed tube (one end sealed): antinode at open end, node at closed end; node-to-antinode = ¼ wavelength.
- Open tube (both ends open): antinode at both ends; different natural frequencies than closed.

**Equations:**
- `v_w = f·λ`
- Closed tube fundamental: `f₁ = v_w / (4L)`
- Closed tube harmonics: `f_n = n·v_w / (4L)`, n = 1, 3, 5, … (odd only)
- Open tube harmonics: `f_n = n·v_w / (2L)`, n = 1, 2, 3, …
- Temperature dependence: `v_w = 331 m/s · √(T / 273 K)`

**Worked example (17.5):** finding tube length from fundamental frequency requires sound speed, which depends on air temperature — instruments need temperature tuning before ensemble play. Second part: higher harmonics occur at integer multiples of the fundamental, though not all appear in real instruments.

**Applications:** noise-canceling headphones (anti-phase destructive interference, ≥30 dB reduction); wind instruments (air-column resonance, holes/valves change effective length); string instruments (resonant sounding boxes); percussion (tunable resonance chambers); vocal acoustics (throat/mouth resonators shaping vowels).

## 17.6 Hearing

**Objectives:** define hearing phenomena (pitch, loudness, timbre, phons, ultrasound, infrasound); compare how frequency and intensity affect perceived loudness; describe inner-ear structures.

**Definitions:**
- **Hearing** — perception of sound waves.
- **Pitch** — perception corresponding to frequency.
- **Loudness** — perception corresponding to intensity and frequency.
- **Timbre** — perceived quality from frequency composition and relative intensities.
- **Phon** — unit of perceived loudness (differs from decibel, which measures physical intensity).
- **Ultrasound** — frequencies above 20,000 Hz, imperceptible to humans.
- **Infrasound** — frequencies below 20 Hz, imperceptible though sometimes felt.
- **Note** — basic musical unit; **Tone** — combination of frequencies with relative intensities.

**Principles:**
- Normal hearing range: 20–20,000 Hz; frequency discrimination ≥0.3%.
- Ear sensitivity peaks at 2,000–5,000 Hz; extremes need higher intensity to seem equally loud.
- Threshold of hearing ~10⁻¹² W/m² (0 dB); tolerable intensity ~10¹² times higher (~1 W/m² pain threshold).
- Equal-loudness curves show frequency-dependent response.
- At 1,000 Hz, phons = decibels numerically.
- Middle-ear pressure amplification ~40× via an ossicle lever system.

**Worked examples (17.6a–c):** use equal-loudness curves to find loudness in phons from frequency and intensity level (a); intensity level from frequency and loudness (b); equate loudness across different frequencies (c). All illustrate the interdependence of frequency and perceived loudness.

**Hearing mechanism:** outer ear (pinna, canal) resonates and enhances 2,000–5,000 Hz; middle-ear ossicles amplify pressure ~40× to the cochlea; inner ear cochlea pressure waves stimulate hair cells (cilia) on the organ of Corti, triggering nerve signals. Below ~1,000 Hz nerves fire at the sound frequency; above ~1,000 Hz frequency is encoded by position along the basilar membrane.

**Applications:** audiograms; noise-induced loss (dip near 4,000 Hz); age-related presbycusis (high frequencies); speech intelligibility thresholds; cochlear implants (~100,000 in use).

## 17.7 Ultrasound

**Objectives:** describe acoustic impedance and intensity reflection coefficient; explain medical and non-medical ultrasonic applications; compute acoustic impedance; determine object velocity from Doppler-shifted ultrasound.

**Definitions:**
- **Ultrasound** — sound above 20 kHz, beyond human hearing.
- **Cavitation** — formation of vapor bubbles in fluids from ultrasonic compression-expansion; destructive when bubbles collapse.
- **Acoustic impedance (Z)** — a medium's resistance to sound propagation; equals density × sound velocity.
- **Intensity reflection coefficient (a)** — ratio of reflected to incident intensity at a boundary.
- **Transducer** — piezoelectric crystal serving as both transmitter and receiver.
- **Doppler-shifted ultrasound** — frequency change of echoes off moving objects, enabling velocity measurement.

**Principles:**
- Impedance matching: energy transfers efficiently between media of similar impedance; larger impedance differences produce stronger reflections.
- Wavelength resolution limit: detail detection cannot exceed the probing wavelength.
- Penetration rule of thumb: effective tissue scanning depth ≈ 500 wavelengths.

**Equations:**
- Acoustic impedance: `Z = ρ·v` (ρ density kg/m³, v sound speed)
- Intensity reflection coefficient: `a = [(Z₂ − Z₁) / (Z₁ + Z₂)]²`
- Doppler (moving observer): `f_obs = f_s · (v_w ± v_obs) / v_w`
- Doppler (moving source): `f_obs = f_s · v_w / (v_w ∓ v_s)`
- Beat frequency: `f_B = |f₁ − f₂|`

**Worked examples:**
- **17.7 (fat–muscle boundary):** impedance and reflection coefficient calculation; similar soft-tissue impedances give minimal reflection (~1.4%), letting ultrasound penetrate tissue.
- **17.8 (blood velocity via Doppler):** double Doppler shift — blood receives a shifted frequency then re-radiates as a moving reflector; beat-frequency measurement detects small differences in the audible range rather than tiny MHz shifts.

**Applications:** therapeutics (gallstone/tumor destruction at 10³–10⁵ W/m²; diathermy deep-heat at 0.8–1 MHz); diagnostics (fetal monitoring, echocardiography, blood-vessel imaging, non-invasive with no documented long-term risk); industrial/research (cavitation cleaning, sonar, distance sensing, flaw detection, coating thickness, solid-state physics).
