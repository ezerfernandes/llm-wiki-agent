## Introduction to Heat and Heat Transfer Methods

This chapter treats thermal energy transfer and its mechanisms. The central framing is that **heat is energy in motion** (transferred because of a temperature difference) rather than a quantity stored inside an object.

**Learning objectives (chapter):**
- Characterize the nature and behavior of thermal energy.
- Distinguish temperature change from phase transitions in thermal systems.
- Analyze the three primary mechanisms of thermal energy movement.
- Apply thermal principles to real-world phenomena (atmospheric cooling on clear nights, planetary cooling, etc.).

**Transfer mechanisms (three pathways):**
1. **Conduction** — direct thermal energy movement through matter via molecular contact.
2. **Convection** — energy transport through fluid circulation driven by density variations.
3. **Radiation** — electromagnetic transmission requiring no intervening medium.

**Historical context:** Eunice Newton Foote performed early experiments linking atmospheric gases to thermal retention, foundational to understanding planetary heating (greenhouse effect).

## 14.1 Heat

**Objectives:** Characterize thermal energy movement and distinguish it from related concepts.

**Definitions:**
- **Heat** — the spontaneous movement of energy arising from temperature differences between systems; continues until thermal equilibrium is reached. Exists only during transfer, not as a stored state.
- **Internal energy** — energy stored within a system; increases with temperature.
- **Thermal equilibrium** — state where two or more objects reach identical temperatures via energy exchange.
- **Temperature vs. heat** — temperature measures average kinetic energy of particles; heat is the energy flow itself. People perceive heat flow, not temperature directly.

**Units of thermal energy:**
- **Joule (J)** — SI unit.
- **Calorie (cal)** — energy to raise 1.00 g of water by 1.00 °C (precisely between 14.5–15.5 °C).
- **Kilocalorie (kcal)** — energy to raise 1.00 kg of water by 1.00 °C; the food "Calorie."

**Equation — mechanical equivalent of heat:**
```
1.000 kcal = 4186 J
```
(Alternative: 4184 J/kcal, the thermochemical calorie.)

**Principle — work–heat equivalence:** Work and heat both transfer energy into/out of a system and change internal energy identically. The final state depends only on total energy change, not on whether energy arrived via work or heat.

**Experimental context:** Joule's paddle-wheel experiment showed mechanical work produces the same thermal effect as direct heat transfer, establishing energy conservation and the kcal–joule equivalence.

**Applications:** Cooking, phase transitions (melting), systems where temperature changes via energy transfer or mechanical work.

## 14.2 Temperature Change and Heat Capacity

**Objectives:** Analyze heat transfer and resulting temperature shifts relative to mass; determine final temperatures when energy redistributes between systems.

**Definitions:**
- **Specific heat (c)** — thermal energy required to raise 1 kg of a material by 1 °C. Units J/(kg·K) or kcal/(kg·°C). Depends on substance type and physical state.
- **Heat transfer (Q)** — energy movement due to temperature differences.
- **Temperature change (ΔT)** — final minus initial temperature; numerically equal in Kelvin and Celsius.

**Principle:** Heat transfer depends on temperature change magnitude, mass, and substance/phase. Internal energy scales with absolute temperature and particle count, so transferred heat scales with mass and ΔT.

**Equations:**
```
Q = m c ΔT
ΔT = Q / (m c)
Q_cold + Q_hot = 0     (thermal equilibrium, isolated system)
```

**Worked examples (concept illustrated):**
- **Ex. 14.1 (Aluminum pan + water):** Despite water's smaller mass, its much higher specific heat (4186 vs. 900 J/kg·°C for Al) means most energy heats the water, not the pan.
- **Ex. 14.2 (Truck brakes):** Mechanical-to-thermal conversion — gravitational potential energy becomes a temperature rise in brake material.
- **Ex. 14.3 (Thermal equilibrium):** Heat redistribution between objects at different initial temperatures; final equilibrium temperature reflects both mass and specific heat contributions.

**Applications:** Climate moderation (water's high specific heat stabilizes Earth's temperature; land/water response differences), calorimetry, brake design.

## 14.3 Phase Change and Latent Heat

**Objectives:** Analyze heat transfer during phase transitions; determine final equilibrium temperatures from heat exchange.

**Definitions:**
- **Phase change** — transformation among solid, liquid, gas without temperature change during the transition.
- **Latent heat** — energy absorbed or released during a phase change, in J/kg. "Latent" (hidden) because energy transfers without an observable temperature shift.
- **Latent heat of fusion (Lf)** — energy per unit mass to convert solid→liquid at the melting point.
- **Latent heat of vaporization (Lv)** — energy per unit mass to convert liquid→gas at the boiling point.
- **Sublimation** — direct solid→vapor transition (latent heat Ls).

**Principles:**
- Energy input breaks cohesive intermolecular forces, enabling greater molecular mobility without raising temperature.
- A phase transition proceeds at constant temperature until complete; only afterward does further heating raise temperature.
- Conservation of energy in an isolated system: heat absorbed by one body equals heat released by another (e.g., Q_ice = −Q_soda).

**Equations:**
```
Q = m Lf      (melting / freezing)
Q = m Lv      (vaporization / condensation)
Q = m Ls      (sublimation)
Q_ice + Q_soda = 0     (heat balance, no external losses)
Tf = [m_soda · c_w · (20 °C) − m_ice · Lf] / [(m_soda + m_ice) · c_w]   (example final-T form)
```

**Worked example (concept illustrated):**
- **Ex. 14.4 (Cooling soda with ice):** A small ice mass produces a large temperature drop because the latent heat of fusion of water (334 kJ/kg) dominates the energy balance over sensible heat. ~18 g ice at 0 °C cools 0.25 kg soda from 20 °C to ~13 °C.

**Applications:**
- **Frost protection:** spraying orchards with water — freezing releases latent heat, protecting fruit.
- **Evaporative cooling:** perspiration removes heat via evaporation (~2428 kJ/kg at body temperature); high humidity inhibits it.
- **Climate moderation:** humid regions rarely exceed ~35 °C due to evaporative absorption; condensation prevents drops below the dew point.
- **Sublimation:** dry ice cooling; volatile-solid air fresheners; frost as reverse sublimation.

## 14.4 Heat Transfer Methods

**Objective:** Distinguish the three mechanisms by which thermal energy moves.

**Definitions:**
- **Conduction** — heat transfer through stationary matter by physical contact (no bulk motion; atoms vibrate microscopically).
- **Convection** — heat transfer by macroscopic movement of a fluid.
- **Radiation** — emission or absorption of electromagnetic radiation (microwaves, infrared, visible light, etc.); needs no medium.

**Principle:** All three are driven by temperature differences. Transfer rate/method depend on material properties, geometry, and environment. Rates can be controlled via material selection (thermal resistance), air-movement management, and surface color/emissivity.

**Equations:** None in this section.

**Applications / example:**
- **Fireplace** illustrates all three: radiation (flames warming the room), conduction (heat into the floor), convection (cold air in via windows, hot air up the chimney).
- Microwave ovens (radiation), furnaces (convection), coffee cups (conduction).

## 14.5 Conduction

**Objectives:** Determine thermal conductivities; analyze heat transfer via molecular collisions; compare thermal properties across substances.

**Definitions:**
- **Thermal conductivity (k)** — material property quantifying how readily heat propagates. Metals (copper, silver, aluminum) conduct well; insulators (wood, rubber, plastic) impede flow.
- **Heat flux** — directional flow of thermal energy from higher to lower temperature, driven by molecular collisions and energy exchange.
- **R-factor (R-value)** — insulation quality metric, R = d/k (thickness / conductivity); larger means better insulation.

**Principles:**
- Conduction occurs through molecular collisions: higher-energy molecules in hot regions transfer kinetic energy to lower-energy molecules in cold regions, producing net heat flow toward cooler zones.
- Three factors affect the rate: temperature differential, cross-sectional area (more area → more collisions), and thickness (more distance → slower transfer).

**Equations:**
```
Q/t = k A (T2 − T1) / d     (conduction rate; W or kcal/s)
Q = m Lf                    (latent-heat coupling, e.g. ice melting)
```

**Worked examples (concept illustrated):**
- **Ice box:** compute total heat transferred over time, then apply latent heat to find mass of ice melted — practical insulation effectiveness.
- **Aluminum pan:** solve for the (small) temperature difference across the material given a known transfer rate — why aluminum cookware transfers stove heat efficiently with little gradient.

**Applications:** Building insulation (fiberglass, Styrofoam), cookware material choice, thermal clothing (blubber, down, wool), refrigeration, climate control.

## 14.6 Convection

**Objective:** Describe heat transfer by bulk movement of matter.

**Definitions:**
- **Convection** — heat transfer driven by macroscopic fluid flow; matter physically moves and carries thermal energy.
- **Natural convection** — fluid circulation propelled by buoyancy: hot, less-dense material rises while cooler material sinks, forming circulation loops without pumping.
- **Air infiltration** — outside air leaking into buildings through gaps around windows, doors, foundations.
- **Wind-chill factor** — the still-air temperature producing the same cooling rate as moving air at a given speed and temperature.

**Principles:**
- Density–temperature relationship: higher temperature → lower density → warmer portions rise, cooler portions descend.
- Large-scale fluid motion transfers energy faster than conduction alone (cf. wind chill).
- Convection becomes much more effective when coupled with phase change (evaporation/condensation) releasing or absorbing latent heat.
- Positive feedback in atmospheric systems: rising water vapor condenses in colder regions, releasing latent heat that drives further expansion and ascent.

**Equations:**
```
Q = m c ΔT
Q/t                         (heat transfer rate)
m/t = Q / (t · Lv)          (rate of evaporative mass loss / cooling)
```

**Worked examples (concept illustrated):**
- **Ex. 14.7 (House air infiltration):** energy/power needed when a whole house's air volume is replaced and reheated — find mass from density × volume, apply Q = mcΔT, divide by turnover time. ~4.6 kW to reheat replacement air in 30 min.
- **Ex. 14.8 (Thermoregulation via evaporation):** divide resting metabolic heat rate (~120 W) by Lv to get evaporated-mass rate ≈ 3 g/min of water loss.

**Applications:** Gravity furnaces (natural convection distributes hot air); human thermoregulation (vasodilation + sweating); ocean–atmosphere energy transfer driving storms; forced convection in vehicle cooling systems.

## 14.7 Radiation

**Objectives:** Analyze heat transfer by electromagnetic radiation; evaluate how material properties affect radiative power.

**Definitions:**
- **Radiation** — energy transfer through electromagnetic waves without a medium; works across empty space.
- **Electromagnetic spectrum** — radiation by wavelength: radio (longest) → microwave → infrared → visible → ultraviolet → X-ray → gamma (shortest); higher frequency = greater energy.
- **Emissivity (e)** — dimensionless 0–1 measure of how effectively a surface emits thermal radiation. Ideal black body: e = 1; perfect reflector: e = 0; real materials lie between.
- **Ideal radiator (black body)** — absorbs all incident radiation and emits maximally; black surfaces approximate this.
- **Thermograph** — thermal image revealing temperature variations.
- **Greenhouse effect** — atmospheric gases (CO₂, H₂O) trap infrared and re-radiate energy back to the surface, maintaining habitable temperatures.

**Laws & principles:**
- **Stefan–Boltzmann law:** radiated power depends on emissivity, surface area, and the **fourth power** of absolute temperature — an exceptionally strong temperature dependence.
- **Net radiative transfer** accounts for simultaneous emission and absorption between object and surroundings.

**Equations:**
```
Q/t = σ e A T^4                  (emitted radiation rate)
Q_net/t = σ e A (T2^4 − T1^4)    (net transfer; T2 = surroundings, T1 = object)
σ = 5.67 × 10^-8 J/(s·m²·K⁴)     (Stefan–Boltzmann constant)
```
(T in kelvin.)

**Worked example (concept illustrated):**
- **Unclothed person in a cool room:** net radiative cooling from skin (e ≈ 0.97 in infrared) to the environment ≈ 99 W — illustrating why clothing reduces radiative loss via lower emissivity and reflectivity.

**Applications:** Building thermography (heat leaks; windows radiate heat outward), urban albedo (dark pavements absorb more solar radiation), medical thermographic imaging, solar collectors/cookers and solar-heated homes (Mária Telkes), and cloud-feedback regulation of planetary temperature.
