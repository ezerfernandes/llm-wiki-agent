## Introduction to Statics and Torque

### Objectives
- Understand the equilibrium conditions for stationary objects and objects moving at constant velocity.
- Apply Newton's second law to systems where the net force is zero.
- Analyze rotational effects through the concept of torque.
- Solve practical statics problems involving real structures.

### Definitions
- **Statics**: study of systems in which forces are balanced and produce no acceleration — a special application of Newton's second law.
- **Equilibrium**: a state in which an object has zero net external force and so either stays at rest or moves at constant velocity.
- **Torque**: the rotational effect produced by a force; considered alongside translational forces.

### Principles
- **Newton's second law in equilibrium form**: net force = 0 (acceleration = 0). Holds for both stationary objects and those at constant velocity.
- **Two conditions for equilibrium** (both must hold simultaneously): net translational force = 0, and net torque = 0.

### Equations
- `F_net = ma`, with `a = 0` for equilibrium.

### Applications
Structural engineering (buildings, bridges), architectural/geological stability (mountains, rock formations), mechanical load-distribution systems, biomechanics (muscle and joint forces).

## 9.1 The First Condition for Equilibrium

### Objectives
- State the first condition for equilibrium.
- Distinguish static from dynamic equilibrium.
- Apply force balance to physical systems.

### Definitions
- **Static equilibrium**: object at rest with zero net force.
- **Dynamic equilibrium**: object moving at constant velocity with zero net external force.
- **Net external force**: the vector sum of all forces acting on a system.

### Principles
- **First condition for equilibrium**: the net external force on the system must be zero. This is necessary but, as later sections show, not sufficient on its own for full equilibrium.

### Equations
- `net F = 0`
- Component form: `net F_x = 0` and `net F_y = 0`

### Worked Examples (concepts)
- **Standing person**: static equilibrium — upward normal force equals downward weight.
- **Vehicle at constant speed**: dynamic equilibrium — propulsion balances drag horizontally; normal force supports weight vertically.
- **Hockey stick (two push scenarios)**: zero net force alone does not guarantee equilibrium; the point of force application determines whether rotation occurs.

### Applications
Structural analysis of stationary objects, steady-speed vehicle dynamics, and why identical forces produce different outcomes depending on where they are applied.

## 9.2 The Second Condition for Equilibrium

### Objectives
- State the second condition for equilibrium.
- Define torque and identify the factors that determine it.
- Explain the role of torque in rotation.

### Definitions
- **Torque (τ)**: the rotational analog of force; measures how effectively a force changes angular velocity (rotation) over time.
- **Perpendicular lever arm (r⊥)**: the shortest distance from the pivot point to the line of action of the force.

### Principles
- **Second condition for equilibrium**: the net external torque on a system must be zero. The chosen pivot point must be used consistently for all torque calculations, though the condition holds for any pivot choice.

### Equations
- `τ = r F sin θ`
- `τ = r⊥ F` (with `r⊥ = r sin θ`)
- `net τ = 0`
- SI unit: newton-meter (N·m).

### Worked Examples (concepts)
- **Opening a door**: rotational effectiveness depends on force magnitude, distance from the hinge, and the angle of application.
- **Seesaw balance**: a lighter child farther from the fulcrum balances a heavier child closer in; equilibrium requires both zero net force AND zero net torque, so unequal masses need inverse distance ratios.

### Applications
Doors, seesaws, wrenches — any system where the line of action and lever arm govern rotational effect.

## 9.3 Stability

### Objectives
- Categorize the three forms of equilibrium.
- Distinguish stable from unstable equilibrium.
- Explain neutral equilibrium.

### Definitions
- **Stable equilibrium**: after a displacement, restoring forces/torques act opposite to the displacement, returning the system toward equilibrium (e.g., a marble at the bottom of a bowl).
- **Unstable equilibrium**: after displacement, the net force/torque acts in the same direction as the displacement, driving the system away from equilibrium.
- **Neutral equilibrium**: equilibrium is unchanged by displacement from the original position.
- **Center of gravity (cg)**: the point where total weight effectively acts; key to determining stability.
- **Base of support**: the area bounded by the contact points between object and surface.

### Principles
- **Stability criterion**: a system is stable only while its center of gravity remains directly above its base of support; once the cg passes outside that boundary, it becomes unstable.
- **Degrees of stability**: some stable systems tolerate larger displacements than others before tipping.
- **Increasing stability**: widen the base of support, lower the center of gravity, or use assistive devices (cane, walker).

### Worked Examples (concepts)
- **Pencil balanced on its eraser**: stable equilibrium — small sideways displacement produces a restoring torque.
- **Pencil balanced on its point**: unstable equilibrium — slight tilt produces a torque that amplifies the displacement.
- **Ball on a flat surface**: neutral equilibrium — position changes do not affect stability.

### Applications
Human posture relies on rapid nervous-system control of torques about the hips. Women typically have a lower cg than men; infants (cg near shoulder level) struggle to learn balance; chickens are very stable due to a low cg below the hips and widely separated feet.

## 9.4 Applications of Statics, Including Problem-Solving Strategies

### Objectives
- Apply equilibrium principles to real-world problems.
- Master a problem-solving approach for static systems.

### Definitions
- **Static equilibrium**: system acceleration is zero and no accelerated rotation occurs.
- **Free body diagram**: a visual identifying all forces on a system with their magnitudes, directions, and points of application.
- **Pivot point**: reference location for torque calculations; a good choice eliminates the torque of unknown forces.
- **Center of gravity (cg)**: point where weight effectively concentrates.

### Principles
- Both equilibrium conditions apply: `ΣF = 0` and `Στ = 0`.
- `τ = r F sin θ` — torque is zero when a force acts at the pivot or along a line through it (useful for eliminating unknowns by pivot choice).

### Worked Examples (concepts)
- **Pole vaulter holding a horizontal pole** (uniform pole, two hands apart, cg offset toward one hand): illustrates how support forces distribute by leverage. The hand closer to the cg bears proportionally more weight; `F_R = (d_cg / d_hands) × mg` and `F_L = mg − F_R`.

### Applications
Two people carrying a load, table-leg support, posture and back-strain prevention, drawbridge operation, and human balance during vehicle acceleration/deceleration.

## 9.5 Simple Machines

### Objectives
- Describe the types of simple machines.
- Calculate mechanical advantage.

### Definitions
- **Simple machine**: a device that multiplies or augments an applied force, usually at the cost of the distance through which the force is applied.
- **Mechanical advantage (MA)**: ratio of output force to input force.
- **Lever**: a rigid bar that rotates about a fixed pivot (fulcrum); torque relationships enable force multiplication.
- **Perpendicular lever arm**: perpendicular distance from a force's application point to the pivot.

### Principles
- Energy conservation: a machine cannot output more work than the energy put in.
- Torque equilibrium at the fulcrum governs a lever: net torque = 0 in static conditions.
- Mechanical advantage depends on the geometry of where forces act relative to the pivot.

### Equations
- General: `MA = F_o / F_i`
- Lever torque equilibrium: `l_i F_i = l_o F_o`
- Lever MA: `MA = F_o / F_i = l_i / l_o`
- Crank/wheel systems: `MA = r_i / r_o`

### Worked Examples (concepts)
- **Wheelbarrow**: load placed between the wheel (pivot) and the hands yields MA > 1, letting a person lift loads heavier than their applied effort.

### Applications
Nail pullers (long handles amplify force), wheelbarrows (high MA), shovels (input near pivot gives MA < 1, trading force for range/speed), cranks (high MA via radius ratios), pulley systems (force multiplied in integer multiples of the tension), inclined planes/ramps (reduce force vs. vertical lifting).

## 9.6 Forces and Torques in Muscles and Joints

### Objectives
- Describe the forces produced by skeletal muscles.
- Explain how poor body positioning strains the spine.
- Explain why muscles attach near joints despite mechanical disadvantage.
- Recognize the complexity of real musculoskeletal systems.

### Definitions
- **Flexor muscles**: contract to close/bend a limb (e.g., biceps).
- **Extensor muscles**: contract to open/extend a limb (e.g., triceps).
- **Mechanical advantage**: ratio of input to output force; most skeletal systems have MA much less than 1.
- **Perpendicular lever arm**: distance from pivot to line of force action; small lever arms require large muscle forces.
- **Center of gravity (cg)**: point where weight effectively acts.

### Principles
- **Second condition for equilibrium**: net torque = 0; with a strategic pivot choice, lets one solve for unknown muscle/joint forces.
- **First condition for equilibrium**: net force = 0 in all directions.
- As the load's distance from a joint increases, the required muscle force increases proportionally.

### Equations
- Forearm torque equilibrium: `r₂(m_arm)(g) + r₃(m_load)(g) = r₁(F_biceps)`
- Biceps-to-load force ratio (Example 9.4): `F_biceps / (w_arm + w_load) ≈ 7.38`
- Vertebral force components: `F_Vy = w_upper + w_load + F_back·sin(29°)`, `F_Vx = F_back·cos(29°)`
- Magnitude: `F_V = √(F_Vx² + F_Vy²)`

### Worked Examples (concepts)
- **Biceps force (Example 9.4)**: the muscle exerts ~7.38× the supported weight because of its short perpendicular lever arm — showing why joint-adjacent attachment is mechanically disadvantageous but functionally useful (small muscle motion → large limb motion/speed).
- **Back lifting (Example 9.5)**: bending over to lift produces forces ~5.6× greater than lifting with an erect back, risking cumulative vertebral damage.

### Applications
Posture (aligning upper-body cg over the hip pivot removes unnecessary muscle effort), sports (tennis elbow from repeated high-torque motion; racquet mass × lever arm sets joint stress), physical therapy (controlled forces/torques for rehabilitation), proper lifting technique (use legs not back), and load carrying (body leans to keep overall cg above the base of support).
