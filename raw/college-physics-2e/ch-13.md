## 13.0 Introduction

The chapter ties together how temperature, the microscopic motion of particles, and the
macroscopic gas laws relate. Temperature is treated as a measurable quantity distinct from
heat; pressure and temperature are explained through atomic/molecular motion (kinetic theory);
the ideal gas law predicts how gases respond to changes in pressure, volume, temperature, and
amount; thermal expansion is analyzed in solids and liquids; phase transitions and their energy
requirements are examined; and evaporation, boiling, and humidity are connected through vapor
pressure. Applications span thermometer design, spacecraft outgassing/contamination control,
engine and turbine gas behavior, climate modeling, and stellar radiation.

## 13.1 Temperature

**Objectives:** give a scientific definition of temperature; convert among Celsius, Fahrenheit,
and Kelvin; explain thermal equilibrium and the zeroth law of thermodynamics.

**Definitions:**
- **Temperature** — operationally, "what a thermometer measures." Defined through measurement
  rather than human sensation; identical thermometers in the same environment read the same.
  It is an **intensive** property (independent of system size).
- **Thermal equilibrium** — state reached when two systems in contact exchange energy until they
  are at the same temperature and no further net heat flows.
- **Absolute zero** — the lowest possible temperature, 0 K = −273.15 °C, where molecular motion
  is minimal. For a constant-volume gas, pressure extrapolates to zero at absolute zero.

**Zeroth law of thermodynamics:** if system A is in thermal equilibrium with B, and B with C,
then A is in thermal equilibrium with C. This transitivity is what makes temperature meaningful
and justifies the use of thermometers.

**Temperature scales:**

| Scale | Freezing pt of water | Boiling pt of water |
|---|---|---|
| Celsius | 0 °C | 100 °C |
| Fahrenheit | 32 °F | 212 °F |
| Kelvin | 273.15 K | 373.15 K |

One Celsius degree = 1.8 Fahrenheit degrees (180/100 = 9/5).

**Conversion equations:**
- T(°F) = (9/5)·T(°C) + 32
- T(°C) = (5/9)·(T(°F) − 32)
- T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15
- T(°C) = T(K) − 273.15
- T(K) = (5/9)·(T(°F) − 32) + 273.15
- T(°F) = (9/5)·(T(K) − 273.15) + 32

**Worked examples (concept illustrated):**
- *Room temperature 25 °C → other scales* — applying cross-scale conversion (→ 77 °F, → 298 K).
- *Réaumur scale* — deriving a conversion factor between non-standard scales by comparing
  scale spans (100 °C = 80 °R).

**Applications:** thermometers exploit several temperature-dependent properties — liquid volume
expansion (alcohol/mercury), bimetallic-strip curvature, electrical resistance, liquid-crystal
color shifts, and emitted infrared radiation. Normal human body temperature averages 37.0 °C
(98.6 °F), survivable roughly 24–44 °C; infrared thermography detects abnormal temperatures
(tumors, circulatory problems, infections). Extreme range examples: lab cooling to 4.5×10⁻¹⁰ K;
Vostok, Antarctica at 183 K (−89 °C); Boomerang Nebula near 1 K. Human perception is unreliable —
wood and metal at equal air temperature feel different because of conductivity, not temperature.

## 13.2 Thermal Expansion of Solids and Liquids

**Objectives:** describe/explain thermal expansion; compute linear, area, and volume changes from
expansion coefficients and temperature change; assess thermal stress from constrained expansion.

**Definitions:**
- **Thermal expansion** — change in size/volume when temperature changes. Atoms vibrate more
  vigorously when hotter, pushing neighbors apart, so the material grows.
- **Coefficient of linear expansion (α)** — material-specific, length change per unit length per
  degree (units 1/°C or 1/K).
- **Coefficient of volume expansion (β)** — governs volume change; β ≈ 3α for most solids.
- **Thermal stress** — internal forces produced when a material is heated/cooled but cannot
  freely expand/contract.

**Equations:**
- Linear: ΔL = α·L·ΔT
- Area: ΔA = 2α·A·ΔT
- Volume: ΔV = β·V·ΔT  (β ≈ 3α)
- Pressure from constrained volume change: P = (ΔV / V₀)·B, where B is the bulk modulus.

**Worked examples (concept illustrated):**
- *Golden Gate Bridge* — measurable linear expansion over a temperature range (~0.84 m across
  55 °C), motivating expansion joints.
- *Gasoline overflowing a steel tank* — differential expansion: gasoline expands much more than
  the steel container, so fuel spills (~1.1 L from a 60 L tank over a 20 °C rise).
- *Sealed tank pressure* — preventing expansion produces enormous internal pressure (~1.83×10⁷ Pa),
  threatening rupture.

**Applications:** expansion joints in bridges/railroads prevent buckling; power lines are left
slack for seasonal contraction; Pyrex's low α resists thermal cracking; sea-level rise is partly
thermal expansion of seawater; dental fillings/implant coatings are chosen to match the expansion
of surrounding tissue/bone.

**Anomaly of water:** between 0 °C and 4 °C water *expands as it cools*, reaching maximum density
at 4 °C. Surface ice is therefore less dense and floats, insulating liquid water below and
letting aquatic life survive.

## 13.3 The Ideal Gas Law

**Objectives:** state the ideal gas law in molecular and molar forms; apply it to changes in P, V,
T, or amount; use Avogadro's number to convert between molecular and molar quantities.

**Definitions:**
- **Ideal gas** — a theoretical gas whose particles have negligible volume and negligible
  intermolecular forces except during elastic collisions. Real gases approximate this under many
  conditions.
- **Boltzmann constant k** = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K — relates energy to temperature.
- **Avogadro's number Nₐ** = 6.02×10²³ mol⁻¹ — particles per mole.
- **Mole** — amount of substance with as many particles as atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12.
- **Equation of state** — relation among the thermodynamic variables of a system.

**Why gases behave alike:** molecules are widely separated relative to their size, so
intermolecular forces are negligible and behavior depends on particle density and temperature,
not on the gas's identity. The ideal gas law summarizes experimental laws — Charles' law
(V ∝ T at constant P) and Boyle's law (PV constant at constant T).

**Equations:**
- Molecular form: PV = NkT
- Molar form: PV = nRT, with R = 8.31 J/(mol·K) = 0.0821 L·atm/(mol·K) = 1.99 cal/(mol·K)
- P–T at constant V, N: P_f/P₀ = T_f/T₀  →  P_f = P₀·(T_f/T₀)
- Solve for count: N = PV/(kT)
- Solve for moles: n = PV/(RT)

P and T must be absolute (Pa and K).

**Worked examples (concept illustrated):**
- *Tire pressure vs. temperature* — at fixed V and N, pressure scales directly with absolute T;
  Celsius must be converted to Kelvin.
- *Molecules per cubic meter at STP* — counting particles at 0 °C, 1 atm; ~2.68×10²⁵ molecules/m³
  regardless of gas type.
- *Molar quantities* — converting molecular populations to moles via Nₐ; ~22.5 L per mole at the
  stated standard conditions.
- *Moles in a tire* — applying the molar form, selecting the R value matching the chosen units.

**Applications:** tire-pressure monitoring (cold-tire spec); pharmaceutical particle counts;
atmospheric density–pressure relations; understanding gas work in engines/turbines.

**Energy connection:** PV has units of work (force × distance) while NkT is proportional to total
translational kinetic energy — linking the gas law to energy conservation.

## 13.4 Kinetic Theory: Atomic and Molecular Explanation of Pressure and Temperature

**Objectives:** connect the ideal gas law to molecular mass and speed; identify thermal energy as
average translational kinetic energy; compute molecular KE from temperature; explain how gas
temperature relates to molecular motion and how speeds are distributed.

**Definitions:**
- **Thermal energy** — the average translational kinetic energy of a molecule in a gas.
- **Root-mean-square (rms) speed** — square root of the mean squared molecular speed; a
  representative molecular speed.
- **Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution** — statistical distribution of molecular speeds; peaked at
  lower speeds with a long high-speed tail.
- **Mean free path** — average distance a molecule travels between collisions.

**Principles:** gas pressure is the cumulative effect of countless elastic molecular collisions
with the container walls; more molecules or higher average speed → more pressure. On an elastic
wall collision the perpendicular momentum component reverses while speed is unchanged. Motion is
isotropic, so the mean squared velocity components are equal:
v̄ₓ² = v̄_y² = v̄_z², giving v̄² = 3·v̄ₓ².

**Equations:**
- Pressure from molecular motion: PV = (1/3)·N·m·v̄²
- Temperature–KE link: KE̅ = (1/2)·m·v̄² = (3/2)·kT
- rms speed: v_rms = √(3kT/m)
- v̄² = 3·v̄ₓ²
- Force from one molecule on a wall: F = m·vₓ²/l

**Worked examples (concept illustrated):**
- *Example 13.10 — N₂ at 20 °C* — thermal energy depends only on absolute temperature, not on
  molecular type; nitrogen's rms speed is high (~511 m/s) even though bulk air seems still.
- *Example 13.11 — escape velocity and helium* — back-solving temperature from a target speed;
  explains why light atoms (helium, hydrogen) reach escape velocity and leak from the atmosphere
  while heavier molecules do not.

**Applications:** sound speed correlates with molecular rms speed; fever moisture loss reflects
faster molecules; Earth vs. Moon atmospheric composition reflects gravity's effect on escape;
Brownian motion of small particles (pollen) arises from statistical collision fluctuations.

## 13.5 Phase Changes

**Objectives:** interpret phase (PT) diagrams; state Dalton's law of partial pressures; identify
the triple point; describe equilibrium between phase pairs (liquid–gas, liquid–solid, solid–gas).

**Definitions:**
- **Phase diagram (PT graph)** — pressure–temperature plot whose regions are the states of matter;
  boundary lines mark conditions where two phases coexist.
- **Isotherm** — a constant-temperature P–V curve.
- **Critical point** — endpoint of the liquid–gas boundary; above it liquid cannot exist.
- **Critical temperature** — highest temperature at which a substance can be liquefied at any
  pressure.
- **Critical pressure** — minimum pressure for liquid to exist at the critical temperature.
- **Triple point** — the unique P, T where solid, liquid, and gas coexist in equilibrium.
- **Sublimation** — direct solid→gas transition (no liquid).
- **Vapor** — the gas phase below the boiling temperature.
- **Vapor pressure** — the pressure at which the gas phase coexists with the liquid/solid phase;
  depends on substance and temperature.
- **Partial pressure** — pressure a component gas would exert alone in the full volume.

**Dalton's law of partial pressures:** total pressure = sum of the partial pressures of the
component gases (ideal behavior, no reactions). It follows from kinetic theory — each gas exerts
its own collisional pressure independently of the others.

**Equations:**
- PV = NkT
- At constant T and N: PV = constant (Boyle) — a hyperbolic isotherm on a PV diagram.

**Representative critical-point values:** water 647.4 K (374.3 °C), 22.12×10⁶ Pa (219 atm);
CO₂ 304.2 K (31.1 °C), 7.39×10⁶ Pa; N₂ 126.2 K, 3.39×10⁶ Pa; O₂ 154.8 K, 5.08×10⁶ Pa.

**Representative triple-point values:** water 273.16 K (0.01 °C), 6.10×10² Pa (0.006 atm);
CO₂ 216.55 K (−56.6 °C), 5.16×10⁵ Pa (5.11 atm); N₂ 63.18 K, 1.25×10⁴ Pa.

**Real vs. ideal gases / PV diagrams:** at low temperature, molecular attractions and finite
molecular volume matter; isotherms deviate from hyperbolas and may include a liquid region.
Volume drops sharply near liquefaction; solids retain a finite molecular volume.

**Phase-diagram features:** the solid–liquid line shows melting temperature varying with pressure
(higher pressure lowers ice's melting point — why snowballs pack and tires can melt snow); the
liquid–gas line ends at the critical point (water boils at 100 °C at 1 atm but 374 °C at ~218 atm);
below ~0.006 atm for water, the solid–gas line means sublimation occurs directly.

**Equilibria:** in a closed container at the boiling temperature, evaporation and condensation
balance (liquid–gas equilibrium); raising T speeds both equally. In open air, pure-water
equilibrium can't establish, so water fully evaporates. Ice and water hold at 0 °C while both
phases coexist (solid–liquid equilibrium).

**Applications:** pressure cookers (higher P → higher boiling T → faster cooking); dry ice
(solid CO₂ sublimes at −78 °C, no liquid at 1 atm); liquid nitrogen (boils at 77 K / −196 °C;
cryogenic preservation, electronics cooling, wart removal); frost-free freezers (frost sublimes);
freeze-drying foods; orange-grove frost protection (freezing water releases heat, holding 0 °C).

## 13.6 Humidity, Evaporation, and Boiling

**Objectives:** relate water's vapor pressure to air's capacity for water vapor; relate relative
humidity to the partial pressure of water vapor; find vapor density from vapor pressure; compute
percent humidity and dew point.

**Definitions:**
- **Relative humidity** — percentage comparing actual water vapor in air to the maximum possible
  at that temperature; at 100% (saturation) net evaporation stops.
- **Dew point** — temperature at which the air would reach 100% relative humidity; cooling below it
  causes condensation (dew/frost).
- **Vapor pressure** — pressure of water molecules in the gas phase in equilibrium with liquid/solid;
  depends only on temperature, not on the surrounding air.
- **Saturation vapor density** — maximum mass of water vapor (g/m³) air can hold at a given
  temperature before condensing.

**Principles:** molecules escape a liquid/solid surface when their kinetic energy exceeds binding
forces. In a sealed container evaporation proceeds until condensation balances it at the saturation
vapor pressure. Higher temperature → faster molecules → higher vapor pressure and saturation
density. The equilibrium is independent of the presence/composition of air.

**Equations:**
- PV = nRT
- Molar density: n/V = P/(RT)  (used to convert vapor pressure → vapor density)
- Percent relative humidity = (vapor density / saturation vapor density) × 100

**Worked examples (concept illustrated):**
- *Example 13.12 — vapor density* — using PV = nRT to turn vapor pressure (2.33×10³ Pa at 20 °C)
  into saturation vapor density (~17.2 g/m³); confirms water vapor behaves nearly ideally.
- *Example 13.13 — humidity and dew point* —
  (a) relative humidity from actual vs. tabulated saturation density (~40.9% at 25 °C with
  9.40 g/m³);
  (b) dew point as the temperature where measured density equals saturation (~10 °C);
  (c) relative humidity from a known dew point (~10.3% when dew point is −10 °C and air is 25 °C).

**Phenomena explained:** hot air speeds evaporation (energized molecules escape faster — why
blow-drying beats cooling); water boils when its vapor pressure equals atmospheric pressure
(1.01×10⁵ Pa at 100 °C); dissolved-air bubbles nucleate boiling and fill with vapor until buoyancy
lifts them; freeze-drying lowers the partial pressure of water vapor so fast molecules leave,
draining average KE and cooling/freezing the sample (evaporative cooling).

**Applications:** dew-point monitoring for frost prediction; high humidity suppresses sweat
evaporation, raising perceived heat stress; controlling saturation prevents condensation damage
in storage; low desert humidity yields large day–night temperature swings (little latent-heat
exchange).
