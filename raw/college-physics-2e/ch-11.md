## Introduction to Fluid Statics

The chapter studies fluids that are not moving (statics). It develops: what distinguishes fluids from solids; how static fluids respond to applied conditions; the mathematics linking pressure and depth; the principles behind buoyancy and flotation; and surface properties such as surface tension. Core principles introduced: Pascal's principle (pressure on a confined fluid transmits uniformly), Archimedes' principle (buoyant force equals the weight of displaced fluid), and hydrostatic pressure rising predictably with depth. Applications span swimming/flotation, hydraulic machinery, physiological pressures, and surface phenomena.

---

## 11.1 What Is a Fluid?

**Objectives:** categorize common states of matter; characterize properties distinguishing solids, liquids, gases; explain atomic-level structural differences.

**Definitions:**
- **Phases of matter:** solid, liquid, gas, plasma.
- **Fluid:** a substance that yields to (deforms under) shearing forces — includes liquids, gases, and plasmas, but not solids.
- **Viscosity:** resistance to flow; how readily a fluid deforms under applied force.

**State characteristics:**
- **Solid:** definite shape and volume; resists all stress types; atoms fixed in lattice positions, vibrating but not exchanging neighbors; spring-like restoring forces.
- **Liquid:** variable shape (takes container), definite volume; atoms closely packed but slide past one another; strong mutual attraction resists compression but allows flow.
- **Gas:** variable shape and volume; atoms widely spaced (separations much larger than atomic size); weak intermolecular forces except in collisions; easily compressed; escapes open containers.
- **Plasma:** variable shape and volume; energized particles; hard to contain.

The submicroscopic atomic picture explains macroscopic behavior, connecting to momentum, collisions, and pressure.

## 11.2 Density

**Objectives:** articulate density; find mass from density; compare densities across materials.

**Definition:** Density is mass per unit volume — how tightly matter is packed. Helps predict floating vs. sinking.

The metric system was originally set so that water at standard conditions has density exactly 1 g/cm³ = 10³ kg/m³ (so 1 L of water ≈ 1 kg). Liquids and solids have comparable densities (atoms in contact); gases are far less dense (large empty spaces).

**Equations:**
- ρ = m / V  (SI: kg/m³)
- m = ρV

**Worked example (concept):** *Reservoir mass from dimensions* — combine geometric volume (surface area × depth) with tabulated water density to find the total mass of water stored behind a dam.

**Applications:** material identification (gold ≈ 2.5× iron's density); flotation prediction; dam and water-management engineering.

## 11.3 Pressure

**Objectives:** define pressure conceptually; relate pressure to applied force; find force from pressure and area.

**Definitions:**
- **Pressure:** the perpendicular force distributed over a surface area — how concentrated a force is.
- **Pascal (Pa):** SI unit, 1 N/m².
- Other units: millibar (mb; 100 mb = 10⁴ Pa, meteorology); psi (tire pressure); mm Hg (blood pressure).

**Principles:** equal forces give very different effects depending on contact area — small area → high pressure, large area → low pressure. Fluid pressure: acts perpendicular to all contacted surfaces; cannot sustain shearing forces; pressure itself is a scalar though it produces directional forces; exerted equally in all directions.

**Equations:**
- P = F / A
- F = PA

**Worked example (concept):** *Pressurized air tank* — high pressure (6.90×10⁶ Pa) on a disk (diameter 0.150 m) yields a large force (≈1.22×10⁵ N), showing why pressure vessels must be robust.

**Applications:** weather systems; blood pressure; tire pressure; fluid mechanics generally.

## 11.4 Variation of Pressure with Depth in a Fluid

**Objectives:** express pressure in terms of fluid weight; describe how pressure increases with depth; find density from pressure and height.

**Key ideas:**
- Pressure at a depth comes from the weight of the fluid column above. Reliable for nearly incompressible liquids to large depths; for compressible gases, valid only when density varies little over the depth (use average density).
- **Standard atmospheric pressure:** 1 atm = 1.01×10⁵ N/m² = 101 kPa.

**Equations:**
- P = mg / A
- m = ρV, V = Ah
- **P = hρg**  (pressure from fluid of depth h)
- P̄ = h̄ρg  (average, using average depth)
- F = P̄A
- ρ̄ = P / (hg)

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- *Dam pressure* — average pressure on a dam depends only on water depth, not reservoir size; computes force on the submerged surface.
- *Atmospheric density* — air density falls dramatically with altitude; the column average (≈0.086 kg/m³) is ~15× smaller than sea-level density (1.29 kg/m³) due to gas compression near the surface.
- *Water pressure equivalence* — only ~10.3 m of water gives the same pressure as the entire ~120-km atmosphere, since water is ~775× denser than air.

**Applications:** diving physiology; dam thickness increasing with depth; atmospheric science; why water pressure rises fast but cabin pressure changes little on ascent.

## 11.5 Pascal's Principle

**Objectives:** state Pascal's principle; recognize real-world uses; solve hydraulic force relationships.

**Definitions:**
- **Enclosed fluid:** a contained volume where fluid cannot escape, so pressure can build.
- **Pascal's principle:** a change in pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished to every portion of the fluid and the container walls.
- **Hydraulic system:** an enclosed mechanism that uses transmitted pressure to generate forces (e.g., vehicle brakes).

**Principle:** applied pressure distributes equally throughout, enabling force multiplication when it acts on pistons of different areas.

**Equations:**
- P = F / A
- F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂  (pistons at equal height, negligible friction)

**Worked example (concept):** *Automobile brake system* — a small input force on a small-area master piston is amplified into a large output force at larger-area wheel cylinders, demonstrating hydraulic force multiplication via area ratio.

**Applications:** vehicle brakes; force multiplication; routing pressure to multiple points simultaneously; spider hydraulic leg movement.

## 11.6 Gauge Pressure, Absolute Pressure, and Pressure Measurement

**Objectives:** distinguish gauge vs. absolute pressure; explain how aneroid gauges and open-tube manometers/barometers work.

**Definitions:**
- **Gauge pressure:** pressure relative to atmospheric — positive above atmospheric, negative below.
- **Absolute pressure:** gauge pressure plus atmospheric pressure.
- **Systolic / diastolic:** maximum / minimum blood pressure over the heartbeat cycle.

**Principles:** Pascal's principle lets pressure be sensed remotely. Because fluids push (not pull), the lowest possible absolute pressure is zero, so minimum gauge pressure = −P_atm.

**Equations:**
- P_abs = P_g + P_atm
- P = hρg (fluid column)
- P_g = hρg (manometer)
- 1.0 mm Hg = 133 Pa

**Worked example (concept):** *IV bag height* — find the height needed for IV fluid to exceed venous blood pressure, converting mm Hg to SI and solving h = P_g/(ρg).

**Applications:** tire gauges (read zero at atmospheric); mercury-manometer blood-pressure cuffs; barometers (also altimeters since pressure varies with altitude); bellows/spring mechanical gauges.

## 11.7 Archimedes' Principle

**Objectives:** understand the origin of buoyant force; state Archimedes' principle and its math; predict float/sink/suspend; connect density to buoyancy.

**Definitions:**
- **Buoyant force:** net upward force on an object in a fluid, arising from the pressure difference between its lower and upper surfaces.
- **Archimedes' principle:** the buoyant force equals the weight of the fluid displaced.
- **Specific gravity:** dimensionless ratio of an object's average density to water's density (at 4.00 °C); < 1 floats, > 1 sinks.

**Equations:**
- F_B = w_fl  (buoyant force = weight of displaced fluid)
- V_st = m_st / ρ_st ; m_w = ρ_w V_w ; F_B = m_w g
- Fraction submerged = V_sub / V_obj = V_fl / V_obj
- Fraction submerged = ρ̄_obj / ρ_fl
- specific gravity = ρ̄ / ρ_w
- ρ̄_person = (fraction submerged) × ρ_fl
- Apparent weight loss = weight of fluid displaced; apparent mass loss = mass of fluid displaced

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- *Shape dependency (steel boat)* — the same steel mass sinks as a solid block but floats when shaped as a boat, because the boat's larger displaced volume raises buoyant force without changing material density.
- *Average density of a floating human* — a person floats because average density (≈970 kg/m³ with air in lungs) is below water's (1000 kg/m³); submersion fraction relates inversely to density.
- *Density via apparent weight* — submerged weight loss reveals an object's density, a method to authenticate artifacts such as ancient coins.

**Applications:** ship design and cargo capacity; hydrostatic body-composition weighing; submarine ballast tanks; hydrometers; isostasy (mountains "floating" on denser mantle).

**Historical context:** Archimedes (ca. 287–212 BC), Greek mathematician and inventor, formulated this principle before rigorous force concepts existed. Legend has him discovering it in a bath and shouting "Eureka!" ("I have found it").

## 11.8 Cohesion and Adhesion in Liquids: Surface Tension and Capillary Action

**Objectives:** distinguish cohesive vs. adhesive forces; define and explain surface tension; analyze capillary action.

**Definitions:**
- **Cohesive forces:** attraction between molecules of the same type; hold a liquid together.
- **Adhesive forces:** attraction between molecules of different types; cause a liquid to cling to other surfaces.
- **Surface tension:** tendency of a liquid surface to contract to minimum area; surface molecules feel a net inward pull, interior molecules are balanced.
- **Contact angle (θ):** angle between liquid-surface tangent and the solid; indicates relative strength of cohesion vs. adhesion (large θ → cohesion dominates).
- **Capillary action:** rise or depression of a liquid in a narrow tube from competing cohesive/adhesive forces.
- **Meniscus:** curved liquid surface in a tube (concave for water-glass, convex for mercury-glass).

**Principles:** lung surfactant lowers alveolar surface tension as area shrinks (preventing collapse), unlike ordinary detergents that lower it uniformly. Smaller tube radius → greater capillary rise (less mass to support). Internal pressure of a spherical bubble exceeds external pressure, and is greater for smaller bubbles.

**Equations:**
- γ = F / L  (surface tension = force per unit length)
- P = 4γ / r  (gauge pressure inside a spherical bubble)
- h = 2γ cos θ / (ρ g r)  (capillary rise height)

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- *Soap bubble pressure* — gauge pressure inside a small bubble (r = 2.00×10⁻⁴ m) reaches several mm Hg, showing the inverse dependence on radius.
- *Tree sap transport* — the capillary radius needed to raise sap 100 m (1.41×10⁻⁷ m) is implausibly small, proving capillarity alone can't lift sap; transpiration-driven negative pressure pulls sap up xylem via cohesive chains.

**Applications:** insects/needles supported on water; alveolar mechanics and infant respiratory distress (synthetic surfactants); capillary blood sampling; water beading on waxed vs. unwaxed surfaces (contact angle); plant nutrient transport.

## 11.9 Pressures in the Body

**Objectives:** understand pressure in human physiology; distinguish systolic vs. diastolic blood pressure; recognize pressures across ocular, pulmonary, spinal, urinary, and skeletal systems.

**Definitions:**
- **Systolic pressure:** peak arterial pressure on heart contraction; normal 100–140 mm Hg.
- **Diastolic pressure:** minimum between beats; normal 60–90 mm Hg.
- **Intraocular pressure:** maintains eye shape; normal 12–24 mm Hg; high values cause glaucoma.
- **Cerebrospinal fluid pressure:** around brain/spinal cord; normal 5–12 mm Hg.
- **Gauge pressure:** pressure above atmospheric.

**Principle:** hydrostatic pressure P = hρg explains how pressure rises with vertical distance (blood pressure in the feet exceeds that at the heart).

**Equations:**
- ΔP = Δh ρ g
- P = hρg
- F = PA

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- *Foot pressure* — a ~1.4 m blood column adds ~108 mm Hg, showing gravity's effect over body height.
- *Eardrum rupture* — force-pressure-area relation gives a maximum tolerable gauge pressure (~226 mm Hg) and equivalent water depth (~3.06 m).
- *Glaucoma force* — a modest pressure rise (85 mm Hg over 6 cm²) produces a substantial force (~6.8 N) able to damage tissue.

**Applications / clinical:** arterial pressure as stroke/heart-attack indicator; glaucoma; spinal-fluid headaches; bladder micturition reflex; joint pressures (up to ~50 atm) and lifting injuries; pleural pressure (−8 to +3 mm Hg) and pneumothorax; managing blood pressure cuts mortality; compression stockings for prolonged standing; Eustachian-tube equalization in diving.
