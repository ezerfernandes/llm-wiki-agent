## Chapter Intro — Introduction to Science and the Realm of Physics

### Learning Objectives
- Understand physics as a discipline describing natural phenomena across all scales.
- Recognize the universal applicability of physical laws.
- Master systems for quantifying and measuring physical properties.
- Develop precision and accuracy in scientific measurement.
- Evaluate the limitations inherent in physical observations.

### Key Concepts & Definitions
- **Physics**: The natural science explaining forces, structures, and interactions from subatomic to cosmic scales through mathematical principles.
- **Light-year**: An astronomical distance unit — the distance light travels in one year, roughly 9.5 trillion km.
- **Supernova remnant**: Expanding material from a stellar explosion (e.g., the Veil Nebula, from a star ~20× the Sun's mass).
- **MOSFET (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor)**: Invented 1959; basis of modern computing; the most-manufactured human-made object.

### Physical Principles
- **Universality of physical laws**: Forces governing astronomical phenomena are identical to those on Earth.
- **Elemental composition**: Stars involve the same elements (hydrogen, oxygen, sulfur) as terrestrial matter.
- **Technological foundation**: All technology, from Stone Age tools to Information Age electronics, derives from applied physics.

### Real-World Applications
- Aerospace engineering (wind forces on flight), medicine (force analysis in physical therapy), electronics (semiconductors), materials science (steel, optical glass), astronomy (comparative nebula imaging).

## 1.1 — Physics: An Introduction

### Learning Objectives
- Distinguish between principles and laws.
- Differentiate models from theories.

### Key Definitions
- **Physics**: Study of interactions between energy, matter, space, and time; focuses on fundamental underlying mechanisms.
- **Model**: A mental representation of phenomena too small/complex to observe directly; justified by evidence but limited in scope (e.g., planetary atomic model with electrons orbiting a nucleus).
- **Theory**: A testable explanation for patterns in nature, supported by evidence and verified by multiple researchers; more complex than a law; describes whole groups of related phenomena; must be experimentally verifiable.
- **Law**: A concise, generalized statement describing a natural pattern, supported by evidence and often expressed mathematically; describes single actions rather than complex systems.
- **Principle**: Less broadly applicable than a law (e.g., Pascal's principle applies only to fluids).

### Fundamental Concepts
- **Underlying order**: Nature shows remarkable simplicity — few unified laws explain vast observations.
- **Law of Conservation of Energy**: Energy changes form but is never lost; unifies topics from food calories to batteries.

### Key Equations
- **Newton's Second Law**: F = ma (force = mass × acceleration).

### Scientific Method
- Systematic process: observation → research → hypothesis → experimentation → analysis → conclusion. Adaptable beyond formal science.

### Historical Context
- Physics grew from natural philosophy. Classical physics (Renaissance–19th c.) differs from modern physics (20th c. onward: relativity and quantum mechanics for extreme speeds and small scales).
- Contributors: Aristotle, Ibn al-Haytham, Galileo, Newton, Marie Curie, Niels Bohr.

### Real-World Applications
- Smartphones/GPS (circuit electricity + relativistic satellite corrections), microwave ovens, medical imaging (MRI, X-ray, ultrasound), cancer radiation therapy, car engines (heat transfer), architecture (structure, acoustics), cell biology (membranes, nerve impulses).

## 1.2 — Physical Quantities and Units

### Learning Objectives
- Perform unit conversions within SI and English systems.
- Explain common metric prefixes and express them in scientific notation.

### Key Definitions
- **Physical Quantity**: A measurable property defined by specifying a measurement procedure or a calculation from other measurements.
- **Units**: Standardized values enabling consistent expression of measurements.
- **Fundamental Units**: The basic units from which others derive — length (meter), mass (kilogram), time (second), electric current (ampere).
- **Derived Units**: Combinations of fundamental units (e.g., speed = distance/time).
- **Order of Magnitude**: The scale of a value as a power of 10; values sharing a power are of the same order.
- **Conversion Factor**: A ratio expressing equivalence between units (e.g., 1000 m = 1 km).

### Fundamental SI Units
| Quantity | Unit | Symbol |
|----------|------|--------|
| Length | Meter | m |
| Mass | Kilogram | kg |
| Time | Second | s |
| Electric Current | Ampere | A |

### Metric Prefixes (powers of 10)
- kilo (k) = 10³
- centi (c) = 10⁻²
- milli (m) = 10⁻³
- micro (µ) = 10⁻⁶
- nano (n) = 10⁻⁹

### Unit Conversion Method
- Multiply the original measurement by conversion factors arranged so unwanted units cancel, leaving the desired units (dimensional analysis).
- Example: Distance (km) = Distance (m) × (1 km / 1000 m).

### Worked Example
- **Example 1.1 — Unit Conversions**: Converting average speed between unit systems. Illustrates that multiple valid conversion pathways exist; checking dimensional consistency and reasonableness validates the result.

### Historical Development of Standards
- **Second** (1967): redefined via cesium-atom vibrations (9,192,631,770 oscillations).
- **Meter** (1983): distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 s in vacuum.
- **Kilogram** (2019): redefined using Planck's constant (formerly a platinum-iridium artifact).

### System Advantages / Drawbacks
- SI/metric: conversions are powers of 10; prefixes span enormous ranges; no need for specialized units.
- English system: non-decimal relationships (12 in/ft; 5280 ft/mile).

### Real-World Applications
- Kilometers for air travel; nanometers for optics; atomic diameter (~10⁻¹⁰ m) vs Sun diameter (~10⁹ m); recipe and walking-direction conversions.

## 1.3 — Accuracy, Precision, and Significant Figures

### Learning Objectives
- Determine appropriate significant figures for arithmetic and multiplicative calculations.
- Calculate percent uncertainty.

### Key Definitions
- **Accuracy**: How closely a measurement approaches the true/accepted value.
- **Precision**: Degree of agreement among repeated measurements under identical conditions (consistency, not correctness).
- **Uncertainty (δA)**: Quantitative expression of deviation from expected value, written as A ± δA.
- **Significant Figures**: Digits reflecting the precision of the instrument; the last digit recorded is the first with some uncertainty.
- **Percent Uncertainty**: Uncertainty expressed as a percentage of the measured value.

### Core Principles
- Measurements always carry uncertainty from equipment limits, user skill, object irregularities, and situational factors.
- Accuracy and precision are independent — a measurement can be accurate but imprecise, or precise but inaccurate (GPS example).

### Equations
- **Percent uncertainty**: % unc = (δA / A) × 100%.

### Significant-Figure Rules
- **Zeros**: significant except when only placekeepers. Leading zeros (0.053) not significant; embedded zeros (10.053) significant; trailing zeros (1300) ambiguous — use scientific notation.
- **Multiplication/Division**: result keeps as many sig figs as the least-precise input.
- **Addition/Subtraction**: result keeps as many decimal places as the least-precise input.

### Worked Example
- **Apple bag example**: Calculating percent uncertainty from measured data and how uncertainty propagates through repeated measurements.

### Real-World Applications
- Medical thermometers (±3.0°C makes readings clinically useless), grocery scale precision variations, GPS accuracy vs precision.

## 1.4 — Approximation

### Learning Objectives
- Formulate reasonable estimates using available data.
- Apply approximation techniques across scientific and engineering contexts.

### Key Concepts
- **Approximation/Estimation**: Calculated guesses about quantities when precise measurements are unavailable; many approximate numbers come from formulae whose inputs are known only to limited accuracy.
- Develops through quantitative thinking, willingness to take calculated risks, familiarity with unit conversions, and experience with reference quantities.
- **Purpose**: Exclude unrealistic scenarios, challenge assumptions, and guide scientific reasoning.

### Worked Examples
- **Example 1.3 — Building height**: Scaling an unknown from a known human-scale reference (~2 m per story); a 39-story building ≈ 156 m.
- **Example 1.4 — Money volume**: Converting an abstract quantity into visualizable dimensions; one trillion dollars in $100 bills stacked on a football field ≈ 8 ft high.

### Real-World Applications
- Building dimensions, infrastructure/debt visualization, circuit current predictions, object density calculations, general problem-solving.
