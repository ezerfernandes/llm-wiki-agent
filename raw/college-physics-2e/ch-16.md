## Introduction

Oscillations are motions that repeat cyclically between extremes — ocean buoys, swings, speaker cones, guitar strings, crystal lattices, biological rhythms. A wave is a disturbance that travels outward from its source carrying energy, whether visible (water, light) or invisible (sound, seismic). The chapter develops periodic motion (period, frequency, amplitude), simple harmonic motion, the pendulum, energy in oscillators, the circular-motion analogy, damping, forced oscillation and resonance, wave fundamentals, superposition/interference, and wave intensity.

---

## 16.1 Hooke's Law: Stress and Strain Revisited

**Terms:**
- **Restoring force** — force exerted by a deformed system that opposes the displacement and acts to return the system to equilibrium.
- **Force constant (k)** — stiffness measure (N/m); larger k means stiffer system.
- **Elastic potential energy** — energy stored in a Hooke's-law deformation.

**Principles:** When restoring force is directly proportional to displacement, the relation is linear; the minus sign shows force opposes displacement. Oscillation requires a net force (Newton's first law) — otherwise motion would be uniform/linear.

**Equations:**
- Hooke's law (restoring force): `F = -kx`
- Force constant: `k = -F/x`
- Elastic potential energy: `PE_el = ½ k x²`

**Worked examples:**
- *Car suspension* — find spring stiffness from settlement under a known load (k ≈ 6.53 × 10⁴ N/m for an 80 kg passenger causing 1.20 cm settle).
- *Toy gun spring* — compressing a spring stores elastic PE (0.563 J) that converts fully to kinetic energy, launching a 2.00 g projectile at 23.7 m/s. Illustrates PE_el → KE conversion.

**Applications:** vehicle suspensions/shock absorbers, energy storage in toys, lab measurement of stiffness, material deformation in engineering.

## 16.2 Period and Frequency in Oscillations

**Terms:**
- **Periodic motion** — motion that repeats at regular time intervals.
- **Period (T)** — time for one full cycle (seconds).
- **Frequency (f)** — number of cycles per unit time.
- **Cycle** — one complete oscillation.
- **Hertz (Hz)** — SI unit of frequency = 1 cycle/second.

**Equation:** `f = 1/T` (frequency and period are reciprocals).

**Worked examples:**
- *Medical ultrasound* — a very short period (0.400 µs) maps to a very high frequency (2.50 MHz). Period→frequency conversion.
- *Musical pitch* — middle C at 264 Hz has a period ≈ 3.79 ms. Frequency→period conversion linking pitch to timing.

**Applications:** ultrasound imaging, instrument tuning, any periodic frequency–period conversion.

## 16.3 Simple Harmonic Motion: A Special Periodic Motion

**Terms:**
- **Simple harmonic motion (SHM)** — periodic oscillation where the restoring force obeys Hooke's law and there is no dissipation (no friction).
- **Amplitude (X)** — maximum displacement from equilibrium.
- Period and frequency in SHM are **independent of amplitude**.

**Principles:** Net force obeys `F = -kx`, giving symmetric oscillation about equilibrium. Only mass m and force constant k set the period/frequency. Amplitude independence makes oscillators good clocks.

**Equations:**
- Period: `T = 2π √(m/k)`
- Frequency: `f = (1/2π) √(k/m)`
- Position: `x(t) = X cos(2π t / T)`
- Velocity: `v(t) = -v_max sin(2π t / T)`, with `v_max = 2π X / T = X √(k/m)`
- Acceleration: `a(t) = -(kX/m) cos(2π t / T)`

**Worked example:** *Bad shock absorbers* — compute oscillation frequency/period from mass and stiffness; suspension bounces at a predictable rate set by vehicle mass and spring stiffness.

**Applications:** instrument strings (frequency independent of pluck strength), automotive suspension, timekeeping, basis for sinusoidal wave shape.

## 16.4 The Simple Pendulum

**Terms:**
- **Simple pendulum** — small-mass bob on a light string/wire with negligible air resistance and string stretch.
- **Restoring force** — tangential component of gravity on the bob.
- **Small-angle approximation** — for angles below ~15°, sin θ ≈ θ (radians), so the pendulum behaves as SHM.

**Principles:** SHM holds when restoring force stays proportional to displacement (angles < ~15°). String tension cancels the gravity component parallel to the string, leaving only the tangential restoring component.

**Equations:**
- Restoring force (small angle): `F ≈ -m g θ`
- Arc length: `s = L θ`, so `θ = s/L`
- Proportional form: `F ≈ -(mg/L) s`
- Effective spring constant: `k = mg/L`
- Period: `T = 2π √(L/g)`
- Solving for gravity: `g = 4π² L / T²`

**Worked example:** measure local gravitational acceleration from pendulum length and measured period — a 75 cm pendulum, T = 1.7357 s gives g ≈ 9.8281 m/s².

**Applications:** pendulum clocks, gravity surveying (plate tectonics, resource exploration), precision measurement of g.

## 16.5 Energy and the Simple Harmonic Oscillator

**Terms:** elastic potential energy, kinetic energy, undamped motion, amplitude (X).

**Principles:** Without dissipation, total mechanical energy is conserved, cycling between KE and elastic PE.

**Equations:**
- Conservation: `KE + PE = constant`
- Combined: `½ m v² + ½ k x² = constant`
- Velocity: `v = ± √(k/m) √(X² - x²)`
- Maximum velocity: `v_max = √(k/m) · X`
- Pendulum energy form: `½ m L² ω² + ½ m g L θ² = constant`
- Pendulum max angular velocity: `ω_max = √(g/L) · θ_max`

**Worked example:** *Automobile suspension (Ex. 16.6)* — v_max scales directly with amplitude, with √k, and inversely with √m. A 900 kg car with k = 6.53×10⁴ N/m and 0.100 m amplitude gives v_max ≈ 0.852 m/s.

**Applications:** suspension dynamics, AC circuit analogies, oscillation control, structural vibration.

## 16.6 Uniform Circular Motion and Simple Harmonic Motion

**Terms:**
- **Uniform circular motion** — motion at constant angular velocity ω on a circular path.
- **Projection** — the position component (shadow) along one fixed axis of a rotating object.
- **Angular velocity (ω)** — ω = 2π/T.

**Principle:** The projection of uniform circular motion onto any fixed axis is exactly SHM; all SHM properties can be derived from circular motion.

**Equations:**
- Position: `x = X cos θ = X cos(ω t) = X cos(2π t / T)`
- Velocity: `v = v_max √(1 - x²/X²)`
- Period: `T = 2π X / v_max = 2π √(m/k)`
- Angular velocity: `ω = 2π/T`

**Conceptual illustration:** a ball rotating on a turntable casts a shadow whose back-and-forth motion is sinusoidal SHM — making oscillation visually observable.

**Applications:** analyzing wave superposition, modeling oscillators without large apparatus, rotating machinery, teaching.

## 16.7 Damped Harmonic Motion

**Terms:**
- **Damping** — removal of mechanical energy from an oscillator by non-conservative forces, usually dissipated as heat.
- **Critical damping** — damping that returns the system to equilibrium as quickly as possible without overshooting/oscillating.
- **Underdamped** — insufficient damping; overshoots and crosses equilibrium repeatedly with gradually decreasing amplitude.
- **Overdamped** — excessive damping; returns slowly without oscillating, slower than critical.

**Principles:** Non-conservative damping forces continuously remove mechanical energy; work done by damping equals the change in total mechanical energy (negative). Damping force models vary (constant friction vs. velocity-proportional viscous drag).

**Equations:**
- Work–energy (non-conservative): `W_nc = Δ(KE + PE)`
- Frictional damping force: `f = μ_k m g`
- Rest condition (motion ceases): `k x = μ_k m g` → `x = μ_k m g / k`
- Energy balance: `W_nc = ½ k [ (μ_k m g / k)² - X² ]`
- Total distance traveled: `d = (k / 2 μ_k m g) [ X² - (μ_k m g / k)² ]`

**Worked example:** *Friction on a spring-mass system (Ex. 16.7)* — energy conservation with frictional damping; m = 0.200 kg, k = 50.0 N/m, μ_k = 0.0800, X = 0.100 m → f = 0.157 N, total travel d = 1.59 m (~15.9 oscillations). Underdamped; final rest offset from equilibrium because static friction exceeds the diminished restoring force.

**Comparative behavior:**
| Regime | Return speed | Oscillation | Overshoot |
|---|---|---|---|
| Critically damped | Fastest possible | None | None (or exactly one) |
| Underdamped | Fast | Multiple cycles | Repeated crossings |
| Overdamped | Slowest | None | At most one crossing |

**Applications:** shock absorbers (critical damping), measurement instruments/bathroom scales (critical damping for smooth reading), guitar strings (underdamped).

## 16.8 Forced Oscillations and Resonance

**Terms:**
- **Natural frequency (f₀)** — frequency a system oscillates at with no driving and no damping.
- **Driving force** — periodic external force at some driving frequency.
- **Resonance** — driving at the natural frequency produces maximum amplitude; the system "resonates."

**Principles:** Energy transfers most efficiently when driven at f₀. Less damping → higher, narrower resonance peak (more frequency-selective); more damping → lower, broader response. All damping curves peak at the same f₀ but differ in height and width.

**Equations:** No explicit closed-form amplitude formula given; amplitude is treated as a function of driving frequency (peaking at f₀).

**Illustrative examples:**
- *Piano string resonance* — a voiced tone at a string's frequency drives it to vibrate sympathetically.
- *Paddle ball on rubber band* — amplitude grows as driving frequency approaches f₀, peaks at f₀, drops at high frequency.

**Applications:** radio tuning, MRI (~100 MHz radio waves resonating hydrogen nuclei), pushing a swing at its natural frequency, suspension design balancing resonance avoidance vs. comfort, Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940) destructive resonance, breathing at chest-cavity resonance.

## 16.9 Waves

**Terms:**
- **Wave** — disturbance that propagates from its source, transporting energy.
- **Wavelength (λ)** — distance between successive identical points along propagation.
- **Wave velocity (v_w)** — propagation speed of the disturbance.
- **Transverse wave** — disturbance perpendicular to travel direction (guitar string, EM waves).
- **Longitudinal wave** — disturbance parallel to travel direction (sound in air/water, spring compression).

**Principle:** Wave energy moves forward, but medium particles stay localized (e.g., circular paths for water). The disturbance travels; the medium does not flow with it. Propagation speed depends on wavelength and frequency.

**Equations:**
- `f = 1/T`
- Wave speed: `v_w = λ / T = f λ`

**Worked example:** ocean wave with 10.0 m crest-to-crest spacing; a gull bobs up-down in 5.00 s → v_w = 2.00 m/s. Wave speed differs from particle oscillation speed.

**Applications:** ocean waves (mixed transverse-longitudinal), seismic waves (both types, different speeds aid earthquake analysis), sound, light/radio, instrument vibrations.

## 16.10 Superposition and Interference

**Terms:**
- **Superposition** — overlapping waves add (forces add); the resultant is the sum of individual disturbances.
- **Constructive interference** — waves in phase; amplitudes add.
- **Destructive interference** — waves fully out of phase; cancel (zero amplitude for pure destructive).
- **Standing wave** — stationary pattern from two identical counter-propagating waves.
- **Nodes** — points of permanent zero displacement.
- **Antinodes** — points of maximum amplitude.
- **Fundamental frequency (f₁)** — lowest resonant frequency (longest wavelength).
- **Overtones/harmonics** — integer multiples of f₁.
- **Beat frequency** — rate of amplitude fluctuation when two close frequencies superimpose.

**Principles:** Disturbances on the same line add algebraically. A string fixed at both ends resonates when its length fits an integer number of half-wavelengths. Higher tension raises wave speed and all frequencies.

**Equations:**
- Wave at a point: `x = X cos(2π f t)`
- Superposition: `x = X cos(2π f₁ t) + X cos(2π f₂ t)`
- Beat resultant: `x = 2X cos(π f_B t) cos(2π f_ave t)`
- Beat frequency: `f_B = |f₁ - f₂|`, with `f_ave = (f₁ + f₂)/2`
- Fundamental (string fixed both ends): `f₁ = v_w / 2L`
- Harmonics: `f_n = n f₁` (n = 1, 2, 3, …)
- Wavelengths: `λ₁ = 2L`, `λ₂ = L`

**Concepts illustrated:** real rooms mix constructive/destructive interference (stereo speakers); string instruments form standing waves and are tuned via tension; buildings can resonate selectively in earthquakes; beating produces a slow loud/quiet envelope at f_B over a carrier at f_ave.

**Applications:** piano tuning by listening for beats, 12-string guitars/mandolins deliberately detuned for slow beats, ultrasonic imaging/radar beat detection, acoustic room design.

## 16.11 Energy in Waves: Intensity

**Terms:**
- **Intensity (I)** — energy flow rate per unit perpendicular area, in W/m².
- **Power (P)** — energy per unit time (W).
- **Amplitude (X)** — sets the energy content.

**Principles:** Wave energy ∝ amplitude² (W ∝ Fx = kx²), so doubling amplitude quadruples energy. Pure constructive interference doubles amplitude (2X) and so quadruples intensity (I ∝ X²); total energy is conserved through spatial redistribution.

**Equations:**
- Intensity: `I = P / A`
- Energy from intensity: `E = I A t`
- Concentration ratio: `I'/I = A/A'` (power constant)

**Applications:** solar flux (~1300 W/m² above atmosphere, ~700 W/m² at surface), acoustics (90 dB ≈ 10⁻³ W/m²), concentrated sunlight (~140,000 W/m² via magnification), ultrasound deep heating, seismic intensity falling with distance.
