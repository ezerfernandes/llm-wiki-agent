## Chapter Introduction

Energy is a unifying concept across all of physics: it appears in many forms and is always conserved. Crucially, not all energy in a system can be converted into useful work. Work is the mechanism by which energy is transferred between systems or converted among forms. Émilie du Châtelet's experiments dropping projectiles into clay showed penetration depth scaled with the square of the velocity (double speed → 4× depth, triple speed → 9× depth), an early demonstration tied to kinetic energy (E = ½mv²). The chapter also references mass–energy equivalence (E = mc²).

---

## 7.1 Work: The Scientific Definition

**Objectives:** Decide whether displacement is required for work; analyze how the angle between force and displacement determines the sign and size of work.

**Definition — Work (scientific sense):** the product of the component of force along the direction of motion and the distance moved. This is narrower than everyday usage — holding a heavy box still does no work because there is no displacement.

**Conditions for work:** a force acts, displacement occurs, and the angle θ between force and displacement governs the result.

**Equation:**
- W = F d cos θ  (W = work, F = force magnitude, d = displacement magnitude, θ = angle between them)

**Worked example — Lawn Mower (Ex. 7.1):** A push directed 35° below horizontal contributes only its horizontal component to the work; illustrates extracting the motion-aligned component and comparing the result to daily food energy.

**Applications:** carrying horizontally does zero work (force perpendicular to motion, cos 90° = 0); lowering an object does negative work (θ = 180°), removing energy; work links mechanical effort to thermal and chemical energy in the body.

## 7.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work-Energy Theorem

**Objectives:** Explain work as energy transfer and net work as the sum over all forces; state and apply the work-energy theorem.

**Definitions:**
- **Translational kinetic energy:** energy of an object's motion through space, proportional to speed squared — why high-speed impacts are so damaging.
- **Net work:** total work by all forces, either summed individually or computed from net force times displacement.

**Principle — Work-Energy Theorem:** the net work on a system equals its change in kinetic energy; valid regardless of the forces involved.

**Equations:**
- W_net = F_net · d  (force parallel to motion)
- W_net = F_net · d · cos θ  (general)
- W_net = m a d  (via kinematics)
- W_net = ½ m v² − ½ m v₀²  (work-energy theorem)
- KE = ½ m v²

**Worked examples:**
- Ex. 7.2 — compute KE directly from mass and speed.
- Ex. 7.3 — compute net work two ways: from net force, and by summing applied, friction, gravity, and normal-force contributions.
- Ex. 7.4 — use the theorem to find final speed from net work and initial KE.
- Ex. 7.5 — use the theorem to find stopping distance when friction removes all KE.

**Applications:** packages on conveyor belts, friction dissipation, final speeds, stopping distances, energy stored by lifting.

## 7.3 Gravitational Potential Energy

**Objectives:** Explain gravitational PE as work stored against gravity; derive and use PE_g = mgh near Earth; use PE to simplify problems.

**Definitions:**
- **Gravitational potential energy (PE_g):** energy stored in the Earth–object system due to position; a property of the system, not a single object.
- **Reference level:** an arbitrary zero point (often Earth's surface); only differences in PE matter.

**Principles:**
- Work done lifting an object equals the PE gained.
- **Path independence:** ΔPE_g depends only on initial and final heights, not the route taken.
- PE_g converts to KE as objects fall, avoiding explicit force calculations.

**Equations:**
- ΔPE_g = m g h
- W = F d = m g h  (work to lift)
- −ΔPE_g = ΔKE  (energy conversion)
- v = √(2 g |h|)  (speed from rest)
- v = √(2 g |h| + v₀²)  (with initial speed)
- (h positive upward, negative downward)

**Worked examples:**
- Ex. 7.6 — Knee-joint force on landing: PE loss → KE → stopping force over a short compression distance; small distances yield huge forces (injury risk).
- Ex. 7.7a — Roller coaster from rest: speed at the bottom depends only on height drop, not track shape.
- Ex. 7.7b — Roller coaster with initial speed: PE loss adds to initial KE.

**Applications:** shock absorption (bending legs, kangaroo hopping spread force over distance), simpler problem-solving via energy, results independent of mass and path.

## 7.4 Conservative Forces and Potential Energy

**Objectives:** Define conservative force, potential energy, and mechanical energy; describe elastic PE via Hooke's law; show mechanical energy is conserved under conservative forces.

**Definitions:**
- **Conservative force:** work depends only on endpoints, not path (e.g., gravity, spring force); fully recoverable.
- **Potential energy:** stored energy from position, shape, or configuration.
- **Mechanical energy:** sum of kinetic and potential energy.

**Principles:**
- **Hooke's law:** spring force is proportional to deformation, F = k x.
- **Conservation of mechanical energy** (conservative forces only): KE + PE = constant; KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f.
- Work-energy form: ΔKE + ΔPE = 0.

**Equations:**
- PE_s = ½ k x²  (spring/elastic PE; k = spring constant, x = displacement from rest)
- ½ m v² + m g h + ½ k x² = ½ m v_f² + m g h_f + ½ k x_f²

**Worked example — Spring-powered toy car:** spring PE converts to KE and gravitational PE; the car reaches the same speed on a straight slope or a loop — demonstrating path independence.

**Applications:** wind-up toys and clocks, vibrating guitar strings, impact-absorbing sports gear.

## 7.5 Nonconservative Forces

**Objectives:** Define nonconservative forces and their effect on mechanical energy; represent conservative forces via PE and nonconservative forces via work.

**Definitions:**
- **Nonconservative force:** work depends on the path traveled (e.g., friction); cannot be assigned a potential energy. Friction converts mechanical energy into thermal energy that is not fully recoverable; such forces add or remove mechanical energy.

**Principles:**
- General work-energy theorem: net work (conservative + nonconservative) equals ΔKE.
- Total mechanical energy changes by exactly the nonconservative work done.

**Equations:**
- W_net = W_nc + W_c
- W_nc + W_c = ΔKE
- W_nc = ΔKE + ΔPE
- KE_i + PE_i + W_nc = KE_f + PE_f

**Worked examples:**
- Ex. 7.9 — Sliding to a stop on level ground: friction dissipates KE as heat; W_nc = −f d; solve for stopping distance.
- Ex. 7.10 — Sliding up an incline: distance is shorter because gravity also opposes motion; friction and rising PE both consume KE.

**Applications:** stopping distances with friction, motion on inclines with energy loss, why stopping heavier objects takes more work.

## 7.6 Conservation of Energy

**Objectives:** State the conservation-of-energy principle; classify forms of energy; compute efficiency (useful output vs. total input).

**Definitions:**
- **Conservation of energy:** total energy is constant in any process; it can change form or transfer between systems, but the total is unchanged.
- **Efficiency:** ratio of useful output energy/work to total input energy.
- **Other energy (OE):** all non-mechanical forms (thermal, chemical, electrical, nuclear, radiant).
- **Thermal energy:** internal energy from random atomic/molecular motion, tied to temperature.

**Principle — Law of conservation of energy:** energy is neither created nor destroyed, only transformed or transferred.

**Equations:**
- KE_i + PE_i + W_nc + OE_i = KE_f + PE_f + OE_f  (general)
- KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f  (mechanical only, conservative forces)
- Efficiency = (useful work/energy output) / (total energy input) = W_out / E_in

**Forms of energy:** chemical (fuels, food), electrical (batteries), radiant/electromagnetic (sunlight), nuclear (mass→energy), mechanical (KE + PE).

**Problem-solving strategy:** define the system; identify knowns/unknowns; classify forces; use mechanical conservation if all conservative, else the general equation; drop zero terms; pick reference points wisely; check reasonableness.

**Efficiency figures (representative):** electric motors ~98%, gas heaters ~90%, diesel engines ~35%, solar cells ~10%, human cycling ~20%; coal plants convert ~42% of chemical energy to electricity.

**Applications:** food metabolism (chemical → thermal + KE + PE), power generation (chemical → thermal → mechanical → electrical), solar tech (EM → electrical → mechanical).

## 7.7 Power

**Objectives:** Compute power from energy change over time; evaluate electricity use and cost.

**Definitions:**
- **Power (P):** rate of doing work or transferring energy; SI unit watt (W) = 1 joule/second.
- **Watt (W):** one joule per second.
- **Kilowatt-hour (kW·h):** commercial energy unit = power (kW) × time (h).

**Principle:** more power means more work/energy delivered in less time (e.g., a powerful car burns fuel quickly). A 60-W bulb dissipates 60 J/s.

**Equations:**
- P = W / t
- E = P t  (energy consumed)
- P = (½ m v_f² + m g h) / t  (power from mechanical energy change)

**Worked examples:**
- Ex. 7.11 — Stair-climbing power: mechanical power from gained KE and gravitational PE; gravitational work usually dominates.
- Ex. 7.12 — Electricity cost: power (kW) × time (h) = kW·h, then × utility rate = cost.

**Applications:** identifying high-draw appliances (water heaters, AC); coal plant producing 1000 MW consumes ~2500 MW of fuel, wasting ~1500 MW as heat; compact fluorescents give ~4× light per watt vs. incandescent; brief human exertion can exceed 1 horsepower.

## 7.8 Work, Energy, and Power in Humans

**Objectives:** Contrast energy use at rest vs. during activity; quantify conversion of food energy into mechanical work.

**Definitions:**
- **Metabolic rate:** rate the body uses food energy to live and perform activities.
- **Basal metabolic rate (BMR):** total energy-conversion rate at rest, distributed across organs.
- **Useful work:** mechanical effort done on the external environment, excluding internal processes.

**Principles:**
- Food chemical energy splits into external work, thermal energy (largest share, ~75% of daily calories), and stored fat.
- Energy expenditure tracks oxygen consumption: ~20 kJ per liter of O₂, roughly independent of food type.

**Equations:**
- Time = Energy / Power  (e.g., 1000 kJ ÷ 400 W ≈ 2500 s ≈ 42 min)
- Fat metabolized = (energy deficit in kJ) × (1.0 g fat / 39 kJ)  (e.g., 1000 kJ → ~26 g)

**Applications & data:** modest caloric overages require substantial exercise to offset; BMR distribution (Table 7.4) roughly — liver & spleen 27%, brain 19%, skeletal muscle 18%, other organs 36%; ~25% of body energy maintains cellular ion gradients and nerve signaling, ultimately becoming heat.

## 7.9 World Energy Use

**Objectives:** Distinguish renewable from nonrenewable resources; explain why unavoidable degradation of energy to heat motivates conservation.

**Definitions:**
- **Renewable sources:** effectively inexhaustible — hydroelectric, wind, solar, biomass.
- **Nonrenewable sources:** finite fossil fuels (oil, natural gas, coal), ~85% of current global use, emitting CO₂ when burned.
- **Energy conservation:** reducing use via behavior (lower thermostats) or efficiency (efficient vehicles).

**Principle — Law of conservation of energy:** total energy in an isolated system is constant. Although energy is conserved, most transformations produce waste heat and lose usefulness for work — the rationale for conserving resources.

**Economic correlation:** higher GDP per capita generally corresponds to higher energy use per capita.

**Applications & data:** oil ~35% of world energy; the U.S. (~4.25% of population) uses ~21% of global oil; Germany targets 65% renewable power by 2030; China leads in solar farms and hydro; India runs the largest solar-cooking program; diversified renewables speed grid recovery after disasters (Katrina, Sandy, 2021 Texas winter storm).
