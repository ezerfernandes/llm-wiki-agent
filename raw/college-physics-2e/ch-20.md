## Chapter Introduction

This chapter moves from electrostatics into dynamic electricity: how moving charge (current) through conductors powers practical systems from household devices to industrial power grids. Core themes: measuring current, applying Ohm's law, distinguishing resistance from resistivity, computing electrical power and energy cost, comparing AC and DC, and the physiological effects of current.

**Equations introduced chapter-wide:** V = IR; P = IV; P = I²R; ρ = RA/L.

---

## 20.1 Current

**Objectives:** define electric current and the ampere; relate conventional current direction to charge-carrier motion; use drift velocity to compute current.

**Definitions:**
- **Electric current (I):** rate at which charge passes through a cross-section.
- **Ampere (A):** SI unit of current; 1 A = 1 C/s.
- **Drift velocity (v_d):** average velocity of charge carriers through a conductor; very slow (~10⁻⁴ m/s) compared with the signal propagation speed (~10⁸ m/s).
- **Conventional current:** direction positive charge would flow; opposite to electron motion in metals.
- **Free electrons:** loosely bound electrons in metals that move among atoms.

**Principles:**
- Conventional current flows + to − externally; electrons drift the opposite way in metals.
- Electric signal propagates near light speed because charges push neighbors immediately, though individual carriers drift slowly.
- Charge does not accumulate in a conductor: carriers entering force an equal number out, enabling near-instant signal transmission.
- Carrier collisions with atoms transfer energy to the conductor as heat.

**Equations:**
- I = ΔQ / Δt
- 1 A = 1 C/s
- I = nqAv_d  (n = free-charge number density, q = charge per carrier, A = cross-sectional area)

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- Ex 20.1a — current magnitude: 720 C in 4 s → 180 A (large starter-motor current).
- Ex 20.1b — time from current: 1 C at 0.3 mA → ~3330 s (small device draws little charge).
- Ex 20.2 — microscopic→macroscopic: 0.3 mA ≈ 1.88 × 10¹⁵ electrons/s.
- Ex 20.3 — drift velocity: 12-gauge copper, 20 A → v_d ≈ −4.53 × 10⁻⁴ m/s.

**Applications:** fuse/breaker ratings, household wiring gauges, filament heating, signal-vs-carrier-speed paradox.

## 20.2 Ohm's Law: Resistance and Simple Circuits

**Objectives:** experimental basis of Ohm's law; compute V, I, R; identify ohmic materials; analyze simple circuits.

**Definitions:**
- **Voltage source:** battery, generator, or outlet that creates a potential difference driving current.
- **Resistance (R):** opposition to current; analogous to mechanical friction; unit ohm (Ω).
- **Ohmic materials:** resistance independent of applied voltage/current (e.g., copper, aluminum).
- **Resistor:** any object with simple resistance.
- **Voltage drop:** potential difference across a resistor carrying current.

**Principles:**
- Ohm's law: current is directly proportional to applied voltage.
- Current is inversely proportional to resistance (double R → half I).
- Energy conservation: in a simple circuit the source energy is fully dissipated in the resistor (usually as heat).

**Equations:**
- I = V/R
- R = V/I
- V = IR
- 1 Ω = 1 V/A

**Worked example (concept):** Car headlight — 2.50 A at 12.0 V gives R = 4.80 Ω; rearranging Ohm's law for an unknown. Note: cold filament resistance differs from operating resistance (temperature effect).

**Applications:** insulators ~10¹² Ω+; human heart ~10³ Ω; copper wire ~10⁻⁵ Ω; circuit design and troubleshooting.

## 20.3 Resistance and Resistivity

**Objectives:** treat resistivity as a material property; compute resistance for various geometries; use temperature coefficient to predict resistance changes.

**Definitions:**
- **Resistivity (ρ):** intrinsic material property quantifying opposition to charge flow; independent of shape/size.
- **Temperature coefficient of resistivity (α):** fractional change in resistivity per unit temperature change.
- Material classes: **conductors** (low ρ, abundant carriers), **semiconductors** (intermediate ρ, carrier density depends on doping), **insulators** (high ρ, bound charges).

**Principles:**
- Resistance increases with length (more collisions) and decreases with cross-sectional area (more parallel paths).
- In conductors, higher temperature → more atomic vibration → more collisions → higher resistivity. Some materials become superconductors (ρ = 0) at very low temperature.
- In semiconductors, higher temperature → more free carriers → lower resistivity (opposite sign).

**Equations:**
- R = ρ L / A
- ρ = ρ₀ (1 + α ΔT)
- R = R₀ (1 + α ΔT)

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- Ex 20.5 — filament diameter: solve R = ρL/A for A, then geometry → diameter.
- Ex 20.6 — hot-filament resistance: apply R = R₀(1 + αΔT) over a large ΔT (room temp to ~2850 °C); dramatic resistance increase.

**Applications:** incandescent filaments; thermistors (exploit strong semiconductor temperature dependence); temperature-independent standards (manganin, near-zero α); superconductor/cryogenic research.

## 20.4 Electric Power and Energy

**Objectives:** compute power dissipated in resistors and delivered by sources; determine electricity cost.

**Definitions:**
- **Electric power (P):** rate of electrical energy conversion/use; unit watt (W).
- **Kilowatt-hour (kW·h):** billing unit of energy; 1 kW·h = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.

**Principles:**
- Power depends on voltage, current, and resistance; higher-wattage devices draw more current at the same voltage.
- Doubling the voltage across a fixed resistor quadruples power (V appears squared).

**Equations:**
- P = IV
- P = V²/R
- P = I²R
- E = Pt
- cost = power × time × rate

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- Hot vs. cold filament power: temperature-dependent resistance causes different power at startup vs. steady state.
- Cost comparison: lower-wattage, longer-life LED/CFL bulbs reduce total cost despite higher purchase price.

**Applications:** 12 V / 20 A auto outlet → 240 W; LED/CFL use 4–10× less energy than incandescent; utility billing in kW·h.

## 20.5 Alternating Current versus Direct Current

**Objectives:** distinguish AC from DC; compute rms voltage, current, and average power; explain why AC dominates distribution.

**Definitions:**
- **Direct current (DC):** charge flows in one direction at (typically) constant voltage.
- **Alternating current (AC):** charge flow periodically reverses, usually sinusoidally.
- **Root mean square (rms):** averaging method (square, average, square-root) used because the simple average of a sinusoid is zero.

**Principles:**
- In a purely resistive AC circuit, voltage and current are in phase.
- Instantaneous power fluctuates; average values are used in practice.
- AC dominates because transformers step voltage up/down efficiently (vs. DC).

**Equations:**
- V = V₀ sin(2πft)
- I = I₀ sin(2πft), with I₀ = V₀/R
- I_rms = I₀/√2
- V_rms = V₀/√2
- P_ave = ½ I₀V₀ = I_rms·V_rms = V_rms²/R = I_rms²R
- I_rms = V_rms/R

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- Peak vs. rms: 120 V rms household → peak ≈ 170 V.
- Transmission losses: high-voltage transmission lowers I for the same power, reducing I²R line losses.

**Applications:** 120 V / 240 V rms residential power; transmission stepped up to 330 kV+; fluorescent lamps flicker 120×/s (imperceptible).

## 20.6 Electric Hazards and the Human Body

**Objectives:** distinguish thermal and shock hazards; analyze current-level effects on physiology.

**Definitions:**
- **Thermal hazard:** excess electrical power producing unwanted heat (e.g., in-wall fires).
- **Shock hazard:** current passing through a person; painful to lethal.
- **Short circuit:** low-resistance path between terminals → high power dissipation.
- **Microshock sensitivity:** heightened danger when skin resistance is bypassed (catheter, pacemaker leads); effects at ~1/1000 of normal currents.
- **Ventricular fibrillation:** disorganized, ineffective heart rhythm; often fatal.

**Principles:**
- Short-circuit heating: P = V²/r (low r → extreme heat).
- Wire heating: P = I²R_w.
- Current magnitude dominates shock severity (path, duration, frequency fixed).
- Skin resistance varies widely (dry ~200 kΩ; wet ~10 kΩ), changing current via Ohm's law.
- Current follows the lowest-resistance path toward ground.

**Current effects (approximate):** 1 mA barely felt; 5 mA max safe; 10–20 mA can't-let-go muscle contraction; 50 mA pain; 100–300 mA ventricular fibrillation (often fatal); 300 mA+ burns; ~6 A sustained heart contraction and breathing paralysis (reversible if removed).

**Safety devices:** fuses (melt to break circuit permanently); circuit breakers (bimetallic strip, resettable). High-voltage systems use insulating gas and arc suppression.

**Modifying factors:** frequency (peak danger ~50–60 Hz; high frequency stays on skin); duration (longer = worse); path (organs involved); wetness (lowers resistance, raises current). Body tissue conducts well (water + electrolytes).

## 20.7 Nerve Conduction–Electrocardiograms

**Objectives:** describe electrical signal propagation along neurons; analyze myelin's effect on velocity/efficiency; interpret ECG components.

**Definitions:**
- **Bioelectricity:** electrical phenomena in living systems (nerve/muscle cells).
- **Neuron:** cell with dendrites (receive) and an axon (transmit).
- **Synapse:** junction transferring signals between neurons.
- **Resting potential:** membrane voltage at rest, ≈ −90 mV (inside negative).
- **Action potential:** voltage pulse from a permeability change → depolarization then repolarization.
- **Depolarization:** charge reversal from Na⁺ influx.
- **Repolarization:** return to resting potential via K⁺ efflux.
- **Myelin sheath:** insulating fatty layer around axon segments, gapped by nodes of Ranvier.
- **Nerve impulse:** propagating action potential.
- **Electrocardiogram (ECG/EKG):** recording of heart depolarization/repolarization voltages.

**Principles:**
- Ion concentration gradients (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻) drive diffusion across the semipermeable membrane.
- Coulomb force opposes complete diffusion, setting up charge layers and a membrane voltage.
- Active transport (Na⁺/K⁺ pump) uses ~25% of cellular ATP to maintain gradients.
- Saltatory conduction: in myelinated axons signals jump node-to-node, regenerating with low energy and high speed.

**Equations:**
- E = V/d  (membrane field; d ≈ 8 nm → E ≈ 11 MV/m)

**Processes:** stimulation opens Na⁺ permeability → influx (diffusion + field) → local depolarization → membrane closes to Na⁺, K⁺ efflux repolarizes → pulse spreads, propagating ~1 m/s unmyelinated, ~100 m/s myelinated.

**ECG waveform:** P wave (atrial depolarization/contraction); QRS complex (ventricular depolarization; hides atrial repolarization); T wave (ventricular repolarization). Lead II = right arm to left leg.

**Clinical applications:** multiple sclerosis (myelin destruction impairs conduction); 12-lead ECG (depolarization vector components; infarcts distort potentials); cardiac pacemaker (sinoatrial node).
