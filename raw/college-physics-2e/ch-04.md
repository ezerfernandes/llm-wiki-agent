## Chapter Introduction: Dynamics — Newton's Laws of Motion

This chapter develops how forces cause changes in motion, distinguishing **kinematics** (describing motion via velocity and acceleration without addressing causes) from **dynamics** (analyzing the forces that produce motion). It introduces Newton's three laws of motion and applies them across terrestrial and space-based systems.

Key terms: dynamics, kinematics, inertia (resistance to changes in motion), and force (an agent producing acceleration or deformation).

The three laws in brief:
1. First Law (Inertia): objects keep constant velocity unless acted on by a net external force.
2. Second Law: net force produces acceleration proportional to that force and inversely proportional to mass.
3. Third Law: forces occur in equal-magnitude, opposite-direction pairs.

Scope of classical (Newtonian) mechanics: valid when objects move much slower than light and are larger than molecular scales (~10⁻⁹ m). Relativity and quantum mechanics govern regimes outside these limits. The chapter also covers normal force, tension, and other force types via systematic problem-solving.

## 4.1 Development of Force Concept

**Learning objective:** Articulate what physicists mean by "force."

**Force:** a push or pull on an object, with both magnitude and direction — a vector quantity. Examples span from very large (cannon firing a projectile) to subtle (Earth's gravity on a small creature).

**Free-body diagram:** a visual technique isolating a body as a single point, showing only the external forces acting on it. Internal forces are excluded since they don't affect the body's motion.

**Restoring force:** the force a stretched spring exerts as it returns to its undistorted shape.

**Vector addition of forces:** multiple forces acting at once combine vectorially (head-to-tail), not algebraically, producing a resultant (net) force.

**Force standardization:** force magnitudes can be measured against a reproducible standard, e.g., the restoring force of a stretched spring as a reference unit.

Applications: ice-skater dynamics (multiple pushes in different directions), spring scales, magnetic forces between current-carrying conductors.

## 4.2 Newton's First Law of Motion: Inertia

**Learning objectives:** clarify mass and inertia; understand Newton's first law.

**Inertia:** the property whereby an object at rest stays at rest, or an object in motion keeps a constant velocity, unless acted on by an external force.

**Mass:** a quantitative measure of the amount of matter in an object (count and types of atoms/molecules). Mass is invariant with location (Earth, orbit, Moon) — unlike weight.

**Net external force:** the cause that changes an object's velocity (magnitude or direction).

**Newton's First Law:** A body at rest stays at rest, and a body in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted on by a net external force. It establishes that changes in velocity require a net external force.

Friction is a common cause: objects on rough surfaces decelerate due to friction; on nearly frictionless surfaces (lubricated surfaces, air-hockey tables) motion persists much longer.

Applications: sliding objects on surfaces with varying friction; air-hockey pucks; orbiting satellites; blood circulation from the heart.

## 4.3 Newton's Second Law of Motion: Concept of a System

**Learning objectives:** distinguish net force, external force, and system; state and apply Newton's second law; compute weight.

**Net force:** vector sum of all external forces on a system (found graphically or by components).
**External force:** acts from outside the system (vs. internal forces between system parts).
**System:** a defined set of objects; boundaries must be set to classify forces as external vs. internal.
**Friction:** a resistive force opposing relative motion between touching surfaces.
**Weight:** the gravitational force on an object from a nearby massive body.
**Free-fall:** state where only gravity acts (air resistance negligible).

Newton's second law: acceleration is directly proportional to net external force and inversely proportional to mass; internal forces are ignored for system motion.

**Equations:**
- a ∝ F_net
- a ∝ 1/m
- a = F_net / m
- F_net = ma
- 1 N = 1 kg·m/s² (1 N ≈ 0.225 lb)
- Weight: w = mg, with g = 9.80 m/s² near Earth's surface

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- Lawn mower acceleration: direct application of F_net = ma to find acceleration from applied force and mass.
- Rocket sled thrust: solving for an unknown force when acceleration and friction are known; multiple forces sum to the net force.

Applications: bathroom scales (spring force measures weight); rocket sleds testing human tolerance to acceleration; why equal forces give different accelerations on a basketball vs. a car; weight variation across celestial bodies (Moon ≈ 1/6 of Earth's surface gravity).

## 4.4 Newton's Third Law of Motion: Symmetry in Forces

**Learning objectives:** understand that forces occur in matched pairs; use the law to choose systems and solve motion problems.

**Newton's Third Law:** When one body exerts a force on a second body, the first experiences a force equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

**Action–reaction:** the original force is the "action," the response is the "reaction." Forces always come in pairs; no object exerts a force without receiving one.

**Key insight:** equal-and-opposite forces do NOT cancel, because they act on *different* objects. Only external forces relative to a chosen system affect that system's motion, so system selection determines the analysis.

**Equations:** a = F_net / m; F_net = ma.

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- Swimmer pushing off a pool wall: the swimmer accelerates because the wall's reaction force acts on the swimmer (not the wall).
- Professor pushing an equipment cart: choosing the system determines which forces are external — only part of the floor reaction force transfers to accelerate the cart.

Applications: walking (floor reaction propels the person); car acceleration (tires push ground back, ground pushes car forward); rocket propulsion (expel gas backward, get forward thrust); flight/lift (push air down/back); impact in combat sports.

## 4.5 Normal, Tension, and Other Examples of Forces

**Learning objectives:** characterize normal and tension forces; apply Newton's laws to varied forces; resolve weight into components.

**Normal force (N):** a contact force perpendicular to a surface. For a stationary object on a horizontal support, the support pushes up with magnitude equal to the object's weight. "Normal" means perpendicular.

**Tension (T):** a pulling force transmitted along a flexible connector (rope, cable, chain), acting parallel to its length. Tension can only pull, never push.

**Weight components on an incline (angle θ):**
- Parallel (down-slope): w∥ = mg sin(θ)
- Perpendicular (into slope): w⊥ = mg cos(θ)

**Newton's second law on inclines:** resolve forces along axes parallel and perpendicular to the slope; acceleration occurs only along the parallel direction.

**Equations:**
- w∥ = mg sin(θ), w⊥ = mg cos(θ)
- Acceleration down a frictionless incline: a = g sin(θ)
- With friction: a = (mg sin(θ) − f) / m
- Tension supporting a symmetric load via a sagging connector: T = w / (2 sin(θ))
- General tension from a perpendicular force at the midpoint: T = F⊥ / (2 sin(θ))
- Static equilibrium of a hanging mass: T = mg

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- Skier on a slope: decomposing weight into incline-aligned components; friction reduces down-slope acceleration; acceleration depends on angle, not mass.
- Tightrope walker: a nearly horizontal flexible connector under a perpendicular load develops enormous tension (the smaller the sag angle, the larger the tension).

Applications: finger tendons redirect muscle force through joints; bicycle brake cables redirect tension; suspension-bridge cables (catenary curves); vehicle recovery using small perpendicular force on a near-horizontal chain to produce large tension.

## 4.6 Problem-Solving Strategies

**Learning objective:** apply a systematic method to Newton's-laws problems.

**Four-step framework:**
1. Identify the physics and sketch the situation; draw force arrows whose lengths/directions are proportional to the actual forces.
2. Define the system of interest and draw a **free-body diagram** showing only external forces (not velocity or acceleration); internal forces are excluded. Choosing the system determines which forces are external.
3. Apply Newton's second law:
   - If acceleration = 0 in a direction: F_net = 0 in that direction (equilibrium).
   - If acceleration ≠ 0: F_net = ma in that direction.
   - For multi-directional problems, decompose forces onto convenient axes (e.g., parallel/perpendicular to an incline).
4. Check reasonableness: confirm the result makes sense and units are consistent (force in newtons).

**Definitions:** system of interest, free-body diagram, external forces, internal forces.

Application: Tarzan hanging stationary from a vine — tension balances weight (equilibrium analysis).

## 4.7 Further Applications of Newton's Laws of Motion

**Learning objectives:** find unknowns in more complex force systems; connect kinematics with Newton's laws.

**Drag force:** a frictional force exerted by fluids (air, water) that opposes an object's motion.
**Normal force:** perpendicular contact force from a surface.
**System of interest:** the object(s) chosen for analysis.

The three laws are applied together. Forces can be combined from perpendicular components and unknown resistive forces solved via the second law.

**Equations:**
- Resultant of perpendicular components: F = √(Fx² + Fy²)
- Direction: θ = tan⁻¹(Fy/Fx)
- F_net = ma
- Drag force from applied force and motion: F_D = F_app − ma
- Supporting force with acceleration: F_s = ma + mg

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- Tugboat and barge: combine perpendicular force vectors and solve for an unknown resistance force.
- Traffic light tensions: decompose tensions at unequal angles into components; apply equilibrium separately to horizontal and vertical axes.
- Elevator scale reading: apparent weight changes with acceleration — the normal force depends on net acceleration, not just gravity.
- Soccer player acceleration: combine kinematics (acceleration from velocity change) with dynamics (force from mass × acceleration).

Applications: tugboat/marine propulsion; suspended loads in structural engineering; elevator passenger experience; athletic biomechanics (ground reaction forces).

## 4.8 Extended Topic: The Four Basic Forces — An Introduction

**Learning objective:** understand the four basic forces underlying all natural phenomena.

**Key concepts:**
- **Force field:** a region where an object experiences a position-dependent force, independent of whether a second object is present; fields transmit forces without contact.
- **Action at a distance:** objects exert forces across space through fields rather than direct contact.
- **Carrier particles:** elementary particles mediating fundamental forces via exchange.
- **Electroweak force:** unified description of electromagnetic and weak nuclear forces under extreme density/temperature.

**The four fundamental forces:**

| Force | Relative strength | Range | Nature | Carrier particle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gravitational | ~10⁻³⁸ | Infinite | Attractive only | Graviton (proposed) |
| Electromagnetic | ~10⁻² | Infinite | Attractive & repulsive | Photon |
| Weak nuclear | ~10⁻⁶ | < 10⁻¹⁸ m | Attractive & repulsive | W⁺, W⁻, Z⁰ |
| Strong nuclear | 1 | ~10⁻¹⁵ m | Attractive & repulsive | Gluons |

**Characteristics:**
- Gravity: extremely weak but always attractive; dominates at astronomical scales; curves spacetime near massive bodies.
- Electromagnetism: unifies electric and magnetic phenomena (19th-century unification); nearly cancels for neutral macroscopic objects.
- Weak and strong nuclear forces: act at subatomic distances, governing nuclear stability, element abundance, and energy release in decay/nuclear reactions.

**Principles:**
- All observed phenomena derive from these four forces.
- Nearly all everyday forces (friction, tension) are electromagnetic in origin at the atomic/molecular level; only gravity is fundamentally distinct among everyday experience.
- Forces propagate through fields, allowing precise mathematical field descriptions.

**Equation referenced:** w = mg (Earth's surface gravitational field relating weight to mass and g).

**Unification:** electromagnetic and weak forces are unified (electroweak) at high energies; Grand Unified Theories (GUTs) aim to connect all four, though gravity remains difficult to include.

**Applications/detection:**
- Gravitational waves: predicted by general relativity; first detected 2015 by LIGO from merging black holes (~29 and ~36 solar masses); LIGO uses laser interferometry across facilities ~3000 km apart.
- LISA: planned space-based EU/US detector (2030s), three satellites in an equilateral triangle (~5 million km sides), targeting supermassive black-hole signals.
- Large Hadron Collider (27 km circumference): probes force carriers and early-universe conditions at very high energies.
