## Introduction to Geometric Optics

**Optics** is the area of physics dealing with how light is generated and propagated and how it interacts with matter. When light interacts with objects that are several times larger than its wavelength, it behaves as though it travels in straight-line **rays** — this regime is called **geometric optics** (the ray model). The complementary regime, where wave behavior (interference, diffraction) dominates because objects are comparable to or smaller than the wavelength, is **wave optics**.

Four central topics: the law of reflection, the law of refraction, total internal reflection, and dispersion. Applications span vision and photography, telescopes and microscopes, fiber-optic communication, solar ovens, and natural phenomena like rainbows.

## 25.1 The Ray Aspect of Light

**Objective:** identify the three ways light travels from source to destination.

A **ray** is a straight line originating at a point; light is modeled as rays when interacting with objects much larger than its wavelength (roughly, wavelength below ~1 micrometer relative to object size).

Three ways light reaches us:
1. Direct transmission through empty space (e.g., sunlight to Earth).
2. Transmission through a material medium (air, glass, water).
3. Reflection off a surface (mirrors, objects).

Light travels in straight lines except where it meets matter, at which point it reflects or refracts and then resumes straight-line travel. Two laws govern these direction changes: the law of reflection and the law of refraction.

## 25.2 The Law of Reflection

**Reflection** is the bouncing of light off a surface. 

- **Specular reflection** is the organized reflection from a smooth surface (mirror, polished metal), producing directional, focused reflections.
- **Diffuse reflection** is scattering in many directions from a rough surface (paper, walls, skin); because light strikes different parts of an uneven surface at different angles, it reflects in many directions. Diffuse reflection is why we see most everyday objects from many viewpoints.

**Law of reflection:** the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence, both measured from the normal (perpendicular) to the surface at the point of incidence.

Equation: θ_r = θ_i

Applications: mirror images, reflecting telescopes, seeing ordinary objects, moonlight on water (mixing specular and diffuse reflection).

## 25.3 The Law of Refraction

**Objective:** find the index of refraction given the speed of light in a medium.

**Refraction** is the change in direction ("bending") of a light ray as it passes between media in which light travels at different speeds.

**Index of refraction (n):** dimensionless quantity measuring how much a medium slows light relative to vacuum; always n ≥ 1.

Equations:
- Speed of light in vacuum: c = 2.99792458 × 10^8 m/s ≈ 3.00 × 10^8 m/s
- Index of refraction: n = c / v  (v = speed of light in the medium)
- Speed in a medium: v = c / n
- **Snell's law (law of refraction):** n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂  (angles measured from the normal)

Worked examples (concept only):
- Speed calculation: use n to compute light's speed in a material (zircon).
- Index determination: solve for an unknown n from measured incident and refracted angles.
- Large angular change: greater index difference produces greater bending (air→diamond bends more than air→water).

Applications: fish appearing displaced in a tank, lenses, optical fibers, prism dispersion, gemstone sparkle (zircon vs. diamond).

## 25.4 Total Internal Reflection

**Total internal reflection** occurs when light traveling in a higher-index medium strikes a boundary with a lower-index medium at an angle large enough that all the light reflects back into the first medium (none refracts out).

**Critical angle (θc):** the incidence angle that produces a refracted angle of exactly 90°. For incidence angles above θc, total internal reflection occurs. Requires n₁ > n₂.

Equations:
- Snell's law: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂
- At the critical angle (θ₂ = 90°): n₁ sin θc = n₂
- Critical angle: θc = sin⁻¹(n₂ / n₁), valid for n₁ > n₂

Worked example (concept only): critical angle for a polystyrene fiber (n = 1.49) in air (n = 1.00) is ≈ 42.2°; any ray exceeding that angle reflects internally, so no silvering is needed.

Applications:
- **Fiber optics:** light guided down plastic/glass fibers by repeated total internal reflection; carries telecom, internet, cable signals. **Cladding** (lower-index coating) keeps light confined and prevents crosstalk between fibers.
- **Endoscopy:** fiber bundles transmit images for minimally invasive diagnostics and laser delivery.
- **Diamond brilliance:** critical angle ≈ 24.4° traps light until it exits through specific facets, concentrating it for sparkle.
- **Corner reflectors:** perpendicular surfaces send light back parallel to its source (bike reflectors, lunar ranging retroreflectors, binoculars, periscopes).

## 25.5 Dispersion: The Rainbow and Prisms

**Dispersion** is the spreading of white light into its full spectrum because the index of refraction varies with wavelength. Any wave can disperse if its speed depends on wavelength.

White light contains all visible wavelengths, from violet (~400 nm) to red (~700 nm). Because n increases as wavelength decreases, violet bends more than red, so a **prism** separates white light into a spectrum from red through violet.

**Rainbow formation** in water droplets: refraction on entry (wavelength-dependent), reflection off the back surface (no wavelength dependence), then refraction on exit (wavelength-dependent again). Different wavelengths emerge at slightly different angles, producing visible color bands.

Applications/trade-offs: lasers emit nearly pure wavelengths and disperse little (good for telecom; white light is problematic because dispersion spreads signals in time). Dispersion of light from space can reveal information about intervening matter.

## 25.6 Image Formation by Lenses

**Objectives:** apply ray tracing to thin lenses; draw image-formation diagrams; compute optical power from focal length.

Definitions:
- **Converging (convex) lens:** bends parallel rays toward a single **focal point (F)** on the far side.
- **Diverging (concave) lens:** spreads parallel rays so they appear to come from a focal point on the near side.
- **Focal length (f):** distance from lens center to focal point.
- **Thin lens:** thin enough that rays bend only once, with negligible dispersion/aberration.
- **Real image:** rays physically converge; can be projected on a screen.
- **Virtual image:** rays only appear to come from the image; cannot be projected.
- **Magnification (m):** ratio of image to object height; sign indicates orientation.

Principles: light bends toward the normal entering a denser medium and away when exiting (net convergence/divergence depending on shape). Ray paths are reversible — a source at the focal point produces parallel outgoing rays.

Equations:
- Optical power: P = 1/f  (f in meters; units diopters, D = m⁻¹)
- Thin lens equation: 1/d_o + 1/d_i = 1/f
- Magnification: m = h_i / h_o = − d_i / d_o  (positive m = upright; negative = inverted)

Thin-lens ray-tracing rules:
1. A ray parallel to the axis passes through F (converging) or appears to come from F (diverging).
2. A ray through the lens center continues undeviated.
3. A ray through the near focal point exits parallel (converging).
4. A ray heading toward the far focal point exits parallel (diverging).

Image-formation cases:
- Case 1 — convex, d_o > f: real, inverted, size varies.
- Case 2 — convex, d_o < f: virtual, upright, magnified (m > 1).
- Case 3 — concave (any d_o): virtual, upright, diminished (m < 1).

Worked examples (concept only):
- Magnifying-glass power: focal length → optical power in diopters; shorter f = stronger lens.
- Light-bulb image: ray tracing matches the equation; object beyond f gives a real, inverted, magnified image.
- Magnifier: object inside f gives an upright, enlarged virtual image.
- Concave lens: always a virtual, upright, diminished image.

Applications: cameras/projectors (Case 1), magnifying glasses (Case 2), vision correction (convex for farsight, concave for nearsight), lighthouse beams (reverse Case 1), the eye forming retinal images.

## 25.7 Image Formation by Mirrors

**Objectives:** describe plane-mirror images; use ray diagrams for spherical mirrors; compute focal length and magnification from radius of curvature and object/image distances.

Definitions:
- **Real image:** reflected rays actually converge; projectable.
- **Virtual image:** reflected rays only appear to come from the image; not projectable (e.g., behind a plane mirror).
- **Focal length (f):** distance from mirror to focal point.
- **Radius of curvature (R):** radius of the sphere forming the mirror.
- **Magnification (m):** image height / object height; negative means inverted.

Principles: all mirror behavior follows the law of reflection (θ_r = θ_i). The thin-mirror approximation (mirror small relative to R) gives a well-defined focal point.

Equations:
- Focal length / radius: f = R / 2
- Mirror equation: 1/d_o + 1/d_i = 1/f
- Mirror power: P = 1/f
- Magnification: m = − d_i / d_o

Mirror ray-tracing rules:
1. A ray parallel to the axis reflects through F (concave) or appears to come from F (convex).
2. A ray to the mirror's center reflects at an equal angle about the axis.
3. A ray through F reflects parallel to the axis (reversible, both types).

Image-formation cases:
- Case 1 — concave, d_o > f: real, inverted, magnified or reduced.
- Case 2 — concave, d_o < f: virtual, upright, magnified.
- Case 3 — convex (any position): virtual, upright, reduced.

Worked examples (concept only):
- Room heater: solve for object distance from image distance and focal length (concentrating thermal radiation).
- Solar concentrator: parabolic/cylindrical mirror geometry for energy collection.
- Keratometer: magnification of a convex reflection reveals corneal curvature (inverse R–m relationship).

Applications: magnifying makeup/dental mirrors (Case 2), wide-view security/shop convex mirrors (Case 3), flashlight/headlight reflectors (concave, parallel beam), parabolic-trough solar thermal systems, corneal-curvature measurement.
