## Introduction

Momentum is treated as a conserved physical quantity that governs how objects interact, especially during collisions. Momentum is a vector combining an object's mass and velocity; everyday usage matches the scientific meaning in that greater mass and speed produce greater momentum. Conservation of momentum holds in isolated systems and, like energy conservation, provides deep insight into how the natural world behaves. The chapter covers linear momentum and force, impulse, conservation of momentum, elastic and inelastic collisions in one dimension, two-dimensional collisions of point masses, and rocket propulsion.

## 8.1 Linear Momentum and Force

### Objectives
- Define linear momentum.
- Relate momentum to applied force.
- State Newton's second law in momentum form.
- Compute momentum from mass and velocity.

### Definitions
- **Linear momentum (p)**: product of an object's mass and velocity; a vector pointing along the velocity. SI unit: kg·m/s. Momentum grows with both mass and velocity.

### Laws / Principles
- **Newton's second law (momentum form)**: the net external force equals the rate of change of a system's momentum. More general than F = ma because it also handles systems of varying mass.

### Equations
- p = mv
- F_net = Δp/Δt
- Δp = mΔv = m(v_f − v_i)
- F_net = m(Δv/Δt) = ma  (reduces to classical form for constant mass)

### Worked Examples (concept only)
- **Football player vs. football**: compares momentum when mass and velocity vary inversely; the player's much larger mass gives ~86× the ball's momentum despite the ball's higher speed, so catching barely changes the player's motion.
- **Tennis serve force**: uses F_net = Δp/Δt to find the average force during a brief racquet-ball contact (~660 N) directly from momentum change and contact time, without needing acceleration.

### Applications
Explains why massive slow objects resist motion changes more than light fast ones; framework extends to variable-mass systems (rockets) and to quantum mechanics.

## 8.2 Impulse

### Objectives
- Define impulse.
- Recognize impulse in everyday situations.
- Use force-vs-time graphs to find average effective force.
- Compute average force and impulse from mass, velocity, and time data.

### Definitions
- **Impulse**: product of average net external force and the time interval over which it acts; the mechanism producing a change in momentum.
- **Change in momentum**: difference between final and initial momentum, equal in magnitude to the impulse.

### Laws / Principles
- **Impulse-momentum theorem**: change in momentum equals net force times duration. Key insight: the same momentum change can come from a large force over a short time or a small force over a long time.

### Equations
- Δp = F_net·Δt
- F_net = Δp/Δt
- Component example (ball at wall): p_xi = mu, p_yi = 0; p_xf = −mu, p_yf = 0
- Impulse ratio (perpendicular vs 30° impact): 2mu / (2mu·cos30°) = 2/√3 ≈ 1.155

### Worked Example (concept only)
- **Billiard balls striking a wall**: directional analysis of impulse; one ball hits perpendicular, another at 30°. The x-component of momentum reverses for both, but the angled ball keeps its y-component, producing different impulse magnitudes despite equal speeds.

### Applications
Vehicle safety (padded dashboards, airbags), automobile crumple zones, collapsible motorsport structures (extend collision time to reduce peak force), and biomechanics (rolling on landing spreads impact force over time to avoid fracture).

## 8.3 Conservation of Momentum

### Objectives
- State the conservation-of-momentum principle and its conditions.
- Derive expressions showing momentum conservation.
- Illustrate conservation with real-world scenarios.
- Connect momentum conservation to atomic/subatomic behavior.

### Definitions
- **Recoil**: backward momentum gained by an object that exerts a force on another.
- **Isolated system**: system with zero net external force (F_net = 0).

### Laws / Principles
- **Conservation of momentum**: total momentum of an isolated system stays constant. Follows from Newton's third law: internal forces are equal and opposite, so individual momentum changes cancel.

### Equations
- Δp₁ = F₁Δt ; Δp₂ = F₂Δt
- F₂ = −F₁  (Newton's third law)
- Δp₁ + Δp₂ = 0
- p_tot = constant ; p_tot = p'_tot
- p₁ + p₂ = p'₁ + p'₂
- F_net = Δp_tot/Δt

### Applications / Concepts Illustrated
- **Two-car collision**: individual momentum changes are equal and opposite, total momentum preserved when friction is negligible.
- **Projectile motion**: directional independence — horizontal momentum is conserved (no net horizontal force) but vertical momentum is not (gravity acts).
- **Jellyfish/squid**: expel water to generate opposing motion.
- **Subatomic physics**: momentum conservation in collision experiments reveals particle properties and unknown particles (e.g., quarks).
- **Ballistocardiograph**: measures reaction forces from heartbeats using momentum conservation.

## 8.4 Elastic Collisions in One Dimension

### Objectives
- Describe one-dimensional elastic collisions of two objects.
- Explain internal kinetic energy.
- Derive relations for conservation of internal kinetic energy in 1D.
- Compute final velocities from initial conditions and masses.

### Definitions
- **Elastic collision**: a collision that conserves internal kinetic energy.
- **Internal kinetic energy**: sum of the kinetic energies of the objects in the system.

### Laws / Principles
- Both momentum and internal kinetic energy are conserved in an elastic collision (with zero net external force).

### Equations
- Momentum: m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v'₁ + m₂v'₂
- Internal KE: ½m₁v₁² + ½m₂v₂² = ½m₁v'₁² + ½m₂v'₂²

### Worked Example (concept only)
- **Light object striking a stationary heavier object**: lightweight projectile bounces backward while transferring momentum forward to the heavier target. The algebra gives two roots; the physical one has the lighter object reversing direction and the heavier one moving forward.

### Applications
Steel blocks on ice, spring-bumpered carts on air tracks, subatomic particle interactions. Macroscopic collisions are never perfectly elastic — friction and sound always remove some kinetic energy.

## 8.5 Inelastic Collisions in One Dimension

### Objectives
- Distinguish inelastic from elastic collisions.
- Identify perfectly inelastic collisions.
- Connect collision physics to sports applications.
- Compute recoil speeds and kinetic-energy changes from data.

### Definitions
- **Inelastic collision**: collision that does not conserve internal kinetic energy; energy may be removed (heat, sound) or added (stored spring energy).
- **Perfectly inelastic collision**: objects stick together after impact — the maximum kinetic-energy loss consistent with momentum conservation.

### Laws / Principles
- Momentum is conserved when external forces are negligible; internal kinetic energy is not conserved and may transform to/from other forms.

### Equations
- p₁ + p₂ = p'₁ + p'₂
- m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v'₁ + m₂v'₂
- Perfectly inelastic (target at rest): v' = m₁v₁ / (m₁ + m₂)
- KE = ½mv²
- Coefficient of restitution: c = √(h/H)  (bounce height over drop height)

### Worked Examples (concept only)
- **Goalie catches puck**: momentum conserved in a perfectly inelastic collision; most kinetic energy becomes heat and sound rather than motion.
- **Spring-loaded carts**: stored potential energy can increase the kinetic energy during a collision, showing not all collisions reduce motion energy.

### Applications
Sports equipment design (heavier racquets/bats transfer more momentum), "sweet spot" physics minimizing vibration, injury reduction (e.g., tennis elbow).

## 8.6 Collisions of Point Masses in Two Dimensions

### Objectives
- Extend 1D collision analysis to 2D.
- Define point masses.
- Apply momentum conservation along perpendicular axes.
- Characterize equal-mass elastic collisions.
- Compute final velocity magnitude and direction from scattering angles.

### Definitions
- **Point mass**: structureless particle that cannot rotate or spin, simplifying analysis by removing rotational effects.
- **Laboratory coordinate system**: frame where the target is at rest and the incoming particle moves along one axis.
- **Scattering angle**: angular deflection of a particle from its original direction after collision.

### Laws / Principles
- With zero net external force, momentum components along x and y axes are conserved independently.

### Equations
- x-axis: m₁v₁ = m₁v'₁cosθ₁ + m₂v'₂cosθ₂
- y-axis: 0 = m₁v'₁sinθ₁ + m₂v'₂sinθ₂
- Equal-mass elastic KE: ½mv₁² = ½mv'₁² + ½mv'₂²
- Equal-mass elastic angle result: cos(θ₁ − θ₂) = 0 ⇒ θ₁ − θ₂ = 90°

### Worked Example (concept only)
- **Example 8.7**: a known incoming object strikes an unknown stationary target; measuring the incident object's post-collision speed and angle lets one solve for the target's velocity using only the two momentum equations — showing how scattering experiments reveal properties of unseen objects.

### Applications
Billiard balls scatter at roughly 90° between them; subatomic scattering (Rutherford's nucleus discovery); particle/medical physics for identifying unknown particles.

## 8.7 Introduction to Rocket Propulsion

### Objectives
- State Newton's third law.
- Explain how rockets and jets propel themselves.
- Derive an expression for rocket acceleration and its governing factors.
- Explain space-shuttle design.

### Laws / Principles
- **Newton's third law**: ejecting matter from the system produces an equal, opposite reaction on the remaining system (gun recoil, squid locomotion).
- **Gravitational impulse**: gravity applies a negative impulse (−mg·Δt), reducing momentum even as mass is expelled.

### Equations
- Acceleration: a = (v_e/m)·(Δm/Δt) − g
  - a = rocket acceleration; v_e = exhaust velocity relative to rocket; m = current rocket mass; Δm/Δt = burn rate; g = gravitational acceleration
- Single-stage final velocity: v = v_e · ln(m₀/m_r)  (m₀ = initial mass, m_r = remaining mass after burn)

### Factors Affecting Acceleration
1. Higher exhaust velocity → higher acceleration (practical limit ~2,500 m/s for conventional systems).
2. Faster burn rate → more thrust and acceleration.
3. Smaller mass → faster acceleration; mass continuously decreases as fuel burns.

### Worked Example (concept only)
- **Saturn V initial acceleration**: applies the acceleration formula to real launch parameters (mass 2.80×10⁶ kg, burn rate 1.40×10⁴ kg/s, exhaust velocity 2.40×10³ m/s) to get ~2.20 m/s²; acceleration rises continuously as mass drops.

### Applications
Multi-stage rockets (single-stage would need ~98.9% fuel mass to reach escape velocity); space-shuttle reusable boosters/orbiter with expendable external tank; air-launch to gain initial velocity and reduce atmospheric drag.
