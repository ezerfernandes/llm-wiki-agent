## Chapter Introduction

Rotational motion mirrors linear motion: each translational concept (position, velocity,
acceleration, mass, force, momentum, kinetic energy) has a rotational counterpart. The chapter
develops angular acceleration, rotational kinematics, the dynamics of rotation (rotational inertia
and a rotational Newton's second law), rotational kinetic energy, angular momentum and its
conservation, collisions of extended bodies, and the vector/gyroscopic aspects of angular momentum.

Conservation of angular momentum is a central theme: with no net external torque, total angular
momentum is fixed. This explains why an ice skater spins faster when pulling limbs inward and why
tornadoes intensify as their radius shrinks (winds can exceed 500 km/h).

## 10.1 Angular Acceleration

Objectives: characterize uniform and non-uniform rotation; compute angular acceleration; relate
rotational and linear acceleration.

Terms:
- Angular velocity ω — time rate of change of angle θ (rad/s); counter-clockwise positive,
  clockwise negative.
- Angular acceleration α — rate of change of angular velocity (rad/s²); positive when ω increases.
- Tangential (linear) acceleration aₜ — changes the magnitude of speed along the circular path.
- Centripetal acceleration aᶜ — changes the direction of velocity; points to the circle's center.

Equations:
- ω = Δθ/Δt
- v = rω  (so ω = v/r)
- α = Δω/Δt
- aₜ = Δv/Δt
- aₜ = rα  (so α = aₜ/r)

Worked examples:
- Ex 10.1 (Bicycle wheel): converting rpm ↔ rad/s and solving for time using α in deceleration.
- Ex 10.2 (Motorcycle wheel): finding α from linear-velocity data via the proportional v = rω link.

Applications: angular and tangential acceleration are proportional through r; smaller wheels give
less linear acceleration for the same α. Rotational quantities are analogous to translational ones
(θ↔x, ω↔v, α↔a).

## 10.2 Kinematics of Rotational Motion

Objectives: describe rotational kinematics; relate the rotational variables; solve problems.

Kinematics = description of motion without reference to forces or masses. Variables: θ, ω, α, t.

Constant-acceleration kinematic equations (rotational ↔ translational analogs):
- ω = ω₀ + αt        (v = v₀ + at)
- θ = ω̄ t            (x = v̄ t)
- θ = ω₀t + ½αt²     (x = v₀t + ½at²)
- ω² = ω₀² + 2αθ     (v² = v₀² + 2ax)

Averages: ω̄ = (ω₀ + ω)/2,  v̄ = (v₀ + v)/2.
Linear links: v = rω, a = rα. Conversion: 1 rev = 2π rad; x = rθ; θ = x/r.

Worked examples:
- Fishing reel: apply ω = ω₀ + αt to find final ω; connect to linear via v = rω.
- Braking: solve time-to-stop using ω = ω₀ + αt with negative α; sign conventions.
- Train wheel: use θ = x/r and ω² = ω₀² + 2αθ when revolutions are given but time is not.
- Rotating platform: θ = ω̄ t at constant ω, convert revolutions to distance with x = rθ.

Applications: rotating machinery; predicting motion without analyzing forces; converting between
rotational and linear quantities for engineering design.

## 10.3 Dynamics of Rotational Motion: Rotational Inertia

Objectives: connect force, mass, and acceleration in rotation; understand torque; see parallels
with translational dynamics.

Terms:
- Torque τ — the turning effectiveness of a force about a pivot; for a perpendicular force,
  τ = Fr.
- Rotational inertia / moment of inertia I — rotational analog of mass; resistance to angular
  acceleration; depends on mass and its distribution relative to the axis.

Laws / principles — rotational Newton's second law:
- net τ = Iα   (so α = net τ / I), parallel to F = ma with τ↔F, I↔m, α↔a.

Equations:
- a = rα; F = mrα; τ = Fr
- Point mass: I = mr²
- General: I = Σ mr²  (sum over point masses; integration for continuous bodies)
- τ = mr²α;  net τ = Iα

Worked example:
- Ex 10.7 (Merry-go-round): how mass distribution changes I and thus α. Empty platform yields a
  larger α; a child seated at 1.25 m adds to I and reduces α for the same applied torque.

Moments of inertia for common shapes (Figure 10.11):
- Solid disk/cylinder about central axis: I = ½MR²
- Hoop / thin ring about central axis: I = MR²
- (Other geometries given in the textbook table, e.g. rod, sphere — see later sections.)

Applications: pushing a door farther from its hinges gives greater α; heavier doors accelerate more
slowly for the same force; the τ–I–α relation holds for any torque about any axis.

## 10.4 Rotational Kinetic Energy: Work and Energy Revisited

Objectives: derive rotational work; compute rotational kinetic energy; apply conservation of energy
to rotating systems.

Terms: rotational work (energy transferred when torque acts through an angular displacement);
rotational kinetic energy (energy of a spinning object); plus I, ω, τ as before.

Equations:
- Rotational work: W_net = τ θ
- Rotational kinetic energy: KE_rot = ½ I ω²
- Work-energy theorem (rotation): W_net = ½Iω² − ½Iω₀²
- Disk: I = ½MR²; thin rod about end: I = (1/3)Mℓ²
- Kinematics: ω² = ω₀² + 2αθ
- Rolling without slipping: ω = v/R
- Energy conservation for rolling: mgh = ½mv² + ½Iω²

Worked examples:
- Ex 10.8 (Grindstone): work done by a torque equals the rotational KE produced.
- Ex 10.9 (Helicopter blade): rotational KE in rotors can vastly exceed translational KE — energy
  storage in spinning components.
- Ex 10.10 (Cylinder rolling down an incline): potential energy splits between translational and
  rotational KE; shape (not mass or radius) sets the rolling outcome.

Applications: flywheels store rotational KE; helicopter pilots keep minimum blade rotation to
preserve lift; rolling-object descent depends on how mass is distributed (rotational inertia).

## 10.5 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation

Objectives: see angular momentum as the analog of linear momentum; relate torque to the rate of
change of angular momentum; apply conservation when net external torque is zero.

Terms: angular momentum L; moment of inertia I; angular velocity ω; torque τ.

Equations:
- Angular momentum: L = Iω  (units kg·m²/s)
- Rotational Newton's 2nd law (momentum form): net τ = ΔL/Δt
- Conservation: if net τ = 0, then ΔL/Δt = 0, so L = constant; Iω = I′ω′
- Uniform sphere: I = (2/5)MR²; disk: I = ½MR²
- KE_rot = ½Iω²; ω² = ω₀² + 2αθ

Worked examples:
- Earth's angular momentum: massive bodies carry enormous L even at modest ω.
- Lazy Susan: a torque applied for a time imparts L; the L gained depends on torque × time.
- Leg kick: torque from muscle force and mechanical advantage; rotational KE built over angular
  displacement.
- Spinning ice skater (signature example): pulling limbs in lowers I, so ω rises sharply while L
  stays constant; KE increases because the skater does internal muscular work.

Applications / principles: a torque must act over time to change L. With negligible external torque,
rotation persists — Earth keeps spinning, tornadoes intensify as they contract, planets/stars form
from collapsing clouds. Every translational quantity has a rotational counterpart
(momentum↔angular momentum, force↔torque, mass↔I). An ice skater speeds up without external
torque by rearranging mass internally.

## 10.6 Collisions of Extended Bodies in Two Dimensions

Objectives: analyze collisions of objects that can rotate; examine the percussion (sweet) point.

Terms:
- Angular momentum L (conserved about a pivot when external forces exert no torque there).
- Moment of inertia I (rod about one end: I = Mℓ²/3).
- Percussion point ("sweet spot") — the impact location producing no net force at a pivot/support.
- Inelastic collision — kinetic energy not conserved.

Laws / principles: conservation of angular momentum holds when external forces exert zero torque
about the chosen pivot (L = L′). A support point can exert an unbalanced force without producing
torque if its moment arm is zero — so angular momentum is conserved even though linear momentum may
not be.

Equations:
- L = Iω
- L = mr²(v/r) = mvr  (point mass at radius r)
- Combined disk-and-rod system: I′ = mr² + Mr²/3 = (m + M/3)r²
- Post-collision angular velocity: ω′ = mvr / I′
- KE = ½mv² (pre, translational); KE′ = ½I′ω′² (post, rotational)
- Linear momentum: p′ = (m + M/2)v′ = (m + M/2)rω′

Worked example:
- Disk striking a pivoted stick: how angular-momentum conservation differs from linear-momentum
  conservation when an external pivot force is present. KE drops (inelastic); linear momentum can
  increase because of the pivot's forward reaction force.

Applications: bowling (pins gain spin and translation); tennis/baseball sweet spot reduces jarring
of the hand by eliminating handle force; vehicles spinning after collisions on slippery surfaces.

## 10.7 Gyroscopic Effects: Vector Aspects of Angular Momentum

Objectives: use the right-hand rule for ω, L, and τ; explain gyroscopic behavior; analyze Earth as
a gyroscope.

Terms:
- Angular momentum L — a vector along the rotation axis.
- Angular velocity ω — a vector parallel to L, perpendicular to the rotation plane.
- Torque τ — vector perpendicular to the plane formed by r and F.
- Precession — slow change in the orientation of a spinning object's axis caused by a torque
  perpendicular to L.
- Gyroscopic effect — the counterintuitive response in which a spinning object moves perpendicular
  to the applied force.

Equations / principles:
- net τ = ΔL/Δt — the direction of ΔL matches the direction of τ.
- L = Iω — L and ω are parallel, both along the axis.

Right-hand rule:
- For ω and L: curl fingers in the rotation direction; the thumb gives the axis direction.
- For τ: curl fingers from r toward F; the thumb gives the torque direction (⊥ to the r–F plane).

Precession mechanism: when τ is perpendicular to L, the change ΔL points along τ; adding ΔL to L
reorients the axis without it falling — the magnitude stays fixed, only the direction shifts. In the
bicycle-wheel demo, trying to rotate a spinning wheel makes it tilt toward the person instead.

Conceptual distinction: L (and ω, τ) are vectors with direction; rotational kinetic energy is always
a scalar.

Applications: gyroscopes hold orientation through rapid spin; spinning bicycle wheels precess; Earth
behaves like a giant gyroscope — its axis points toward Polaris, and gravitational torque from the
Sun and Moon precesses it with a period of about 26,000 years, slowly shifting the pole star.
