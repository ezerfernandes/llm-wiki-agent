## 15.0 Introduction to Thermodynamics

Thermodynamics studies how thermal-energy transfer relates to the production of mechanical work, and the fundamental limits that physical law (not just engineering) places on that conversion. Core ideas: heat transfer can be converted to work; heat engines turn thermal input into work output (a car engine is the canonical example); efficiency is the ratio of useful work to heat input and is capped by the second law; entropy measures disorder and the unavailability of energy for work. Applications: automobile and combustion engines, thermal power plants (coal/nuclear/gas), and refrigeration/heat pumps that move heat "uphill" using work input.

## 15.1 The First Law of Thermodynamics

**Objectives:** state the first law; tie energy conservation to thermal systems; compute internal-energy changes from heat and work; recognize biological examples.

**Definitions:**
- **Internal energy (E_int):** sum of the kinetic and potential energies of all atoms/molecules in a system. A **state function** — depends only on the system's current state, not the path taken.
- **Heat (Q):** energy transferred because of a temperature difference; a disorganized transfer. Q > 0 = heat into the system.
- **Work (W):** organized energy transfer via macroscopic forces over distance. W > 0 = work done *by* the system (energy leaving).
- **Path independence:** ΔE_int is fixed by the endpoints; Q and W individually are path-dependent.

**Law / Equation — First Law of Thermodynamics:**
$$\Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q - W$$
Restatement of conservation of energy: internal-energy change = heat added minus work done by the system.

**Worked examples (concept):**
- Multiple Q/W transactions sum to one net ΔE_int — illustrates that internal energy is path-independent.
- A different process (heat leaving, work entering) gives an identical ΔE_int — reinforces dependence on endpoints only.

**Applications:** Human metabolism — the body holds temperature roughly constant while doing work, so it loses internal energy that food replenishes; conversion of internal energy to useful work is poor (most leaves as heat). Body fat → work/heat is irreversible (heat won't reconvert to fat), an example of thermodynamic irreversibility.

## 15.2 The First Law and Some Simple Processes

**Objectives:** describe basic heat-engine operation; distinguish isobaric, isochoric, isothermal, adiabatic processes; find total work in cyclic processes.

**Definitions:**
- **Heat engine:** device converting heat transfer into mechanical work (car engine, steam turbine).
- **PV diagram:** pressure–volume plot; work = area under the curve.
- **Isobaric:** constant pressure.
- **Isochoric (isovolumetric):** constant volume; vertical line on a PV diagram; W = 0.
- **Isothermal:** constant temperature.
- **Adiabatic:** no heat transfer, Q = 0.
- **Reversible process:** idealized; system and surroundings can both be returned to initial states. Real processes have dissipation (friction, turbulence) and are irreversible.
- **Cyclical process:** returns the system to its starting state; net work = enclosed PV-loop area.

**Equations:**
- Isobaric work: $W = P\,\Delta V$
- General work: $W = \int P\,dV$ (area under PV curve)
- Cyclical net work = enclosed loop area (positive clockwise, negative counterclockwise)
- Isothermal (ideal gas): $PV = \text{const}$, $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$, so $Q = W$
- Monatomic ideal-gas internal energy: $E_{\text{int}} = \frac{3}{2}NkT$
- Adiabatic: $Q = 0$, so $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = -W$ (work done at the expense of internal energy)
- Heat-engine first law over a cycle: $W = Q_{\text{in}} - Q_{\text{out}}$ (ΔE_int = 0)

**Process summary:**
| Process | Constraint | Work | Heat | ΔE_int |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Isobaric | P const | PΔV | nonzero | changes |
| Isochoric | V const | 0 | all → E_int | changes |
| Isothermal | T const | Q | = W | 0 |
| Adiabatic | Q = 0 | −ΔE_int | 0 | decreases on expansion |

**Worked example (concept):** Example 15.2 — net work of a rectangular PV cycle computed two ways (segment-by-segment vs. enclosed-area geometry) gives the same answer, confirming work = enclosed area.

**Key insights:** Starting from the same point, an isothermal expansion curve stays above the adiabatic one (continuous heat input sustains pressure), so isothermal yields more work; adiabatic temperature/pressure drop yields less. Loop direction sets sign of net work.

## 15.3 Second Law: Heat Engines and Efficiency

**Objectives:** state equivalent forms of the second law; compute thermal efficiency and CO₂ output of power plants; describe the Otto cycle.

**Definitions:**
- **Hot/cold reservoirs (T_h, T_c):** constant-temperature heat source and sink.
- **Irreversible process:** occurs spontaneously in one direction only; path-dependent; statistically impossible to reverse.

**Laws — Second Law of Thermodynamics:**
- *Form 1:* Heat flows spontaneously from hotter to colder bodies, never spontaneously the reverse (no process whose sole result is heat from cold to hot).
- *Form 2 (Kelvin statement):* It is impossible for a cyclical process to convert heat from a reservoir entirely into work — Q_c > 0 always; 100% efficiency is forbidden.

**Equations:**
- Net cycle work: $W = Q_h - Q_c$
- Efficiency: $\text{Eff} = \dfrac{W}{Q_h} = 1 - \dfrac{Q_c}{Q_h}$
- General first law: $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q - W$

**Worked example (concept):** Coal power station, Q_h = 2.50×10¹⁴ J, Q_c = 1.48×10¹⁴ J per day → W ≈ 1.02×10¹⁴ J, Eff ≈ 40.8%, with daily CO₂ ≈ 3.7×10⁸ kg. Illustrates that real engines convert only a fraction of heat to work; ~59% is unavoidable waste heat.

**Otto cycle (four-stroke model):** AB adiabatic compression (work in, T rises); BC constant-volume heating (ignition raises P, T); CD adiabatic expansion (power stroke, more work out); DA constant-volume cooling (heat rejected, reset). Net work = enclosed area; heat rejection along DA is mandatory. Higher T_h and lower T_c increase efficiency.

**Irreversibility examples:** braking turns kinetic energy fully to heat (never reverses); gas expands into vacuum and never spontaneously recompresses; broken objects don't reassemble. These reflect entropy increase / probability.

**Applications:** coal plants ~42% efficient; waste heat warms waterways; coal emits the most CO₂ per unit energy vs. oil/gas; thermodynamic limits apply to all heat engines including nuclear.

## 15.4 Carnot's Perfect Heat Engine — Second Law Restated

**Objectives:** identify a Carnot cycle; compute maximum theoretical efficiency; understand how irreversibility lowers real performance.

**Definitions:**
- **Carnot cycle:** theoretical cycle of only reversible isothermal and adiabatic steps — most efficient possible between two reservoirs.
- **Carnot engine:** any engine running the Carnot cycle; achieves the theoretical max efficiency for its T_h, T_c.
- **Absolute temperature:** kelvins; required in efficiency formulas.

**Law — Carnot's principle (second-law form):** A Carnot engine has the greatest efficiency of any engine between two given temperatures; all engines using only reversible processes share this same maximum. No real engine reaches it because all real processes are irreversible.

**Equations:**
- $\text{Eff}_C = 1 - \dfrac{T_c}{T_h}$ (T in kelvin)
- For a Carnot engine: $\dfrac{Q_c}{Q_h} = \dfrac{T_c}{T_h}$ (unique to reversible processes; underlies the efficiency formula and entropy)

**Carnot cycle steps:** (1) isothermal expansion at T_h, heat Q_h in; (2) adiabatic expansion, cools to T_c, no heat; (3) isothermal compression at T_c, heat Q_c out; (4) adiabatic compression, reheats to T_h. PV loop area = net work.

**Worked example (concept):** Pressurized-water reactor, T_h = 300 °C (573 K), T_c = 27 °C (300 K) → Eff_C = 1 − 300/573 ≈ 47.6%. Illustrates Celsius→kelvin conversion and applying the Carnot formula as a ceiling (real nuclear plants ~35%).

**Sadi Carnot (1796–1832):** young French engineer who formulated the Carnot cycle in 1824 during the rise of heat-engine technology; his framework clarified the second law and set theoretical efficiency limits.

**Implications:** 100% efficiency would require T_c = 0 K (absolute zero), unattainable. Maximize efficiency by raising T_h and lowering T_c. Peripheral irreversibilities (friction, transmissions, transformers) further cut real output. The "drinking bird" toy illustrates near-reversible cyclic operation.

## 15.5 Applications: Heat Pumps and Refrigerators

**Objectives:** describe heat engines run in reverse for heating/cooling; explain heat-pump operation; distinguish heat pumps from refrigerators; compute COP.

**Definitions:**
- **Heat pump:** moves heat from a cold outside reservoir into a warmer space using work input (reversed heat-engine cycle).
- **Refrigerator / air conditioner:** extracts heat from a cooler space inside a warmer environment using work input.
- **Coefficient of performance (COP):** dimensionless ratio of beneficial heat transfer to required work input.
- **Working fluid, evaporator coils (absorb heat), condenser coils (release heat):** the refrigerant circuit components.

**Laws / Equations:**
- Energy balance: $Q_h = Q_c + W$
- Heat-pump performance: $\text{COP}_{hp} = \dfrac{Q_h}{W}$
- Refrigerator/AC performance: $\text{COP}_{ref} = \dfrac{Q_c}{W}$
- Relation: $\text{COP}_{ref} = \text{COP}_{hp} - 1$
- Inverse relation to engine efficiency: $\text{COP}_{hp} = \dfrac{1}{\text{Eff}}$ (best Carnot heat pump uses Eff_C)
- Energy efficiency rating: $\text{EER} = \dfrac{Q_c/t_1}{W/t_2}$ (mixed SI/British units)

**Worked example (concept):** Example 15.5 — best heat pump for a home, T_h = 45 °C (318 K), T_c = −15 °C (258 K) → Eff_C ≈ 0.189, COP_hp ≈ 5.30 (delivers 5.3× the work input as heat). Shows smaller temperature gaps (milder climates) give higher COP.

**Applications:** Heat pumps deliver more heat than the work costs, but electricity may be pricier per joule than burning gas; best in mild climates with cheap power. Real COP_hp ≈ 2–4; refrigerators/AC COP_ref ≈ 2–6. Energy Star ratings and EER (6–12 for room units) guide consumers. A heat pump must produce working fluid hotter than indoors (to deliver heat) and colder than outdoors (to absorb heat), bounding practical COP.

## 15.6 Entropy and the Second Law — Disorder and Unavailability of Energy

**Objectives:** compute entropy change for reversible and irreversible processes; state the universe's thermodynamic fate; quantify increasing disorder.

**Definitions:**
- **Entropy (S):** measure of the energy in a system unavailable to do work; equivalently, a measure of disorder.
- **State property:** entropy depends only on current state, so ΔS can be computed via any convenient reversible path.
- **Reversible process:** total entropy constant; **irreversible process:** total entropy increases.

**Equations:**
- Entropy change (reversible): $\Delta S = \dfrac{Q}{T}$
- Total over reservoirs: $\Delta S_{\text{tot}} = \Delta S_h + \Delta S_c$
- Carnot relation: $\dfrac{Q_c}{T_c} = \dfrac{Q_h}{T_h}$ (so ΔS_tot = 0 for a Carnot cycle)
- Unavailable work (energy lost to work): $W_{\text{unavail}} = \Delta S \cdot T_0$, with $T_0$ the lowest available temperature.

**Law — Second Law (entropy form):** The total entropy of a system never decreases; it stays constant (reversible) or increases (irreversible). Spontaneous hot→cold heat transfer increases total entropy.

**Worked examples (concept):**
- Irreversible heat transfer between unequal-temperature reservoirs → net ΔS > 0 (gain at the cold side exceeds loss at the hot side).
- Equal heat into perfect engines yields different work depending on ΔS — more entropy = less useful work.
- Melting ice → large entropy increase as ordered crystal → disordered liquid.

**Cosmic implication — heat death:** universe entropy rises; eventually all temperature gradients equalize and no work is possible (maximum entropy). Estimated extraordinarily far off.

**Life and the second law:** organisms decreasing local entropy is allowed because environmental entropy rises more: $\Delta S_{\text{tot}} = \Delta S_{\text{system}} + \Delta S_{\text{environment}} > 0$. Solar energy through Earth enables biological organization while increasing entropy elsewhere.

## 15.7 Statistical Interpretation of Entropy

**Objectives:** recognize probabilistic foundations of entropy; apply statistical reasoning to entropic systems.

**Definitions:**
- **Macrostate:** aggregate system description (e.g., "3 heads, 2 tails") without specifying individual elements.
- **Microstate:** complete specification of every element (e.g., "HTHTH").
- **Boltzmann's constant:** k = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K, linking microscopic counting to macroscopic thermodynamics.

**Law — Statistical second law:** entropy stays constant or increases because disordered configurations vastly outnumber ordered ones; decreases are possible but astronomically improbable for macroscopic systems. "Disorder is simply vastly more likely than order." Equilibrium = the most populated microstate region.

**Equations:**
- Boltzmann entropy: $S = k \ln W$ (W = number of microstates for a macrostate)
- Entropy change: $\Delta S = S_f - S_i = k \ln W_f - k \ln W_i$

**Coin-toss analogy:** Five coins → 32 microstates across 6 macrostates; 3H/2T (10 microstates) is 10× more likely than 5H (1 microstate). Hundred coins → 50/50 has ~1.0×10²⁹ microstates (most probable) out of ~1.27×10³⁰ total; returning to all-heads is effectively impossible within a trillion universe lifetimes.

**Gas example:** 1 cm³ of gas at STP has ~2.7×10¹⁹ atoms; each macrostate (P, T, V) corresponds to an enormous number of microstates. Likely state = random Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution (max entropy); unlikely = all atoms in one corner (min entropy). Assumes all microstates equally probable.

**Worked example (concept):** 100 coins going 60H/40T → 50H/50T; ΔS ≈ +2.7×10⁻²³ J/K. Reverse (50/50 → 60/40) has ~1-in-90 chance; returning to a still-more-ordered state ~1-in-10³⁰ — macroscopic entropy decreases are effectively impossible.

**Significance:** the numerical dominance of disordered microstates explains why heat flows hot→cold, why available energy declines, and why natural processes increase disorder — by probability, not by an imposed force. This statistical interpretation links thermodynamic entropy to the information-theoretic notion of entropy.
