## 32.0 Introduction

Nuclear physics underpins a broad range of practical technologies spanning medicine (diagnostic imaging and cancer treatment), energy generation (fission reactors and prospective fusion plants), and food/material sterilization. The same body of knowledge enables both humanitarian benefit and severe harm (nuclear weapons); the science itself is morally neutral, and outcomes depend on human choices. The chapter frames nuclear physics as a case study in responsible technological development.

## 32.1 Diagnostics and Medical Imaging

**Objectives:** explain how an Anger (gamma) camera forms an image; compare SPECT and PET.

**Terms:**
- **Radiopharmaceutical** — a radioactive compound (a radioisotope incorporated into a drug) engineered to localize in a specific organ or tissue.
- **Tagged / labeled compound** — a molecule in which a radioactive nuclide substitutes for a stable atom, preserving chemistry while making the molecule detectable by its radiation.
- **Radioimmunoassay (RIA)** — quantitative assay based on competitive binding between antibodies and a target substance; concentration inferred from measured unbound antibody.
- **Anger (gamma) camera** — detector that uses a lead collimator (bored apertures) to admit only gamma rays traveling along known directions, mapping 2-D activity in tissue.
- **SPECT** (Single-Photon Emission Computed Tomography) — rotating gamma-camera array that reconstructs a 3-D distribution of a radiopharmaceutical; resolution ~1 cm.
- **PET** (Positron Emission Tomography) — uses positron-emitting tracers; the positron annihilates a nearby electron, producing two 0.511-MeV gamma photons emitted in opposite (~180°) directions, which coincidence detection localizes precisely; resolution ~0.5 cm.

**Principles:**
- Gamma radiation penetrates tissue readily, so internally administered gamma emitters can be detected externally.
- Positron-electron annihilation converts both rest masses into two 0.511-MeV photons emitted back-to-back (conservation of momentum and energy).

**Key radioisotope — Technetium-99m (⁹⁹ᵐTc):** half-life 6.0 h; emits a single 0.142-MeV gamma; produced by neutron activation of molybdenum followed by beta decay; used in ~80% of nuclear-medicine procedures.

**Clinical tracers / applications:**
- Thyroid: ¹³¹I or ¹²³I (~0.05–0.07 mCi); iodine concentrates in thyroid, high uptake flags overactive tissue.
- Cardiac perfusion: ²⁰¹Tl (~3 mCi); thallium follows blood flow, revealing coronary blockage.
- Tumor / infection: ⁶⁷Ga; accumulates at sites over 3–4 days.
- Bone scan: ⁹⁹ᵐTc (~10 mCi).
- Brain / metabolism (PET): ¹⁸F, ¹¹C, ¹³N, ¹⁵O (~10–50 mCi); ¹⁵O oxygen-metabolism imaging can detect early Alzheimer's changes.

## 32.2 Biological Effects of Ionizing Radiation

**Objectives:** define units of radiation dose; explain relative biological effectiveness (RBE).

**Mechanism:** biological damage originates from molecular disruption, especially to DNA. Cells repair ~10⁶ DNA lesions/day; when repair is overwhelmed a cell may go dormant (senescence), die (apoptosis), or become cancerous (uncontrolled division). Radiation both causes and treats cancer — rapidly dividing tumor cells are more radiosensitive than normal cells.

**Dose units:**
| Quantity | Definition | Conversion |
|---|---|---|
| rad | absorbed-dose unit | 1 rad = 0.01 J/kg |
| gray (Gy) | SI absorbed dose | 1 Gy = 1 J/kg = 100 rad |
| RBE (= QF) | relative biological effectiveness / quality factor | depends on radiation type & energy |
| rem | dose equivalent (traditional) | rem = rad × RBE |
| sievert (Sv) | SI dose equivalent | 1 Sv = 100 rem |

**RBE:** reflects how concentrated the ionization is. Alpha particles deposit ionization densely along short tracks, causing localized, hard-to-repair damage → RBE ≈ 10–20; X-rays and gamma rays have RBE = 1; fast neutrons/protons intermediate.

**Equations:**
- Absorbed dose: Dose (Gy) = Energy absorbed (J) / mass (kg)
- Dose equivalent: Dose (Sv) = Dose (Gy) × RBE
- Traditional: rem = rad × RBE
- Number of decays: N = R · t  (R = activity in decays/s, t = time)
- Energy deposited: E = N × (energy per decay)

**Risk models / principles:**
- **Linear (no-threshold) hypothesis:** low-dose risk assumed proportional to high-dose risk (debated; some evidence for adaptive repair / hormesis at very low doses).
- **Latency:** leukemia ~2 yr; solid cancers ~15 yr; risk window ≥30 yr. Lifetime cancer-mortality risk ≈ 10 per million per rem per year.
- **Protection standards:** occupational whole-body limit ~20–50 mSv/yr (country-dependent); public limit from a nuclear facility ≤0.05 mSv/yr.

**Acute-dose effects:** <0.1 Sv none detectable; 0.1–1 Sv reduced white cells; ~0.5 Sv temporary sterility; 2–5 Sv hair loss/hemorrhage/possible death; **LD50/32 ≈ 4.5 Sv** (50% die within 32 days); >20 Sv fatal within hours (CNS collapse).

**Worked example (concept):** *Plutonium inhalation dose* — convert a retained ²³⁹Pu activity (1.00 μCi in 2.00 kg lung) into annual decay count, then deposited energy (5.23 MeV/decay → joules), then absorbed dose (Gy), then dose equivalent (Sv) via alpha RBE ≈ 20. Illustrates why internal alpha emitters are dangerous despite carrying the same energy as beta/gamma sources.

**Applications / context:** diagnostic doses — chest X-ray 0.02 mSv, abdominal CT 10 mSv, dental film 0.01 mSv. Average background ~2.8 mSv/yr (radon 1.2, terrestrial 0.48, medical 0.40, cosmic 0.39, internal 0.29). Protection by time, distance, and shielding (lead aprons, barriers, fast imaging).

## 32.3 Therapeutic Uses of Ionizing Radiation

**Objectives:** define radiotherapy; state typical therapeutic doses.

**Terms:**
- **Radiotherapy** — therapeutic use of ionizing radiation, primarily to kill malignant tumors (since the discovery of X-rays).
- **Therapeutic ratio** — fraction of cancer cells killed relative to normal cells damaged; the quantity every technique tries to maximize.

**Principle:** tumor cells divide faster and are more radiosensitive than normal tissue; treatment exploits this while sparing healthy structures.

**Modalities (applications):**
- **External-beam radiotherapy:** narrow beams from multiple angles converge on the tumor, concentrating dose at the target while spreading exposure over surrounding tissue. Sources: X-rays, ⁶⁰Co gamma rays, accelerator particle beams.
- **Brachytherapy (internal implants/seeds):** radioactive seeds (e.g. ¹²⁵I, ¹⁰³Pd) placed in the tumor; short-range emitters with high local RBE.
- **Targeted radiopharmaceuticals:** radioactive antibodies or tracers that home to cancer (e.g. radioactive iodine for thyroid cancer).

**Dosing:** roughly three to five ~200-rem treatments per week over several weeks; intervals let normal tissue recover. Total dose varies by cancer (lung ~10–20 Sv; brain 80+ Sv).

## 32.4 Food Irradiation

**Objectives:** explain food irradiation; distinguish low- vs high-dose treatment; define free radicals.

**Terms:**
- **Food irradiation** — exposing food to ionizing radiation to kill pests/microbes and slow spoilage.
- **Low dose:** up to ~1000 Gy — reduces microbial load on fresh produce with minimal chemical change.
- **High dose:** above ~10,000 Gy — significant nutrient loss (~15%) and vitamin degradation (~25%).
- **Free radicals** — reactive molecular fragments (reactive O/H) created by ionization, which drive cascading chemistry.
- **Radiolytic products** — the many compounds produced when free radicals react; can damage cell membranes and DNA (which is how microbes are killed).

**Key principle:** because the photon/electron energies used induce no nuclear reactions, irradiated food carries **no residual radioactivity**.

**Sources:** ⁶⁰Co γ (~1.25 MeV, high penetration); ¹³⁷Cs γ (~0.67 MeV); X-rays up to 5 MeV; electron beams up to 10 MeV.

**Applications:** spices, fruit, vegetables, red meat, poultry; approved in 40+ countries; can cut spoilage and raise effective crop yield by 25%+; also sterilizes medical supplies (bandages, tampons). High-water foods (lettuce, peaches) may spoil faster after treatment; milk develops off-flavors. No short-term ill effects observed; long-term effects considered uncertain due to limited consumption history.

## 32.5 Fusion

**Objectives:** define nuclear fusion; describe the conditions/methods for controlled fusion power.

**Terms:**
- **Nuclear fusion** — two light nuclei combine into a heavier nucleus, releasing energy via mass-energy conversion.
- **Thermonuclear power** — controlled fusion harnessed for energy.
- **Binding energy per nucleon (BE/A):** peaks near iron-56; fusing light nuclei (below the peak) releases energy.
- **Coulomb barrier:** electrostatic repulsion between positive nuclei that must be overcome (or tunneled through) for fusion.
- **Quantum tunneling:** allows fusion even when classical kinetic energy is below the barrier.
- **Ignition:** fusion becomes self-sustaining with no external heating. **Break-even:** fusion power output equals heating input.

**Principles:**
- Mass-energy equivalence (E = Δm c²) supplies the released energy.
- Fusion rate rises steeply with temperature (more kinetic energy and more tunneling).
- Stars self-regulate by negative feedback: more fusion → hotter → expansion → cooling.
- Practical fusion requires the right combination of temperature, density, and confinement time (a Lawson-criterion-style trade-off).

**Reactions (with Q values):**
Proton-proton cycle (stellar):
1. ¹H + ¹H → ²H + e⁺ + νₑ  (0.42 MeV)
2. ¹H + ²H → ³He + γ  (5.49 MeV)
3. ³He + ³He → ⁴He + ¹H + ¹H  (12.86 MeV)
Net: 2e⁻ + 4¹H → ⁴He + 2νₑ + 6γ  (26.7 MeV total)

Deuterium/tritium reactions:
- ²H + ²H → ³H + ¹H  (4.03 MeV)
- ²H + ²H → ³He + n  (3.27 MeV)
- ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n  (17.59 MeV)
- ²H + ²H → ⁴He + γ  (23.85 MeV)

Neutron-induced:
- n + ¹H → ²H + γ  (2.22 MeV)

**Worked example (concept):** energy/power from D-T fusion of 1 kg of fuel reacting over a year (~3.37 × 10¹⁴ J). Illustrates fusion's enormous energy density (equivalent to millions of gallons of gasoline) yet modest steady power, motivating large-scale plants.

**Applications:** the Sun (proton-proton cycle in core, solar neutrinos confirm it); magnetic confinement (**tokamak** with toroidal fields, e.g. **ITER** targeting break-even); inertial confinement (laser-compressed D-T pellets, e.g. National Ignition Facility's 192 UV beams).

## 32.6 Fission

**Objectives:** define nuclear fission; describe fission fuels and products; distinguish controlled vs uncontrolled chain reactions.

**Terms:**
- **Nuclear fission** — a heavy nucleus splits into lighter fragments, releasing energy (opposite of fusion).
- **Fission fragments** — the two daughter nuclei produced.
- **Critical mass** — minimum fissile mass for a self-sustaining chain reaction.
- **Criticality** — self-sustaining state; **supercriticality** — exponentially growing fission rate.
- **Moderator** — material (often water) that slows neutrons to thermal speeds where ²³⁵U readily fissions.
- **Neutron-induced fission** — a free neutron is absorbed by a nucleus, which then splits.

**Principles:**
- Energy is released because BE/A is higher in medium-mass fragments than in the heavy parent; the mass deficit converts to kinetic energy (E = Δm c²).
- **Liquid-drop model:** an absorbed neutron deforms the nucleus until Coulomb repulsion overcomes the nuclear force, splitting it plus emitting neutrons.
- **Chain-reaction condition:** average neutrons released per fission, x, must satisfy x > 1.
- Odd-neutron nuclides (²³⁵U, ²³⁹Pu) absorb an extra neutron with ~2 MeV extra binding energy, boosting fission probability; ²³⁸U (99.27% of natural U) is hard to fission.

**Reactions:**
- General: n + ᴬX → FF₁ + FF₂ + x·n
- Example: n + ²³⁵U → ¹⁴²Ba + ⁹¹Kr + 3n
- Spontaneous: ²³⁸U → ⁹⁵Sr + ¹⁴⁰Xe + 3n
- Breeding ²³⁹Pu: ²³⁸U + n → ²³⁹U + γ; ²³⁹U → ²³⁹Np + β⁻ + ν̄ₑ (t½ = 23 min); ²³⁹Np → ²³⁹Pu + β⁻ + ν̄ₑ (t½ = 2.4 d)

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- *Energy from spontaneous fission* — use the mass defect to find energy released; ²³⁸U fission yields ~171 MeV (less than the ~240 MeV ideal because fragments are unequal and neutrons are emitted).
- *Energy per kilogram of fuel* — 1 kg of ²³⁵U releases ~8.21 × 10¹³ J (≈14,000 barrels of oil), showing fission's huge power density (though per-kg energy is below fusion).

**Applications:**
- **Pressurized-water reactors:** fuel rods in water; water both moderates neutrons and gives negative feedback (if it boils away, the chain reaction stops).
- **Control rods:** strong neutron absorbers regulate flux to keep the reactor critical but not supercritical, preventing meltdown.
- **Fuel & enrichment:** ²³⁵U (0.72% of natural U) and reactor-bred ²³⁹Pu; ²³⁵U enrichment by gaseous diffusion of UF₆ exploiting mass-dependent molecular speeds.
- **Breeder reactors:** convert abundant ²³⁸U into ²³⁹Pu, potentially using ~99% of natural uranium; economic/safety concerns remain.

## 32.7 Nuclear Weapons

**Objectives:** distinguish fission from thermonuclear (fusion) weapons; describe destructive effects.

**Terms:**
- **Critical mass** — minimum fissile mass to sustain an uncontrolled chain reaction.
- **Chain reaction** — self-perpetuating fission where emitted neutrons trigger more fissions.
- **Yield** — explosive energy, in kilotons (1 kT = energy of 1000 tons of TNT) or megatons.
- **Fission bomb** — rapidly assembles subcritical masses into a supercritical configuration.
- **Thermonuclear / fusion (H-) bomb** — a fission trigger ignites fusion of hydrogen isotopes.

**Principles:** each fission emits multiple neutrons → exponential growth; fusion yields several times more energy per kilogram than fission.

**Fission-bomb designs:**
- **Gun-type (²³⁵U):** chemical explosive drives two subcritical masses together (1-D assembly); must stay compressed microseconds with a neutron initiator.
- **Implosion (²³⁹Pu):** shaped high-explosive "lenses" compress a spherical core to supercritical density in 3-D; faster and more efficient, needs precise neutron timing.

**Thermonuclear architecture:** a primary fission bomb's gamma rays and neutrons heat/compress fusion fuel (lithium deuteride). Reaction n + ⁶Li → ³H + ⁴He breeds tritium fuel in place. An outer ²³⁸U tamper reflects neutrons and itself fissions under fast neutrons, boosting yield.

**Historical yields:** Trinity (Jul 16 1945, Pu fission, ~10 kT); Hiroshima (Aug 6 1945, ²³⁵U gun, ~15 kT, ~80,000 immediate deaths); Nagasaki (Aug 9 1945, Pu implosion, ~20 kT, ~50,000 deaths); first US thermonuclear test (Oct 31 1952, ~10 MT, ~670× Hiroshima).

**Energy distribution:** conventional explosive ~90% blast/10% thermal; typical nuclear ~50% blast / 35% thermal / 15% radiation; neutron (enhanced-radiation) bomb ~40% blast / 30% prompt radiation / 25% thermal / 5% delayed.

**Proliferation context:** peak combined US-USSR arsenal ~60,000 warheads (1986); post-2002 Moscow Treaty ~2,000 strategic each. Limiting factor is access to fissile material (enriched uranium / plutonium), as the design knowledge is widely available.
