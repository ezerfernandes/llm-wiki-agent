## 28.0 Introduction to Special Relativity

### Objectives
- Grasp Einstein's foundational postulates (constancy of light speed; consistency of physical laws across inertial frames).
- See how observers in different reference frames measure space and time differently.
- Understand relativistic effects on momentum and energy at high speed.
- Apply relativistic ideas to real technologies.

### Definitions
- **Relativity**: the study of how different observers measure the same event across reference frames.
- **Special relativity**: applies to observers moving at constant velocity relative to one another (no acceleration, no gravity).
- **General relativity**: extends to accelerating frames and gravitation.
- **Time dilation**: time runs slower for an observer in relative motion.
- **Length contraction**: lengths shrink along the direction of motion at relativistic speed.

### Principles
- At everyday speeds (~1% of c) and macroscopic scales, relativistic mechanics reduces to Newtonian mechanics (classical limit).
- Velocities do not simply add; they combine through modified formulas that keep c as an upper limit.

### Applications
- GPS / satellite navigation requires relativistic corrections for accuracy.
- High-energy particle physics requires relativistic treatment.

## 28.1 Einstein's Postulates

### Objectives
- State and interpret Einstein's two postulates.
- Define an inertial reference frame.
- Note one way light's speed can be altered.

### Definitions
- **Inertial reference frame**: a coordinate system in which a body at rest stays at rest, and a moving body keeps constant velocity in a straight line, unless acted on by a net force. Physics laws take their simplest form here.
- **Special relativity**: theory built from two postulates describing high-speed (non-gravitational) phenomena.

### Laws / Principles
- **First postulate**: the laws of physics are the same (and take their simplest form) in all inertial frames. No preferred frame exists.
- **Second postulate**: the speed of light c in vacuum is constant, independent of the motion of the source or observer.
- **Michelson–Morley experiment**: showed light's vacuum speed is independent of Earth's orbital motion — light needs no medium (no "ether").

### Equations
- c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s
- E = mc² (named here as a consequence of the postulates)

### Implications
- Classical momentum and energy must be redefined.
- Observers disagree on time intervals and distances; matter and energy can interconvert.
- Caveat: light travels at c only in vacuum; in matter its speed drops to c/n (n = refractive index).

## 28.2 Simultaneity and Time Dilation

### Objectives
- Explain why simultaneity is relative.
- Describe time dilation; compute the Lorentz factor γ.
- Distinguish proper time from observed (dilated) time.
- Resolve the twin paradox.

### Definitions
- **Simultaneity**: two events are simultaneous only if a given observer measures them at the same instant; observers in relative motion may disagree.
- **Time dilation**: time passes more slowly for an observer moving relative to another.
- **Proper time (Δt₀)**: the time interval measured by an observer at rest relative to the event; this is the shortest possible interval for the process.
- **Lorentz factor (γ)**: dimensionless factor quantifying relativistic effects; grows without bound as v → c.

### Laws / Principles
- **Relativity of simultaneity**: events simultaneous in one frame need not be in another. The order of causally connected events is preserved for all observers.
- Speed of light is invariant in all inertial frames.
- SR applies only to inertial (non-accelerated) frames.

### Equations
- Δt = Δt₀ / √(1 − v²/c²) = γ Δt₀
- γ = 1 / √(1 − v²/c²)
- Light-path on the moving "light clock": s = √(D² + (vΔt/2)²)
- Derivation relation: (Δt)² = (Δt₀)² + (v²/c²)(Δt)²

### Worked Example (concept)
- **Cosmic muon decay**: a muon at 0.950c with proper lifetime 1.52 μs has γ = 3.20, so Earth observers measure its lifetime as 4.87 μs. Confirms moving clocks run slow; experimentally verified.

### Applications
- GPS: orbiting satellite clocks experience time dilation; without correction, positioning errors accumulate within minutes.

### Twin Paradox
- An astronaut traveling at γ = 30.0 ages 2 years on a round trip while her Earth twin ages 60 years. The apparent paradox (each sees the other moving) is resolved by asymmetry: the traveler accelerates/decelerates/re-accelerates, so her frame is non-inertial. SR applies only to inertial frames; the situation is genuinely asymmetric and the traveler really ages less.

## 28.3 Length Contraction

### Objectives
- Define proper length.
- Compute contracted lengths.
- Explain why the effect is negligible at everyday speeds.

### Definitions
- **Proper length (L₀)**: the distance between two points measured by an observer at rest relative to both points (e.g., Earth observers measure the proper Earth–muon-production distance).
- **Length contraction (L)**: the reduction in measured length of an object moving relative to the observer, occurring only along the direction of motion.

### Principles
- All observers agree on relative velocity even though they measure different times; since v = distance/time, they must also measure different distances to keep v consistent.

### Equations
- v = L₀ / Δt   (Earth frame)
- v = L / Δt₀   (moving frame)
- L = L₀ / γ = L₀ √(1 − v²/c²)
- γ = 1 / √(1 − v²/c²)

### Worked Example (concept)
- **Distance to Alpha Centauri**: an astronaut at γ = 30 sees the 4.3 ly distance contracted to 0.143 ly, so she can traverse it without exceeding c in her own frame.

### Applications
- SLAC's 3 km linac appears only ~0.5 m long to high-speed electrons, easing beam alignment.
- Compression of a charged particle's electric field lines along its motion has observable detector effects.

## 28.4 Relativistic Addition of Velocities

### Objectives
- Compute how velocities combine at relativistic speeds.
- Recognize when relativistic vs. classical addition applies.
- Compute the frequency/wavelength shift of light from a moving source.

### Definitions
- **Classical velocity addition**: velocities add as simple sums (u = v + u′).
- **Relativistic velocity addition**: a correction prevents combined velocities from exceeding c.
- **Relativistic Doppler shift**: observed wavelength/frequency changes with relative source–observer motion; receding → redshift, approaching → blueshift.

### Laws / Principles
- Second postulate: light always moves at c relative to every observer; classical addition cannot apply to light.

### Equations
- Classical: u = v + u′
- Relativistic: u = (v + u′) / (1 + v u′ / c²)
- Doppler (wavelength): λ_obs = λ_s √[(1 + u/c)/(1 − u/c)]
- Doppler (frequency): f_obs = f_s √[(1 − u/c)/(1 + u/c)]
  - u > 0: moving away; u < 0: moving toward.

### Worked Examples (concept)
- **Light-speed constancy**: a ship at 0.500c emits a laser toward Earth; light still arrives at c, not 1.500c.
- **Asymmetric combination**: a ship at 0.500c fires a canister at 0.750c — toward Earth observers measure 0.909c (not 1.250c); away they measure 0.400c. Relativistic addition lacks classical symmetry.
- **Galactic redshift**: a galaxy receding at 0.825c emits 0.525 m radio waves, observed stretched to 1.70 m.

### Applications
- Radar speed measurement; astronomy (stellar velocities, galactic distances, quasars, black holes); radio communications from fast-moving sources.

## 28.5 Relativistic Momentum

### Objectives
- Compute momentum for objects near light speed.
- Explain why rest mass is the only meaningful mass.

### Definitions
- **Relativistic momentum**: product of rest mass, velocity, and the Lorentz factor.
- **Rest mass (m)**: the mass measured by an observer at rest relative to the object — the only well-defined mass.

### Laws / Principles
- **Conservation preserved**: momentum (defined with γ) is conserved at relativistic speeds, satisfying the first postulate.
- **Classical limit**: at low speed γ ≈ 1, so p → mu.
- **Speed-limit implication**: as u → c, p → ∞, so massive objects cannot reach c.

### Equations
- p = γ m u
- γ = 1 / √(1 − u²/c²)

### Worked Example (concept)
- **Electron at 0.985c**: shows how γ dramatically amplifies momentum relative to the non-relativistic mu prediction.

### Applications
- High-energy collisions in accelerators; subatomic particle behavior; modern collision analysis.

## 28.6 Relativistic Energy

### Objectives
- Compute total energy of a relativistic object.
- Compute relativistic kinetic energy.
- Explain rest energy and mass-energy conversion.
- Explain why massive objects cannot reach c.

### Definitions
- **Rest energy (E₀)**: intrinsic energy of a stationary mass; matter–energy equivalence.
- **Total energy (E)**: rest energy plus energy of motion; → ∞ as v → c.
- **Relativistic kinetic energy**: motional energy for high-speed objects; diverges from classical at high speed.
- **Mass-energy equivalence**: mass is a form of energy; small mass holds enormous energy.

### Equations
- Rest energy: E₀ = mc²
- Total energy: E = γ mc²,  γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²)
- Relativistic kinetic energy: KE_rel = (γ − 1) mc²
- Energy–momentum relation: E² = (pc)² + (mc²)²
- Classical limit: KE_class = ½ m v²

### Laws / Principles
- **Relativistic conservation of energy**: energy is conserved in all inertial frames when mass-energy conversion is included.
- **Ultimate speed limit**: no massive object can reach c (would need infinite energy).
- **Classical transition**: at v ≪ c the relativistic forms reduce to classical ones (binomial expansion).

### Worked Examples (concept)
- **Rest-mass energy scale (28.6)**: 1 g of matter ≈ 9 × 10¹³ J — nuclear-weapon scale; explains why rest energy is unnoticed daily.
- **Stored energy (28.7)**: a charged car battery's mass rises by ~10⁻¹⁰ kg (a billionth of a percent) — why mass-energy change is undetectable outside nuclear processes.
- **High-velocity KE (28.8)**: an electron at 0.990c has relativistic KE ~12.4× the classical prediction.

### Applications
- Nuclear reactors/weapons (fission/fusion mass deficits); stellar fusion and geothermal (radioactive decay) energy; particle accelerators (SLAC, Fermilab) creating new particles from kinetic energy; cosmic-ray physics where E ≈ pc and rest mass is negligible (ultra-relativistic).
