## 24.0 Introduction

### Objectives
- See how electromagnetic (EM) waves are predicted by Maxwell's equations and how they differ fundamentally from mechanical waves.
- Recognize that all EM phenomena — from visible light to gamma rays — are manifestations of one underlying wave phenomenon.
- Survey how EM waves are produced, categorized across the spectrum, and how they transport energy.

### Key ideas
- An **electromagnetic wave** is a self-propagating disturbance of oscillating electric and magnetic fields; it needs no medium and travels through vacuum at the speed of light.
- A central contrast with **sound** (which needs a material medium): EM waves traverse empty space.
- The same physics underlies the whole spectrum despite vastly different observable behavior. Radio, for example, encodes sound as EM signal, transmits it, and decodes it back to acoustic vibration.

### Applications
X-ray medical imaging, microwave heating, radar navigation, human vision (visible light), and tracking spacecraft/celestial objects via EM signals.

## 24.1 Maxwell's Equations: Electromagnetic Waves Predicted and Observed

### Objectives
- Restate Maxwell's four foundational equations of electromagnetism.

### Terms
- **Maxwell's equations** — four mathematical statements unifying all known electric and magnetic phenomena (mid-19th century).
- **Electric constant (ε₀)** — permittivity of free space; sets the strength of electric forces.
- **Magnetic constant (μ₀)** — permeability of free space; sets the strength of magnetic forces.
- **Electromagnetic wave** — oscillating, mutually perpendicular E and B fields propagating perpendicular to both.

### Maxwell's four equations (paraphrased)
1. **Gauss's law for electricity** — electric field lines begin on positive charges and end on negative charges; field strength relates to ε₀.
2. **Gauss's law for magnetism** — magnetic field lines are continuous loops with no start or end; magnetic monopoles do not exist.
3. **Faraday's law of induction** — a changing magnetic field induces an emf and an electric field that opposes the change.
4. **Ampère–Maxwell law** — moving charges *and* changing electric fields generate magnetic fields. Maxwell's key addition: an oscillating/changing electric field itself creates a magnetic field.

### Key insight
Maxwell introduced symmetry: just as a changing magnetic field produces an electric field (Faraday), a changing electric field produces a magnetic field. The two are "different manifestations of the same thing — the electromagnetic force."

### Equations
- Speed of EM waves: `c = 1 / sqrt(μ₀ ε₀)`
- Numerical check: `c = 1 / sqrt[(8.85×10⁻¹² C²/N·m²)(4π×10⁻⁷ T·m/A)] = 3.00×10⁸ m/s`
- Resonant frequency (Hertz's RLC circuit): `f₀ = 1 / (2π sqrt(LC))`
- Wave relationship: `v = f λ`

### Historical verification
- **Prediction**: Maxwell computed that EM waves travel at the measured speed of light, concluding "light is an electromagnetic wave."
- **Confirmation**: Heinrich Hertz (1887) built an AC RLC circuit feeding a wire loop with a small gap. Sparks across the gap radiated EM waves; a second, tuned receiving loop across the room sparked when waves arrived. Measuring interference patterns, Hertz found the wavelength and confirmed propagation at light speed — validating Maxwell years after Maxwell's death.
- **Significance**: called "the greatest triumph of physics since Newton"; unified electricity, magnetism, and optics and opened the way to radio and wireless communication. The SI unit of frequency, the hertz, honors him.

## 24.2 Production of Electromagnetic Waves

### Objectives
- Describe how E and B field components emanate from sources such as an AC generator/antenna.
- State the relationship between the magnitudes of E and B in an EM wave.
- Compute maximum B from maximum E.

### Terms
- **Transverse wave** — field oscillations perpendicular to the propagation direction.
- **Broadcast antenna** — conductor that radiates EM waves by sustaining oscillating charge driven by AC current.

### Principles
- **Production**: an AC source drives charge up and down an antenna; the accelerating charge creates a propagating E field, and (via Ampère's law) the antenna current creates circular B field lines. Both propagate outward at speed c.
- **In phase**: E and B oscillate synchronously, reaching maxima and minima together.
- **Orthogonality**: E and B are mutually perpendicular and both perpendicular to the direction of propagation (a transverse wave).
- **Reception**: a receiver antenna detects waves as the incoming field accelerates its electrons into a standing-wave pattern; resonance/tuning selects the frequency.

### Equations
- Field-strength ratio in vacuum: `E / B = c`
- Magnetic field from electric field: `B = E / c`
- In a medium of refractive index n: `E / B = c / n`

### Worked example (Example 24.1)
Calculating B fields in a strong EM wave: with E = 1000 V/m, the corresponding B ≈ 3.33×10⁻⁶ T — far weaker than Earth's field, illustrating how the magnetic component of an EM wave is numerically tiny relative to a "strong" electric component (because of the large factor c).

### Applications
Radio/TV broadcasting via resonant antennas; microwave ovens (oscillating current in a hidden antenna); signal reception; resonance tuning by adjusting antenna dimensions/electrical properties.

## 24.3 The Electromagnetic Spectrum

### Objectives
- State three general rules of thumb for EM-wave behavior across frequency.
- Explain the inverse relationship between frequency and wavelength.
- Sketch the spectrum and describe how each band is produced.

### Terms
- **Frequency (f)** — cycles per second (Hz).
- **Wavelength (λ)** — distance between successive crests; inversely proportional to f.
- **Wave speed (v_W / c)** — propagation velocity.

### Wave equation
- `c = f λ`, with c = 3.00×10⁸ m/s in vacuum. Higher frequency ⇒ shorter wavelength.

### Three rules of thumb
1. **Energy & penetration** — higher-frequency waves carry more energy and penetrate matter more effectively.
2. **Information capacity** — higher frequencies can encode more information per unit time.
3. **Spatial resolution** — shorter wavelengths resolve finer detail (resolution limit ≈ wavelength).

### Transmission, reflection, absorption
At a boundary, EM radiation may be transmitted, reflected, and/or absorbed, often simultaneously. Transparency is frequency-dependent (glass passes visible light but is opaque to UV).

### Worked example concepts
- **Wavelength from frequency** — apply `c = f λ`: AM radio at 1530 kHz → λ ≈ 196 m; a cell phone at 1.90 GHz → λ ≈ 0.158 m. Explains why long wavelengths need large antennas and diffract around obstacles.
- **Laser eye surgery (UV)** — high-frequency UV delivers precise energy to tissue (thermal energy + phase change), showing how short wavelengths enable precision.

### Spectrum bands

**Radio & TV** — ~50 Hz to ~1000 MHz. Produced by accelerating charges in circuits/conductors. Longest wavelengths (km for power lines). AM (540–1600 kHz), FM (88–108 MHz), VHF/UHF TV. Very long wavelengths penetrate salt water (submarine comms). Uses: broadcasting, remote controls, MRI (~100 MHz), power transmission. **AM** varies amplitude (constant frequency); **FM** varies frequency (constant amplitude), inherently less noise-prone.

**Microwave** — ~10⁹ to ~10¹² Hz. Produced by circuit resonance and thermal molecular motion. Needs line-of-sight. Uses: satellite/terrestrial comms, radar (Doppler shift → velocity; λ limits resolution), microwave ovens (water absorbs ~2.45 GHz via dielectric heating), microwave diathermy, planetary mapping (penetrates Venus's clouds).

**Infrared** — overlaps microwaves up to the low end of visible light. Produced by thermal motion and molecular vibration/rotation plus electronic transitions. Emitted strongly by warm objects (skin emissivity ≈ 0.97). Uses: thermal imaging (night vision, building heat-loss, reconnaissance), heat lamps, blackbody studies. **Greenhouse effect**: CO₂ and H₂O absorb Earth's emitted IR and re-radiate it downward, warming the surface ~40°C above the no-greenhouse case.

**Visible light** — 400–750 nm. Produced by atomic/molecular vibrations and electronic transitions. The narrow band the eye detects. Red = lowest frequency/longest λ; violet = highest frequency/shortest λ. Detected via electronic transitions in retinal molecules. Uses: vision, photosynthesis, optics.

**Ultraviolet** — ~400 nm down to ~10 nm. Produced by electronic transitions and thermal motion. **UV-A** (320–400 nm, ~99% of solar UV at surface), **UV-B** (290–320 nm, mostly absorbed by ozone), **UV-C** (220–290 nm, fully absorbed by ozone). Damages collagen and DNA; UV-B is the primary skin-cancer cause; tanning (melanin) is protective; can cause cataracts; UV-B drives vitamin-D synthesis (~10 min/day). Uses: sterilization, treating skin conditions/jaundice, fluorescence analysis, finer-than-visible imaging. Ozone depletion (CFC breakdown) lets more UV-B/C reach the surface.

**X-rays** — overlap high-frequency UV and extend much higher. Produced by (1) **characteristic X-rays** (high-voltage electrons eject inner-shell electrons; cascades emit characteristic energies) and (2) **bremsstrahlung** ("braking radiation" as fast electrons decelerate in collisions). Highly penetrating, damage is density-dependent. Uses: medical imaging (radiography, CT), security screening, X-ray diffraction (key to DNA structure), X-ray fluorescence for trace elements. Can induce cancer but also treat it.

**Gamma rays** — highest-frequency EM radiation; overlap the X-ray upper end. Produced by nuclear decay and reactor/weapon processes. Named "gamma" because their nature was unknown at 1896 discovery. Most penetrating nuclear radiation; identical in properties to same-frequency X-rays, differing only by **source** (nuclear vs. atomic). Uses: nuclear medicine imaging, cancer therapy, food preservation, security scanning, astrophysical observation via space telescopes.

## 24.4 Energy in Electromagnetic Waves

### Objectives
- Relate EM-wave amplitude to energy transport.
- Find microwave-oven intensity from power and area.
- Compute peak E and B from intensity.

### Terms
- **EM-wave energy** — fields carry energy independent of absorption; field strength sets the work/energy magnitude.
- **Intensity (I)** — power per unit area (W/m²). For sinusoidal waves, peak = twice average: `I₀ = 2 I_ave`.
- **Amplitude relationship** — energy transport ∝ amplitude², common to strings, water, sound, and EM waves.

### Principles
- **Wave–particle duality** — EM radiation shows both wave and particle behavior; matter (electrons, protons) likewise shows wave behavior — a deep symmetry.
- **Resonant efficiency** — energy transfer peaks when wave frequency matches a system's natural frequency (e.g., microwaves at water-molecule resonance).

### Equations
- Average intensity (electric field): `I_ave = c ε₀ E₀² / 2`
- Average intensity (magnetic field): `I_ave = c B₀² / (2 μ₀)`
- Combined fields: `I_ave = E₀ B₀ / (2 μ₀)`
- Field relationship: `B₀ = E₀ / c`

### Worked example concept
**Microwave-oven analysis**: convert power/area into intensity, derive peak electric field from intensity, then compute the corresponding peak magnetic field via `B₀ = E₀ / c`.

### Applications
Microwave heating (resonant coupling), solar-energy assessment, gamma-ray biological hazard estimation, and predicting field strengths in appliances.
