## 3.0 Introduction to Two-Dimensional Kinematics

Extends the one-dimensional kinematics framework to motion confined to a plane (two-dimensional, e.g. a ball on a pool table) and, more generally, to three-dimensional motion (e.g. a vehicle on a mountain road). Curved paths describe most natural motion. The key tool is decomposing complex motion into independent components along perpendicular axes, so each can be analyzed separately.

Chapter sections: foundational 2D concepts, graphical vector methods, analytical vector methods, projectile motion, and addition of velocities (relative motion).

Real-world applications: tennis/basketball arcs, satellite orbits, swimming dives, blood flow, vehicles on curved roads.

## 3.1 Kinematics in Two Dimensions: An Introduction

**Learning objectives:** recognize that 2D motion comprises independent horizontal and vertical components; understand perpendicular components do not affect one another.

**Key terms:** vectors (magnitude + direction, drawn as arrows); two-dimensional motion (simultaneous perpendicular motion); independence of perpendicular motions.

**Principles:**
- Pythagorean theorem for perpendicular components: c = √(a² + b²). Total straight-line displacement of a city-block walk (e.g. 9 blocks east + 5 blocks north) is shorter than the path length walked.
- Independence principle: horizontal and vertical components of 2D motion are independent; motion in one direction does not affect the perpendicular direction, whether sequential or simultaneous.

**Applications:** urban grid navigation (displacement vs. distance), horizontally-thrown projectiles falling under gravity while keeping constant horizontal velocity.

## 3.2 Vector Addition and Subtraction: Graphical Methods

**Learning objectives:** rules for vector addition, subtraction, and scalar multiplication; graphical techniques to add/subtract vectors and find displacement.

**Key terms:** vector (magnitude + direction — displacement, velocity, acceleration, force); graphical representation (arrow: length = magnitude, orientation = direction); head-to-tail method (tail = start, head/tip = end); resultant (the vector sum); negative vector (same magnitude, opposite direction).

**Head-to-tail method:** draw vectors tip-to-tail; the resultant runs from the tail of the first to the head of the last. Measure magnitude (scaled length) and direction (protractor angle).

**Principles / equations:**
- Commutativity: A + B = B + A
- Subtraction: A − B = A + (−B)
- Scalar multiplication: positive scalar scales magnitude (direction unchanged); negative scalar scales magnitude and reverses direction.

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- Example 3.1: three-leg displacement (e.g. a hiker's path) — head-to-tail addition of multiple vectors to find resultant displacement.
- Example 3.2: navigation/heading-error scenario — vector subtraction via adding the negative (opposite-direction) vector.

**Applications:** navigation and trip planning (e.g. inter-island journeys), sailing/course correction, multi-segment displacement.

## 3.3 Vector Addition and Subtraction: Analytical Methods

**Learning objectives:** add/subtract vectors analytically with geometry and trigonometry; decompose a vector into perpendicular components; find resultant magnitude and direction from components.

**Key terms:** vector components (perpendicular projections along x and y axes; independent, recombinable); resultant vector; analytical methods (trigonometric, more accurate than graphical drawing).

**Principles:**
- Independence of perpendicular directions — components on different axes treated separately.
- Components on the SAME axis add algebraically like scalars; but vector magnitudes do not add directly (3 m east + 4 m north → 5 m resultant, not 7 m).

**Equations:**
- Resolve: Ax = A cos θ ; Ay = A sin θ
- Recombine magnitude: A = √(Ax² + Ay²)
- Recombine direction: θ = tan⁻¹(Ay / Ax)
- Add by components: Rx = Ax + Bx ; Ry = Ay + By
- Subtraction: A − B ≡ A + (−B), negating the subtracted vector's components.

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- City-walking problem: resolve a 10.3-block displacement at 29.1° into ~9.0 blocks east + 5.0 blocks north — computing components from magnitude and angle.
- Two-leg walk addition: add 53 m at 20° to 34 m at 63° → ~81.2 m at 36.6°, by summing x-components (~65.2 m) and y-components (~48.4 m) then applying the Pythagorean theorem and arctangent.

**Applications:** projectile motion, navigation, force analysis — anywhere perpendicular quantities behave independently and precision beyond graphical methods is needed.

## 3.4 Projectile Motion

**Learning objectives:** describe projectile properties (gravity, range, max height, trajectory shape); compute position and velocity anywhere on the path; apply independence of perpendicular motion to solve 2D problems.

**Key terms:** projectile motion (object launched into air, acted on only by gravity, air resistance neglected); trajectory (the curved path, a parabola); apex (highest point, where vertical velocity = 0); range R (horizontal distance on level ground).

**Principles:**
- Horizontal acceleration ax = 0 (no air resistance) → constant horizontal velocity.
- Vertical acceleration ay = −g = −9.80 m/s² (up = positive).
- Resolve initial velocity: v0x = v0 cos θ0 ; v0y = v0 sin θ0.

**Equations:**

Horizontal (ax = 0): x = x0 + vx t ; vx = v0x (constant)

Vertical (ay = −g):
- y = y0 + ½(v0y + vy) t
- vy = v0y − g t
- y = y0 + v0y t − ½ g t²
- vy² = v0y² − 2 g (y − y0)

Recombine:
- s = √(x² + y²) ; θ = tan⁻¹(y / x)
- v = √(vx² + vy²) ; θv = tan⁻¹(vy / vx)

Max height: h = v0y² / (2g)
Range (level ground): R = v0² sin(2θ0) / g

**Four-step method:** (1) resolve into horizontal + vertical components; (2) treat as two independent 1D motions; (3) solve each separately (time is the shared variable); (4) recombine to get total displacement and velocity.

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- Fireworks shell (≈70 m/s at 75°): use vertical motion to find max height; find time of flight; constant horizontal velocity sets the horizontal distance.
- Volcanic rock (25 m/s at 35°, lands 20 m below launch): solve a quadratic for flight time; compute impact speed and direction; vertical velocity is negative at impact below the launch point.

**Key insights:** Max range on level ground at θ0 = 45° (≈38° with air resistance). Complementary launch angles (summing to 90°) give equal ranges but different heights. For very large ranges, Earth's curvature matters; enough initial speed yields orbit.

**Applications:** cannon/ballistics range, sports (football, basketball), orbital mechanics (lunar golf drive travels farther due to lower g), volcanology (ejecta trajectories), fireworks display safety.

## 3.5 Addition of Velocities

**Learning objectives:** apply vector addition to find relative velocity between objects/frames; explain why velocity depends on the observer's reference frame.

**Key terms:** relative velocity (velocity measured from a given observer; differs with the observer's own motion); reference frame (coordinate system from which motion is measured — velocity is meaningless without it); classical relativity (relating observations across frames; valid for speeds below ~1% of c, < ~3,000 km/s).

**Principles:**
- Velocity is a vector; velocities combine by vector addition, not scalar arithmetic (except in 1D).
- Independence of perpendicular velocity components (e.g. a boat's heading velocity vs. a river's perpendicular current).
- The same event yields different observed trajectories in different frames, but physical outcomes are consistent.

**Equations:**
- Components: vx = v cos θ ; vy = v sin θ
- Magnitude: v = √(vx² + vy²)
- Direction: θ = tan⁻¹(vy / vx)

**Worked examples (concepts):**
- Boat crossing a river: vector addition of perpendicular boat and current velocities to get velocity relative to shore.
- Airplane in wind: solve for an unknown velocity (heading or wind) using component equations.
- Dropped coin in a moving vehicle: same event is a straight vertical fall to a passenger but a parabola to a stationary ground observer — frame dependence of trajectory.

**Applications:** aircraft correcting for crosswind, river crossing, motion seen from moving vehicles, general transportation relative-motion problems.
