## Introduction

The chapter treats optical systems — both biological (the eye) and engineered (corrective lenses, microscopes, telescopes) — as devices that form and manipulate images by refracting or reflecting light. Physics is framed as an "enabling science" whose understanding of image formation drives progress in biology and medicine. Six topics follow: the optics of the eye, vision correction, color perception, microscopes, telescopes, and optical aberrations. The chapter applies geometric-optics tools (thin lens equation, lens power, magnification) and previews where wave optics matters (resolution limits).

## 26.1 Physics of the Eye

### Objectives
- Describe how the eye forms a focused image on the retina.
- Explain why peripheral vision is less sharp and less color-sensitive.
- Identify refractive indices of eye components.
- Analyze how the eye changes focus for objects at different distances (accommodation).

### Definitions / Terms
- **Accommodation**: the process of adjusting the eye's focal length (by changing lens shape) so the image stays focused on the retina as object distance changes.
- **Refractive index**: material property setting how much light bends on entering; governs image formation.
- **Fovea**: retinal region of highest receptor density and sharpest acuity.
- **Near point**: closest distance at which an object can be focused sharply; conventionally 25 cm for normal vision.
- **Presbyopia**: age-related loss of accommodation due to lens stiffening.

### Laws / Principles
- The cornea and lens act together as a single converging system, producing a real, inverted image on the retina.
- The cornea supplies roughly two-thirds of the eye's focusing power because of the large air-to-cornea index change.
- For clear vision the image distance must equal the fixed lens-to-retina distance (~2.00 cm); focus is achieved by varying the eye's power, not its image distance.
- The ciliary muscle changes lens curvature/thickness to vary optical power.
- The eye detects light over a range spanning a factor of ~10^10 in intensity without damage.

### Equations
- Power of a lens: P = 1/f
- Thin lens equation (power form): P = 1/d_o + 1/d_i
- Magnification: m = h_i/h_o = − d_i/d_o

### Refractive Indices (eye components)
| Material | n |
|---|---|
| Air | 1.0 |
| Water | 1.33 |
| Aqueous humor | 1.34 |
| Vitreous humor | 1.34 |
| Cornea | 1.38 |
| Lens | 1.41 (variable; densest at center) |

### Worked Examples (concept only)
- **Example 26.1 — Retinal image size**: shows that a small object (a hair at arm's length) forms an extremely small retinal image (~4×10⁻⁴ cm), yet detail is recovered by retinal and brain processing. Illustrates the magnification relation.
- **Example 26.2 — Power range of the eye**: the eye's power ranges from ~50.0 D (relaxed, distant object) to ~54.0 D (fully accommodated, near object), about an 8% change for young adults; less in older eyes.

### Applications
- Distant objects: nearly parallel rays need minimal power; eye relaxed.
- Near objects: diverging rays demand greater curvature/power.
- Pupil adapts aperture to light intensity.
- Reading glasses (≈1.0–3.5 D) add converging power for presbyopia.
- Historical: Ibn al-Haytham established that light enters the eye (rather than being emitted by it).

## 26.2 Vision Correction

### Objectives
- Identify common vision defects.
- Explain mechanisms and corrections for myopia and hyperopia.
- Describe laser vision correction.

### Definitions / Terms
- **Myopia (nearsightedness)**: distant objects blur; the eye over-converges so rays focus in front of the retina.
- **Hyperopia (farsightedness)**: near objects blur; the eye under-converges, focusing behind the retina.
- **Far point**: farthest distance focused clearly (infinity for normal vision).
- **Near point**: nearest distance focused clearly (~25 cm normal).
- **Astigmatism**: uneven focus (often from corneal irregularity) giving direction-dependent blur.

### Laws / Principles
- **Myopia correction**: a diverging (concave, negative-power) lens forms a virtual image nearer than the object, within the wearer's far point.
- **Hyperopia correction**: a converging (convex, positive-power) lens forms an image farther than the object, beyond the wearer's near point.
- **Power addition**: powers of lenses placed close together add: P = P_1 + P_2 + …

### Equations
- P = 1/d_o + 1/d_i (P in diopters, distances in meters)

### Worked Examples (concept only)
- **Example 26.3 — Myopia correction**: finds the negative-power lens for a far point of 30.0 cm (≈ −3.51 D). Diverging lenses are thinnest at the center.
- **Example 26.4 — Hyperopia correction**: finds the positive-power lens letting a person with a 1.00 m near point read at 25.0 cm (≈ +3.24 D). Converging lenses are thickest at the center.

### Applications
- Spectacles: compensate the eye's deficiency without changing the eye.
- Contact lenses: fixed relative to the eye, better peripheral vision, can mask corneal surface defects via the tear layer.
- LASIK: a computer-controlled excimer laser (193 nm UV) ablates corneal tissue in ~0.3 μm layers to reshape the cornea; corrects myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism with >90% success.
- Cylindrical (asymmetric) lens components correct astigmatism.

## 26.3 Color and Color Vision

### Objectives
- State the basic theory of color vision.
- Describe how light sources display color via emitted wavelengths.
- Explain the Retinex theory.

### Definitions / Terms
- **Color**: perception tied to visible-light wavelength (~350–700 nm).
- **Photoreceptors**: light-sensing retinal cells.
  - **Rods**: ~120 million; ~1000× more sensitive than cones; peripheral and low-light vision; color-insensitive.
  - **Cones**: ~6 million; concentrated in the fovea; high-resolution color vision; three types.
- **Primary colors (cone peaks)**: ~420 nm (blue), ~520 nm (green), ~590 nm (yellow).
- **Color constancy**: perceiving an object's true color despite changing illumination.
- **Simplified theory of color vision / Retinex theory**: three cone systems form comparable images jointly processed by the eye-brain system to yield perceived color.

### Laws / Principles
- **Trichromatic (three-color) vision**: all perceived hues arise from combinations of the three cone responses; RGB displays exploit this.
- An object's true color comes from its absorption/reflection properties, independent of the illuminating source.
- Retinal nerve connections compare neighboring receptors before transmission, giving heightened sensitivity to edges over uniform illumination.
- Color matching is non-unique: different wavelength mixtures can look identical (e.g., pure yellow vs. red+green).

### Equations
- None (qualitative section).

### Applications
- Television/display RGB color mixing.
- Understanding color blindness via cone-sensitivity differences.
- Color appearance under different sources (sunlight, fluorescent, incandescent, laser).

## 26.4 Microscopes

### Objectives
- Survey microscope types.
- Explain how a compound microscope forms a magnified image with multiple lenses.

### Definitions / Terms
- **Compound microscope**: a multi-element system (more than one lens/mirror) combining an objective and an eyepiece for progressive magnification.
- **Objective lens**: primary magnifier (≈5×–100×) nearest the sample; usually parfocal so focus persists across magnifications.
- **Eyepiece (ocular)**: second lens stage that magnifies the intermediate image.
- **Numerical aperture (NA)**: light-gathering measure depending on medium index and acceptance angle.
- **Working distance**: objective-to-specimen separation (mm); decreases as NA/magnification rise.
- **f-number**: image-plane light intensity measure; inversely related to aperture diameter and NA.

### Laws / Principles
- Image-formation sequence: objective forms an enlarged inverted real image beyond its focal length; this intermediate image is the object for the eyepiece, which acts as a magnifying glass to form a further-magnified virtual image.
- Overall magnification is the **product** of individual magnifications, not the sum.
- Immersion media (oil, glycerin, water) reduce index discontinuities, allowing NA > 1.0 and improving resolution.

### Equations
- Overall magnification: m = m_o · m_e
- Objective magnification: m_o = − d_i/d_o
- Lens relation: 1/d_i = 1/f_o − 1/d_o
- Eyepiece magnification: m_e = − d_i'/d_o'
- Numerical aperture: NA = n sin α  (n = medium index, α = half acceptance angle)
- f-number: f/# = f/D ≈ 1/(2·NA)

### Worked Example (concept only)
- **Example 26.5 — Microscope magnification**: demonstrates sequential image formation through multiple lenses and cumulative magnification: locate the intermediate image with the lens formula, compute objective magnification, then eyepiece magnification (using the intermediate image as its object), then multiply (result −250×).

### Applications
- Optical microscopy: up to ~1500× with resolution near 0.2 μm.
- Electron microscopy: same principles with electrons, up to ~50 million× and single-atom localization; needs vacuum.
- Immersion techniques for higher light collection.
- Transmission, dark-field, and reflected-light illumination for contrast.
- Electronic scanning stitches point images for larger fields of view.

## 26.5 Telescopes

### Objectives
- Note the historical invention of the telescope.
- Explain how telescopes magnify distant objects and gather light.

### Definitions / Terms
- **Angular magnification (M)**: ratio of the angle subtended through the instrument to that seen by the unaided eye, M = θ'/θ.
- **Objective (lens or mirror)**: primary light-gathering element facing the distant object.
- **Eyepiece**: lens near the eye that magnifies the intermediate image.
- **Case 1 image**: real, inverted image formed by the objective at its focal length.
- **Case 2 image**: virtual, magnified image formed when the eyepiece magnifies the Case 1 image.

### Laws / Principles
- For a very distant object (d_o ≈ ∞), the intermediate image forms at the objective focal point: d_i = f_o.
- Greatest angular magnification needs a long-focal-length objective and a short-focal-length eyepiece.

### Equations
- Lens relation: 1/d_i = 1/f_o − 1/d_o = 1/f_o when d_o ≈ ∞, so d_i = f_o
- Angular magnification: M = θ'/θ = − f_o/f_e (negative sign = inverted image)

### Applications & Designs
- **Galilean**: convex objective + concave eyepiece; upright image; opera glasses, spyglasses.
- **Keplerian/refracting**: two convex lenses; inverted image; astronomy.
- **Erecting system**: third lens re-inverts for upright terrestrial use.
- **Reflecting**: concave-mirror objective avoids chromatic aberration and scales to large apertures for faint, distant objects.
- **Multi-spectrum**: radio (Australia Telescope), X-ray (Chandra), and adaptive-optics systems extend reach across the spectrum.

## 26.6 Aberrations

### Objectives
- Characterize optical aberrations and their effects on images.

### Definitions / Terms
- **Aberration**: a distortion in a real lens's image, deviating from thin-lens predictions.
- **Chromatic aberration**: wavelength-dependent refraction focuses different colors at different points with different magnifications (index varies with wavelength).
- **Coma**: off-axis aberration where lens zones miss a common focus, giving pear-shaped distortion.
- **Spherical aberration**: outer lens zones focus light closer than central zones, spreading the focus.
- **Astigmatism**: focus differs between two perpendicular planes (natural or manufactured).
- **Achromatic doublet**: converging crown-glass lens + diverging flint-glass lens, paired to largely cancel chromatic aberration.

### Laws / Principles
- Mirrors avoid chromatic aberration because reflection is wavelength-independent.
- Lens material, diameter, thickness, and object position all affect aberrations.
- Smaller apertures reduce aberrations but cut light throughput (dimmer image).
- Aspheric surfaces and graded-index (GRIN) materials correct aberrations.
- Known aberrations can be partly corrected by post-capture digital processing.

### Equations
- None (qualitative section).

### Applications
- Telescopes favor mirrors over lenses.
- High-end camera lenses use many elements.
- Advanced spectacle designs.
- Endoscopes with GRIN lenses at fiber ends.
- Digital aberration-correction algorithms in imaging software.
