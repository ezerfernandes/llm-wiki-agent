## 33.0 Introduction to Particle Physics

Particle physics studies subatomic constituents, seeking truly fundamental particles that have no internal structure. Matter is organized in a nested hierarchy: solids → molecules → atoms → nuclei → nucleons → quarks. Like the periodic table organized chemistry, particles display systematic patterns that allow classification and prediction. An open question remains whether quarks and electrons are the ultimate constituents or whether deeper structure exists. The Large Hadron Collider (LHC) at CERN (Switzerland–France border) probes these questions by colliding beams at extreme energies.

## 33.1 The Yukawa Particle and the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle Revisited

- **Carrier particle**: mediates a force between objects via field interactions.
- **Virtual particle**: briefly exists, permitted by quantum uncertainty; its effects are detectable but direct observation would permanently violate energy conservation.
- **Yukawa particle / pion**: proposed (Yukawa, 1935) as the exchange particle transmitting the strong nuclear force between nucleons; a **meson** (intermediate mass between electron and nucleon) that feels the strong force.

Equations:
- Heisenberg uncertainty (energy–time form): ΔE·Δt ≥ h/(4π)
- Maximum range: d ≈ c·Δt
- Mass–energy: ΔE = mc²

Worked example — Pion mass estimation: From the ~1 fermi (10⁻¹⁵ m) range of the strong force, derive existence time (d/c), then energy via the uncertainty relation, then convert to mass (MeV/c²). Yields ~100 MeV/c²; experimentally pions are 135–139.6 MeV/c². Illustrates connecting force range to carrier-particle mass.

History: Pions (π⁺, π⁻, π⁰) first seen in cosmic rays (1947), later created in accelerators. Muons appeared at the same time but do not feel the strong force.

## 33.2 The Four Basic Forces

- **Carrier particles** (gauge bosons) mediate the forces as virtual particles.
- **Feynman diagrams**: time on vertical axis, position on horizontal; visualize interactions and compute their probabilities/strengths.
- **Quantum electrodynamics (QED)**: the quantum mechanics of electromagnetism; extremely successful.

The four forces:

| Force | Relative strength | Range | Carrier particle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gravity | ~10⁻³⁸ | infinite | graviton (conjectured, massless) |
| Electromagnetic | ~10⁻² | infinite | photon (observed, massless) |
| Weak nuclear | ~10⁻¹³ | < 10⁻¹⁸ m | W⁺, W⁻, Z⁰ (observed, massive) |
| Strong nuclear | 1 | < 10⁻¹⁵ m | gluons (carrier; pions are composite) |

Key principles:
- Carrier mass ↔ range: massless carriers (photon, graviton, gluon) → long/infinite range; massive carriers (W, Z, ~1000× pion mass) → short range.
- All carrier particles have integer spin → bosons.
- Under extreme conditions the relative strengths may converge, hinting at unification.

History: Richard Feynman (1918–1988) developed Feynman diagrams and QED; shared 1965 Nobel Prize with Schwinger and Tomonaga.

## 33.3 Accelerators Create Matter from Energy

- **Cyclotron**: fixed-frequency alternating field accelerates particles in expanding circular orbits within a magnetic field.
- **Synchrotron**: voltage frequency and magnetic field increase together to keep orbital radius constant; reaches very high energies.
- **Synchrotron radiation**: EM radiation emitted by charged particles accelerated perpendicular to motion; an energy-loss factor but also a useful intense radiation source.
- **Linear accelerator (linac)**: straight line of aligned acceleration tubes; reduces synchrotron radiation losses (SLAC, 3.2 km, ~50 GeV).
- **Colliding beams**: head-on opposing beams create massive particles with near-zero net momentum, maximizing energy available for new matter.

Equations:
- m = ΔE/c² (more input energy → more matter created)
- PE = qV (energy gained per accelerating gap)
- 1 eV = (1 V)(1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C)

Worked example — Gap voltage: An 800-MeV proton beam through 2000 tubes needs 800 MV / 2000 = 400 kV per gap (achievable in vacuum). Illustrates distributing total beam energy over many acceleration stages.

Facilities: Van de Graaff (~50 MV, 1932); cyclotron (1930); SLAC linac (50 GeV); LHC at CERN (7 TeV per beam, proton-proton/heavy ion); Tevatron at Fermilab (proton-antiproton, ~2 TeV). Detectors capture the particle "spray" to reveal mass and charge.

## 33.4 Particles, Patterns, and Conservation Laws

- **Antimatter**: each particle has an antiparticle with opposite charge but identical mass, spin, and lifetime. Particle–antiparticle pairs annihilate to energy (usually photons). Predicted by Dirac (1928), positron confirmed (1932).
- **Hadrons**: feel the strong force (proton, neutron, pions). Subdivided into mesons and baryons.
- **Leptons**: do not feel the strong force, feel the weak force; six types (electron, muon, tau + their neutrinos); fundamental/pointlike.
- **Mesons**: hadrons that decay to leptons without producing other hadrons; not conserved in number; ~135–550 MeV/c².
- **Baryons**: hadrons that always decay into other baryons; nucleons + hyperons (Λ, Σ, Ξ, Ω); ~939–1673 MeV/c².
- **Bosons** (integer spin, no Pauli exclusion) vs **fermions** (half-integer spin, obey Pauli exclusion).
- **Strangeness (S)**: quantum number conserved by the strong force but not the weak force.

Conservation laws:
- Baryon number B: matter baryons +1, antibaryons −1, mesons/leptons 0; total B conserved.
- Lepton family numbers Lₑ, Lμ, Lτ each conserved separately (neutrino oscillations are exceptions).
- Charge conservation always holds.
- Strangeness conserved by strong force; weak decays may change S.

Decay processes (weak force unless noted):
- Muon: μ⁻ → e⁻ + ν̄ₑ + νμ (lepton family numbers conserved)
- Tau: τ⁻ → μ⁻ + ν̄μ + ντ
- Neutron beta decay: n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ (B stays +1)
- ⁸Be → α + α (strong-force decay, ~10⁻¹⁶ s, no leptons)

Worked examples:
- Ξ⁻ → Λ⁰ + π⁻: strangeness changes by 1 (−2 → −1) — weak decay; B and charge conserved; shows S not conserved by weak force.
- K⁺ → μ⁺ + νμ: allowed decay; charge, B, lepton family conserved; ΔS permitted via weak force.

Decay lifetimes by force: strong 10⁻²³–10⁻¹⁶ s; weak 10⁻¹²–10⁻¹⁶ s (longer ⇒ weak). The "particle zoo" of hundreds of particles showed patterns pointing to quark substructure.

## 33.5 Quarks: Is That All There Is?

- **Fundamental/elementary particle**: no internal structure or finite size beyond its wavelength.
- **Quarks**: second class of fundamental particles (after leptons); proposed 1963 by **Murray Gell-Mann** and **George Zweig**. Fermions (half-integer spin), fractional charge ±(2/3)qₑ or −(1/3)qₑ, combine into hadrons, cannot exist in isolation.

Six quark flavors:
| Flavor | Charge | Notable quantum number | Approx mass |
|---|---|---|---|
| up (u) | +2/3 qₑ | — | ~0.005 GeV/c² |
| down (d) | −1/3 qₑ | — | ~0.008 GeV/c² |
| strange (s) | −1/3 qₑ | strangeness −1 | ~0.50 GeV/c² |
| charm (c) | +2/3 qₑ | charm +1 | ~1.6 GeV/c² |
| bottom (b) | −1/3 qₑ | bottomness −1 | ~5 GeV/c² |
| top (t) | +2/3 qₑ | topness +1 | ~173 GeV/c² |

- **Color charge**: quantum property with three values per quark (red, green, blue); antiquarks carry anticolors. Underlies **quantum chromodynamics (QCD)**, the theory of the strong force via color exchange.
- **Quark confinement**: only colorless ("white") combinations are observable — three RGB quarks (baryons) or quark–anticolor pairs (mesons). Free quarks cannot exist.
- **Flavor conservation**: strong force cannot change quark flavor; weak force can (e.g. d ↔ u, s → u/d), explaining weak-force strangeness violation.
- Quarks obey the Pauli exclusion principle (must differ in spin or color within a baryon).

Composition rules and charges:
- Baryons = 3 quarks; antibaryons = 3 antiquarks; mesons = quark + antiquark.
- Proton (uud): (+2/3 + 2/3 − 1/3)qₑ = +qₑ
- Neutron (udd): (+2/3 − 1/3 − 1/3)qₑ = 0
- π⁺ (ud̄): (+2/3 + 1/3)qₑ = +qₑ
- Beta-minus at quark level: d → u + β⁻ + ν̄ₑ
- Baryon number: each quark +1/3, each antiquark −1/3 (baryons +1, mesons 0).
- Strangeness: each strange quark −1, others 0.

Worked example — Ξ⁰ (uss) quantum numbers: charge (+2/3 − 1/3 − 1/3) = 0; B = +1; S = −2; charm/topness = 0. Matches observed data; mirrors how Gell-Mann/Zweig validated the model and predicted the then-unseen Ω⁻.

Evidence and history: SLAC electron scattering (1967) revealed three pointlike charges inside protons; three-jet events in colliders match quark fragmentation. November Revolution (1974): J/ψ (cc̄) discovered by Ting and Richter (Nobel 1976); Upsilon (bb̄, 1976); top quark (Fermilab, 1995). Quarks and leptons organize into three families; higher generations decay down to stable u and d.

## 33.6 GUTs: The Unification of Forces

- **Grand Unified Theory (GUT)**: the four forces are different manifestations of one unified force at extreme conditions.
- **Electroweak theory**: gauge theory (Weinberg, Glashow, Salam, 1960s) showing EM and weak forces become identical at ~100 GeV.
- **Gluons (g)**: massless carriers of the strong force; eight types (six carry color/anticolor, two colorless); mediate quark interactions by exchanging color charge.
- **QCD**: theory of quark/gluon interactions via color charge.
- **Standard Model**: the combined framework of QCD + electroweak theory describing particles and force transmission.
- **Higgs boson**: massive particle tied to electroweak symmetry breaking, giving carrier particles mass; tentative LHC evidence at ~125 GeV.
- **Symmetry breaking**: carriers are distinct at ordinary energies but become unified/identical at high energies.

Equations / scales:
- Predicted W⁺, W⁻ ~81 GeV/c²; Z⁰ ~90 GeV/c².
- Proposed proton decay: p → π⁰ + e⁺ (violates baryon and electron-family number).
- Predicted proton lifetime ~10³¹ yr; experimental lower bound > 5×10³² yr.
- ~100 GeV ↔ 10⁻¹⁸ m (electroweak scale).
- GUT scale ~10¹⁵ GeV (electroweak + strong converge); TOE scale ~10¹⁹ GeV (gravity joins); superstring scale ~10⁻³⁵ m.

Conceptual illustrations: Z⁰ mediating electron–neutrino interaction (analogous to photon exchange); a red-antigreen gluon swapping color between a down and strange quark; pion exchange arising from internal quark–gluon dynamics; a force-strength-vs-energy graph where EM and weak merge near 100 GeV, then with strong at ~10¹⁵ GeV, then gravity at ~10¹⁹ GeV.

Applications: W/Z discovery at CERN (1983) confirmed electroweak theory (Rubbia, van der Meer, Nobel 1984); atomic spectra show weak-force effects; reduced quark momentum in electron scattering indicates gluons carry momentum; proton-decay experiments constrain GUTs; Big Bang cosmology and the cosmic microwave background test GUT-scale physics inaccessible to accelerators.
