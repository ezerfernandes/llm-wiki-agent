## Chapter Introduction

The chapter studies curved motion — especially circular paths traversed at constant speed (uniform circular motion) — and the forces, gravity foremost, that sustain such motion. Motion is categorized as pure translation (no rotation), pure rotation (all points trace arcs about one fixed point), or a mixture (e.g., a spinning puck sliding on ice). By Newton's first law, an object travels in a straight line at constant velocity unless a net external force acts; a curved path therefore requires a continuously applied force directed toward the center of curvature. Applications span race cars rounding curves, planetary orbits, and rotating machinery.

## 6.1 Rotation Angle and Angular Velocity

**Objectives:** define arc length, rotation angle, radius of curvature, and angular velocity; compute angular velocity (e.g., of a car wheel).

**Terms:**
- **Rotation angle (Δθ):** rotational displacement, defined as arc length over radius; measured in radians.
- **Arc length (Δs):** distance traveled along the circular path.
- **Angular velocity (ω):** rate of change of rotation angle; rad/s.
- **Tangential / linear speed (v):** linear speed of a point on the rim.
- **Radius of curvature (r):** distance from rotation axis to the point.

**Principles:** every point on a rigid rotating body sweeps the same angular displacement in the same time, regardless of its radius. One full revolution = 2π rad = 360°. Linear and angular velocity are proportional through the radius.

**Equations:**
- Δθ = Δs / r
- One revolution: Δθ = 2πr / r = 2π
- 1 rad = 360°/2π ≈ 57.3°
- ω = Δθ / Δt
- v = Δs / Δt
- v = rω, equivalently ω = v / r

**Worked example (car tire):** illustrates that higher linear speed gives higher angular speed, and at a given linear speed a larger-radius wheel turns more slowly. A 0.300 m tire at 15.0 m/s spins at 50.0 rad/s; a 1.20 m tire at the same speed at 12.5 rad/s.

**Applications:** automotive wheel analysis; effect of tire size on rotational rate; rotation direction conventions.

## 6.2 Centripetal Acceleration

**Objectives:** derive the centripetal acceleration expression; explain centrifuge operation.

**Terms:**
- **Centripetal acceleration (a_c):** acceleration of an object in uniform circular motion, always directed toward the center ("center-seeking").
- **Uniform circular motion:** constant-speed circular motion in which velocity *direction* changes continuously.

**Principles:** acceleration is any change in velocity — magnitude or direction. Even at constant speed, the continuous change in direction produces an acceleration toward the center. The magnitude follows from similar triangles of the velocity and radius vectors.

**Equations:**
- a_c = v² / r
- a_c = r ω²
- v = r ω
- Similar-triangles relation: Δv / v = Δs / r

**Worked examples:**
- *Highway curve (Ex. 6.2):* compares a_c on a gentle turn to g (a_c ≈ 0.128 g), noticeable to an unbelted passenger.
- *Ultracentrifuge (Ex. 6.3):* shows a_c reaching ~472,000 g, enabling rapid sample separation.

**Applications:** centrifuges separate materials by density (bacteria, viruses, blood cells, DNA, proteins); human centrifuges test astronaut g-tolerance.

## 6.3 Centripetal Force

**Objectives:** find the friction coefficient needed for tires on a turn; compute ideal banking angle and speed.

**Terms:**
- **Centripetal force (F_c):** any net force directed toward the center of a circular path, perpendicular to velocity. Sources include tension, gravity, friction, and the normal force on a banked surface.

**Principles:** Newton's second law applied to circular motion — net inward force equals mass times centripetal acceleration. For fixed mass and speed, a larger F_c yields a smaller radius (tighter curve).

**Equations:**
- F_c = m a_c
- F_c = m v² / r
- F_c = m r ω²
- r = m v² / F_c
- Flat curve friction: μ_s = v² / (r g)
- Ideal (frictionless) banking angle: θ = tan⁻¹(v² / (r g))

**Worked examples:**
- *Ex. 6.4:* friction supplies the centripetal force on a level curve; finds the minimum μ_s for safe turning.
- *Ex. 6.5:* the ideal banking angle depends only on speed and radius, not on vehicle mass.

**Applications:** banked race tracks; safe speeds on unbanked curves; road-condition effects on cornering.

## 6.4 Fictitious Forces and Non-inertial Frames: The Coriolis Force

**Objectives:** distinguish inertial from non-inertial frames; explain fictitious forces; characterize the Coriolis force.

**Terms:**
- **Inertial frame:** a non-accelerating frame in which Newton's laws hold directly and forces have real physical sources; Earth is approximately inertial.
- **Non-inertial frame:** an accelerating/rotating frame in which fictitious forces appear.
- **Fictitious (pseudo-) force:** an apparent force arising from observing motion in an accelerated frame, with no real physical origin (e.g., feeling pushed back during takeoff).
- **Centrifugal force:** the fictitious outward "force" felt in a rotating frame; distinct from centripetal force.
- **Coriolis force:** a fictitious force deflecting moving objects sideways (perpendicular to velocity) in a rotating frame.

**Principles:** objects move straight unless a real force acts (inertia). In a rotating/accelerating frame, observers misattribute the resulting apparent curvature to fictitious forces. Inertia is the deeper explanation than the invented force.

**Equations:** none given; treated qualitatively.

**Concepts illustrated:** car turning right (passenger continues straight, feels pushed left); merry-go-round rider thrown "outward" by inertia; ball released on a rotating platform curves relative to the surface (Coriolis); centrifuge sedimentation as inertia plus centripetal constraint.

**Applications:** Coriolis deflection drives storm rotation — counterclockwise hurricanes in the Northern Hemisphere, clockwise cyclones in the Southern; large-scale wind circulation.

## 6.5 Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation

**Objectives:** explain Earth's and the Moon's gravity; explain apparent weightlessness in orbit; evaluate the Cavendish experiment.

**Terms:**
- **Center of mass (CM):** mass-averaged position of a body/system; external gravity acts as if mass were concentrated there.
- **Gravitational constant (G):** universal proportionality constant, G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg².
- **Tidal bulge:** water displacement from differential gravitational pull.
- **Microgravity:** environment of negligible apparent acceleration relative to Earth's surface.

**Law:** every particle of mass attracts every other with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centers. The force acts along the line joining the centers and is always attractive.

**Equations:**
- F = G m M / r²
- g = G M / r²  (object mass cancels; ≈ 9.80 m/s² at Earth's surface)
- a_c = v² / r  or  a_c = r ω²
- Earth's mass: M = g r² / G

**Worked example (Ex. 6.6):** gravity as the centripetal force for the Moon — Earth's gravitational acceleration at lunar distance (2.70 × 10⁻³ m/s²) closely matches the centripetal acceleration the Moon's orbit requires (2.72 × 10⁻³ m/s²); the <1% gap reflects orbital ellipticity.

**Cavendish experiment:** Henry Cavendish (1731–1810) first accurately measured G in 1798 using a torsion balance — small attraction between ordinary masses (tens of kg) deflected a mirror-mounted suspension. His value is within ~1% of modern figures and let physicists compute Earth's mass via M = g r² / G. Later work (Eötvös) showed gravity depends only on mass, independent of material composition to five parts per billion; modern tests check the inverse-square law at sub-millimeter scales.

**Applications:** tides (two bulges, spring tides at alignment, neap tides at 90°); gravity supplies centripetal force for orbits at all scales; apparent weightlessness as continuous free fall; physiological effects of microgravity (muscle/bone loss, cardiovascular and immune changes); improved crystal growth in microgravity.

## 6.6 Satellites and Kepler's Laws: An Argument for Simplicity

**Objectives:** state Kepler's three laws; derive the third law for circular orbits; contrast Ptolemaic and Copernican models.

**Terms:**
- **Satellite:** a smaller body gravitationally bound in orbit around a much larger one.
- **Ellipse:** closed curve where the sum of distances to two foci is constant; a circle is the degenerate case with coincident foci.
- **Period (T):** time for one complete orbit.
- **Orbital radius (r):** average distance to the parent body's center.

**Kepler's Laws:**
1. **Law of Ellipses:** each planet orbits in an ellipse with the Sun at one focus.
2. **Law of Equal Areas:** the line from Sun to planet sweeps equal areas in equal times; a planet moves fastest at closest approach.
3. **Harmonic Law:** for two bodies orbiting the same parent, the ratio of period-squared equals the ratio of average-distance-cubed.

**Equations:**
- Third law (comparative): T₁² / T₂² = r₁³ / r₂³
- Derivation: G m M / r² = m v² / r  →  G M / r = v²
- With v = 2πr / T:  G M / r = 4π² r² / T²
- Period: T² = (4π² / (G M)) r³
- Mass from orbit: r³ / T² = G M / (4π²)

**Worked example (Earth satellite period):** uses the Moon (T = 27.3 d, r = 3.84 × 10⁸ m) as reference to find the period of a satellite at 1500 km altitude (r₂ = 7880 km), giving T₂ ≈ 1.93 h; demonstrates that orbital period at a given altitude is independent of satellite mass.

**Applications:** measuring masses of celestial bodies from orbital data; predicting positions of planets and moons; explaining orbital deviations (Mercury's precession via general relativity); satellite mission design.

**Underlying point:** Newton's gravitation law is the causal mechanism behind Kepler's descriptive laws; the constancy of r³/T² for satellites of one parent shows the unity and simplicity of physical law.
