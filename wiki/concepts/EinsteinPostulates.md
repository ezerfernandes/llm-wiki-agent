## Definition
Einstein's two postulates are the foundation of [[SpecialRelativity|special relativity]]. The **first postulate** (principle of relativity) states that the laws of physics take the same simplest form in every inertial [[ReferenceFrame|reference frame]] — no frame is preferred. The **second postulate** states that the [[SpeedOfLightPhysics|speed of light]] c in vacuum is the same for all observers, independent of the motion of source or observer.

## Key Points
- An **inertial reference frame** is one in which a body at rest stays at rest and a moving body keeps constant straight-line velocity unless a net force acts; physics laws are simplest there.
- The second postulate breaks classical intuition: light cannot be "caught up to," which is why velocities do not simply add ([[RelativisticVelocityAddition]]).
- The **Michelson–Morley experiment** found light's vacuum speed independent of Earth's orbital motion, undermining the "ether" and supporting the second postulate.
- Together the postulates force [[TimeDilation|time]] and [[LengthContraction|length]] to be observer-dependent and require redefining [[RelativisticMomentum|momentum]] and [[RelativisticEnergy|energy]].
- Caveat: light moves at c only in vacuum; in a medium it slows to c/n (n = refractive index).

## Equations
- c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s (invariant in vacuum for all inertial observers)
