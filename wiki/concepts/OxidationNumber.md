# Oxidation Number

The **oxidation number** (or **oxidation state**) of an atom is the charge it *would* have if the compound were purely ionic. It is a bookkeeping device for tracking electron distribution, and a change in oxidation number is what defines an [[OxidationReduction|oxidation-reduction reaction]].

## Rules for Assigning Oxidation Numbers

1. An atom in an **elemental substance** has oxidation number **0** (e.g. Na, O₂, P₄).
2. A **monatomic ion** has an oxidation number equal to its charge (e.g. Na⁺ is +1, S²⁻ is −2).
3. For common nonmetals in compounds:
   - **Hydrogen:** **+1** with nonmetals; **−1** with metals.
   - **Oxygen:** **−2** in most compounds; **−1** in peroxides (O₂²⁻); rarely −½ in superoxides (O₂⁻); **positive** when combined with fluorine.
   - **Halogens:** **−1** for F always; −1 for other halogens *except* when combined with oxygen or another halogen (then positive).
4. The **sum** of oxidation numbers over all atoms equals the overall charge: **0** for a neutral molecule, the ion charge for a polyatomic ion.

## Notation Caution

Ionic **charge** is written number-then-sign (e.g. `2+`), whereas an **oxidation number** is written sign-then-number (e.g. `+2`).

## Use

Compute oxidation numbers for each element on both sides of an equation. An element whose value **increases** is oxidized; one whose value **decreases** is reduced — see [[OxidationReduction]].
