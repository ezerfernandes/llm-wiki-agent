# Word2Vec

A family of shallow neural models — [[SkipGram|skip-gram]] (predict context words from a center word) and [[CBOW|continuous bag of words]] (predict the center word from averaged context words) — that learn dense [[WordEmbedding|word embeddings]] from co-occurrence ([[TomasMikolov|Mikolov]] et al. 2013, at [[google|Google]]). Each word has two $d$-dim vectors (as center / as context); the conditional probability is a softmax over dot products. Trained tractably via [[NegativeSampling]] or [[HierarchicalSoftmax]] to avoid the $\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{V}|)$ softmax bottleneck.

Foundational pre-[[Transformer]] representation, now largely subsumed by contextual encoders like [[bert]], but its **conceptual framework** — self-supervised dense distributed representations trained from co-occurrence — anchors every successor (including [[GloVe]], [[FastText]], and the input-embedding layer of every modern LLM). See [[d2l-nlp-pretraining]] for the from-scratch implementation walk-through on [[PTB]].

## From [[hands-on-llm-ch01-introduction-to-llms|*Hands-On LLMs* Ch 1]]

[[JayAlammar|Alammar]] and [[MaartenGrootendorst|Grootendorst]] introduce word2vec in Ch 1 as the **dense-embedding pivot** in the history of [[LanguageAI|Language AI]]:

> "Released in 2013, word2vec was one of the first successful attempts at capturing the meaning of text in embeddings. Embeddings are vector representations of data that attempt to capture its meaning. To do so, word2vec learns semantic representations of words by training on vast amounts of textual data, like the entirety of Wikipedia." — Ch 1

The chapter's training-procedure framing: each vocabulary word gets an initial random vector (e.g., 50 dimensions); training pairs are *(word, candidate-neighbor)*; the model predicts whether the pair are likely neighbors in a sentence; co-occurring words' embeddings get pulled closer together.

Ch 1 contrasts word2vec with [[BagOfWords|bag-of-words]] explicitly: bag-of-words is *document-level* and *order-insensitive*; word2vec is *word-level* and captures semantic similarity via geometric proximity in the embedding space. The chapter forward-references Ch 2 for the deep-dive on word2vec's training procedure and Ch 5 for n-dimensional embedding compression.

The chapter also names the **static-embedding limitation** that motivated the move to context-sensitive representations:

> "The training process of word2vec creates static, downloadable representations of words. For instance, the word 'bank' will always have the same embedding regardless of the context in which it is used. However, 'bank' can refer to both a financial bank as well as the bank of a river. Its meaning, and therefore its embeddings, should change depending on the context." — Ch 1

— motivating the [[RNN|RNN]]-encoder-decoder + [[Attention|attention]] → [[transformer|Transformer]] → [[bert|BERT]]-context-sensitive-embedding arc the rest of the chapter narrates.

## From [[hands-on-llm-ch02-tokens-and-embeddings|*Hands-On LLMs* Ch 2]]

Ch 2 is the wiki's **canonical pedagogical walk-through of the word2vec training procedure**. The chapter's framing:

**Step 1 — generate training examples via a [[SkipGram|sliding window]].** Example sentence: *"Thou shalt not make a machine in the likeness of a human mind"* (from [[FrankHerbert|Frank Herbert]]'s *Dune*). With window size 2, the (center, context) pairs are generated by sliding the window across the text; each window produces up to $2m$ training examples.

**Step 2 — recast as binary classification.** Each pair (`word`, `candidate-neighbor`) is labeled 1 (true neighbor) or 0 (not). The model is a neural network that takes two input words and outputs a probability.

**Step 3 — [[NegativeSampling|negative sampling]].** Without negative examples *"a model can cheat and ace it by outputting 1 all the time."* The fix: enrich the dataset with **randomly-sampled non-neighbor pairs** labeled 0. *"It turns out that we don't have to be too scientific in how we choose the negative examples"* — random sampling works. Inspired by [[NoiseContrastiveEstimation|noise-contrastive estimation]] (Gutmann & Hyvärinen 2010).

**Step 4 — train the embedding matrix.** A `vocab_size × embedding_dim` matrix of randomly-initialized vectors; the training loop updates the relevant rows on each step so that co-occurring words' vectors move closer and non-co-occurring words' vectors move apart.

**Two main ideas of word2vec** (Figure 2-14): **[[SkipGram|skip-gram]]** (selecting neighboring words from text) + **[[NegativeSampling|negative sampling]]** (adding random non-neighbor pairs).

**The pattern's generalization beyond words.** The chapter's punchline:

> "This idea of a model that takes two vectors and predicts if they have a certain relation is one of the most powerful ideas in machine learning, and time after time has proven to work very well with language models." — Ch 2

The chapter forward-references **Chapter 10** (contrastive training for sentence-embedding models) and **Chapter 9** (the same idea for image-caption alignment in multimodal models) as direct descendants. The chapter also includes the [[Word2VecRecommender|song-recommendation worked example]] — applying word2vec to **playlist sequences** to learn song similarities — demonstrating that the algorithm is useful far beyond NLP.

**Pretrained embeddings via [[Gensim]]** (Ch 2 worked example):
```python
import gensim.downloader as api
model = api.load("glove-wiki-gigaword-50")  # 66MB, GloVe trained on Wikipedia, 50-dim
model.most_similar([model['king']], topn=11)
# [('king', 1.0), ('prince', 0.82), ('queen', 0.78), ('emperor', 0.77), ...]
```

## From [[hands-on-llm-ch10-creating-text-embedding-models|*Hands-On LLMs* Ch 10]]

Ch 10 cites word2vec as **the historical predecessor of [[ContrastiveLearning|contrastive learning]] in NLP** — one of the first methods to operationalize the *"learn by contrast"* paradigm at scale:

> *"One of the earliest and most popular examples of contrastive learning in NLP is actually word2vec, as we discussed in Chapters 1 and 2. The model learns word representations by training on individual words in a sentence. A word close to a target word in a sentence will be constructed as a positive pair whereas randomly sampled words constitute dissimilar pairs. In other words, positive examples of neighboring words are contrasted with randomly selected words that are not neighbors. Although not widely known, it is one of the first major breakthroughs in NLP that leverages contrastive learning with neural networks."* — Ch 10

The structural lineage Ch 10 establishes: **word2vec → modern sentence-embedding contrastive learning** is the same paradigm applied at different granularities. Word2vec contrasts (center, neighbor) pairs against random non-neighbors at the **word** level; [[SBERT|Sentence-BERT]] contrasts (anchor, positive) sentence pairs against in-batch negatives at the **sentence** level; [[CLIP]] contrasts (image, caption) pairs against random non-matches at the **multimodal** level. The architectural specifics differ — shallow embedding matrix vs Transformer encoder vs dual-encoder — but the contrastive objective is the through-line.
