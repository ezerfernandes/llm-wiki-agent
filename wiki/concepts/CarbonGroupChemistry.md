# Carbon Group Chemistry (Carbon, Carbonates, and Silicon)

This page covers the descriptive *inorganic* chemistry of **carbon** (a nonmetal) and its **carbonates**, the contrast between CO₂ and SiO₂, and the place of silicon/germanium in group 14. (Climate-domain pages such as CarbonEmissions/CarbonFootprint are unrelated; silicon's semiconductor chemistry is at [[Metalloids]].) The vastly larger chemistry of carbon–carbon and carbon–hydrogen compounds — exploiting the same sp³/sp²/sp carbon bonding seen here in diamond and graphite — is **[[OrganicChemistry|organic chemistry]]** (note that carbonates, CO, CO₂, and cyanides are conventionally *not* organic).

## Carbon Allotropes
- **Diamond:** each C forms four single bonds (sp³, tetrahedral) in a 3-D network (giant molecule), very hard, mp ~4400 °C.
- **Graphite:** layers of sp² carbon (three σ bonds, trigonal planar) with delocalized π bonds within layers (resonance) and weak London forces between layers — soft, conductive (pencil "lead", lubricant).
- **Buckminsterfullerene C₆₀**, **graphene** (single graphite layer; strong, conductive), and **carbon nanotubes** (sp² rings wrapped to tubes; harder than diamond; conductor or semiconductor by shape). Also carbon black, charcoal, coke.

## Carbon Oxides and Carbonates
Group 1, 2, and Zn/Cd/Hg/Pb(II) metals form ionic **carbonates** (CO₃²⁻); group 1, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba also form **hydrogen carbonates** (HCO₃⁻).

Preparation and reactions:
- Na₂O(s) + CO₂(g) → Na₂CO₃(s); Ca(OH)₂(s) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l)
- Precipitation: Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → CaCO₃(s)
- Carbonates are moderately strong bases: CO₃²⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻
- With acids: CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) (CaCO₃ is the antacid in Tums)
- Cave chemistry: CaCO₃(s) + CO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2HCO₃⁻(aq) (limestone caves, stalactites)

Sodium carbonate comes from the mineral **trona**; baking soda (NaHCO₃) leavens dough by releasing CO₂ in baking powder.

## CO₂ vs SiO₂ — A Bonding Contrast
Dry ice is discrete CO₂ molecules with C=O double bonds and weak intermolecular forces (volatile), whereas SiO₂ is a **covalent network solid** — silicon's weak π bonding (period 3) means SiO₂ uses only σ bonds in corner-sharing SiO₄ tetrahedra. Silicon and germanium (group 14 metalloids) and their semiconductor chemistry are treated in [[Metalloids]].
