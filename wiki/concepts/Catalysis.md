# Catalysis

A **catalyst** is a substance that increases a [[ReactionRate|reaction rate]] without being consumed in the reaction. **Catalysis** is the action of a catalyst, which works by providing an alternative [[ReactionMechanism|mechanism]] with a lower [[ActivationEnergy|activation energy]] for its [[RateDeterminingStep|rate-determining step]].

Key facts:
- The catalyst is regenerated, so it is not used up overall.
- It does **not** change the reaction thermodynamics: ΔH (the [[EnthalpyChemistry|enthalpy]] difference between reactants and products) is unchanged, and because the catalyst speeds the forward *and* reverse reactions equally, it does **not** shift the position of [[ChemicalEquilibrium|equilibrium]] (nor change K, per [[LeChatelierPrinciple|Le Châtelier's principle]]) — only how fast equilibrium is reached.
- On a [[TransitionState|reaction energy diagram]], the catalyzed (often multistep) path has a lower barrier than the uncatalyzed path.

## Homogeneous catalysts

Same phase as the reactants; they form an intermediate that later regenerates the catalyst.
- **NO-catalyzed ozone decomposition** (overall 2O₃ → 3O₂).
- **Cl-catalyzed ozone destruction** (Cl from CFC photolysis): Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂; ClO + O → Cl + O₂. One Cl atom destroys thousands of O₃ molecules — the discovery (Molina and Rowland, 1995 Nobel Prize in Chemistry) behind the Montreal Protocol.

## Heterogeneous catalysts

A different phase (usually a solid surface) from the reactants, acting through: (1) adsorption of reactant(s), (2) activation, (3) reaction, (4) desorption of product(s). Example: Ni-catalyzed hydrogenation C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆. Industrially essential for ammonia, nitric acid, sulfuric acid, and methanol. **Automobile catalytic converters** (Pt–Rh) simultaneously reduce NOₓ, oxidize CO, and oxidize hydrocarbons.

## Enzymes

[[Enzyme|Enzymes]] are biological (usually protein) catalysts that bind a substrate at an **active site** (lock-and-key or induced-fit), forming an enzyme–substrate complex.
