# Organic Isomerism (Structural and Geometric)

**Isomers** are compounds with the **same molecular formula but different structures**. In organic chemistry two kinds appear prominently. (Named *OrganicIsomerism* to keep this distinct from coordination-compound isomerism in [[CoordinationIsomerism]].)

## Structural (constitutional) isomers

**Structural isomers** (also **constitutional isomers**) have the same molecular formula but **different connectivity / spatial arrangement of atoms**. Classic example: **C₄H₁₀** exists as
- **n-butane** — an unbranched chain (no carbon bonded to more than two others), and
- **2-methylpropane (isobutane)** — a branched chain (center carbon bonded to three others).

The number of structural isomers grows explosively with chain length ([[Alkane|alkanes]]: butane 2, hexane 5, octane 18, decane 75, octadecane 60,523). Note that Lewis structures that *look* different may still represent the **same** molecule (e.g., three drawings of n-butane all have one unbranched four-carbon chain).

## Geometric isomers (cis / trans)

In **geometric isomers** the same atoms are attached to each other in the same order, but the **3-D geometries differ**. They arise at a **rigid [[Alkene|C=C double bond]]**: rotation about the double bond is blocked because it would break the side-by-side [[SigmaAndPiBonds|π overlap]]. So **2-butene** has
- a **cis** isomer — both methyl groups on the **same** side of the double bond, and
- a **trans** isomer — methyl groups on **opposite** sides.

Geometric isomers have **different physical properties** (boiling points, etc.) and can be separated. (For substituted [[AromaticHydrocarbon|benzene]] rings, the analogous positional isomers are ortho / meta / para.)
