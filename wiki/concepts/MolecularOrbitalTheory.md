# Molecular Orbital (MO) Theory

**Molecular orbital theory** explains chemical bonding by combining [[AtomicOrbital|atomic orbitals]] into **molecular orbitals that are delocalized over the entire molecule**, rather than localized on individual atoms as in [[ValenceBondTheory|valence bond theory]]. It succeeds where [[LewisStructure|Lewis structures]] fail — most famously, predicting that O₂ has two unpaired electrons and is therefore [[Paramagnetism|paramagnetic]]. (Named *MolecularOrbitalTheory* to avoid generic-name collisions.)

MO theory considers electrons delocalized throughout the molecule, builds bonding and antibonding interactions from orbital overlap, predicts how electrons are arranged, and handles cases Lewis structures cannot.

## Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals (LCAO)

The mathematical process of combining atomic orbitals into molecular orbitals is the **linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO)**. When atomic-orbital wave functions combine:

- **Constructive interference** (in-phase) raises electron density between the nuclei → a lower-energy **bonding** orbital.
- **Destructive interference** (out-of-phase) creates a node of zero density between the nuclei → a higher-energy **antibonding** orbital.

See [[BondingAndAntibondingOrbitals]] for the σ/σ* and π/π* orbitals this produces.

## Reading Molecules from MO Diagrams

A [[MODiagram|MO diagram]] places the orbitals of one atom on the left, the other on the right, and the molecular orbitals between them. Electrons fill by the Aufbau principle (lowest energy first), with degenerate orbitals each taking one electron before pairing (Hund's rule) and at most two opposite-spin electrons per orbital. From the resulting configuration one reads the [[BondOrder|bond order]] and whether unpaired electrons exist.

## s–p Mixing in Second-Period Diatomics

The energy ordering of the second-period molecular orbitals depends on **s–p mixing**: the σ₂ₛ wavefunction mixes with σ₂ₚ, lowering σ₂ₛ and raising σ₂ₚ. For Li–N (≤3 p electrons) the *s–p* gap is small enough that mixing pushes σ₂ₚ **above** the π₂ₚ orbitals; for O, F, Ne the larger gap makes mixing negligible, so σ₂ₚ stays **below** π₂ₚ. (Mixing reorders existing orbitals — it does not create new ones.)

## Extension: Band Theory

In solids with vast numbers of atoms, the closely spaced bonding orbitals form a **valence band** and the antibonding orbitals a **conduction band**; the **band gap** between them distinguishes conductors (tiny gap), semiconductors (moderate), and insulators (large).
