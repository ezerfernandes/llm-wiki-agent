# Ionic Compound

An **ionic compound** is a [[ChemicalCompound|compound]] that contains [[ChemicalIon|ions]] held together by **[[IonicBonding|ionic bonds]]** — the electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged cations and anions.

## Formation

When a **metal** (readily loses [[Electron|electrons]]) reacts with a **nonmetal** (readily gains electrons), electrons transfer, producing cations and anions that attract one another. The recognition rule: *a metal combined with one or more nonmetals is usually ionic* (with exceptions, e.g., AlCl₃ is not ionic). Contrast this with the electron *sharing* of [[MolecularCompound|molecular compounds]].

## Electrical Neutrality and Formulas

In every ionic compound, the total positive charge equals the total negative charge — the compound is electrically neutral overall. Formulas are determined by balancing charges:

- **Aluminum oxide**: Al³⁺ and O²⁻ → 2 Al (6+) and 3 O (6−) → **Al₂O₃**.
- **With polyatomic ions**, treat the ion as a unit and use parentheses: calcium phosphate = 3 Ca²⁺ + 2 PO₄³⁻ → **Ca₃(PO₄)₂**.
- The written formula must preserve the real [[ChemicalIon|polyatomic ion]]: sodium oxalate is **Na₂C₂O₄** (Na⁺ + C₂O₄²⁻), not the reduced "NaCO₂".

## Physical Properties

- High melting/boiling points (NaCl: melts 801 °C, boils 1413 °C).
- Not electrically conductive as solids (ions are fixed); conductive when molten or dissolved (ions move freely).
- Crystalline, rigid, and brittle. Because ionic bonds are **isotropic**, ions pack into a tightly bound three-dimensional **lattice** (each ion surrounded by oppositely charged neighbors — six in NaCl), not discrete molecular pairs. The written formula gives only the simplest charge-balanced ratio, not the physical arrangement.

## Stability and Lattice Energy

The strength of the bonding — and hence the compound's stability, melting point, and hardness — is measured by its **[[LatticeEnergy|lattice energy]]**, which grows with larger ionic charge and smaller ionic radius and is computed via the **[[BornHaberCycle|Born-Haber cycle]]**.

## Dissolution and Electrolyte Behavior

When an ionic compound dissolves in water it undergoes **dissociation** — water's [[Solvation|ion-dipole solvation]] separates the lattice into individually hydrated cations and anions (e.g., KCl(s) → K⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)). Because dissociation is essentially complete, ionic compounds are **strong [[Electrolyte|electrolytes]]** — even sparingly soluble ones, since whatever dissolves dissociates fully. Whether the compound dissolves at all depends on whether the [[Solvation|solvation]] energy outweighs the interionic forces (the [[EnthalpyOfSolution|enthalpy of solution]]). Each dissolved formula unit yields multiple particles, which is why ionic solutes amplify [[ColligativeProperties|colligative properties]] (see the [[VantHoffFactor|van 't Hoff factor]]).
