# Deductive Reasoning

A **deductive argument** is one *"whose conclusion is supposed to follow from its premises with absolute certainty, thus leaving no possibility that the conclusion doesn't follow"* ([[logic-text-v2|Van Cleave]] §1.8). Deduction is the realm where [[Validity|validity]] and [[Soundness|soundness]] apply.

## Defining features
- **Aims at certainty.** A successful deductive argument is **valid**: the truth of the premises *guarantees* the truth of the conclusion. To fail at this is to fail *as a deductive argument*.
- **Not defeasible.** Once an argument is valid, **no additional premise can overturn its validity** — contrast [[InductiveReasoning|inductive arguments]], which adding information can defeat. Example: "All healthy birds fly; Tweets is a healthy bird; ∴ Tweets flies" stays valid even if you add "Tweets is 6 ft tall and runs 30 mph" — you'd then have to imagine a world where even such birds fly.
- **Has an agreed-upon standard of evaluation** — validity — which is why logic texts spend most of their time here. (Induction has no comparably settled standard.)

## How it's evaluated
- **Informally** — the imagination/counterexample method (the [[Validity|informal test of validity]]).
- **Formally** — [[PropositionalLogic|propositional logic]] via [[TruthTable|truth tables]] and the **8 [[RulesOfInference|valid forms of inference]]** (proofs), and [[CategoricalLogic|categorical logic]] via Venn diagrams.
