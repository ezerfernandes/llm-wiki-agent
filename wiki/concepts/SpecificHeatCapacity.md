# Heat Capacity and Specific Heat

These properties relate the [[HeatThermodynamics|heat]] absorbed or released by a substance to its temperature change.

- **Heat capacity (C)** — the quantity of heat a body absorbs or releases per 1 °C (or 1 K) of temperature change:

  **C = q / ΔT**

  It is an **extensive** property (proportional to the amount of substance), measured in J/°C.

- **Specific heat capacity (c)** ("specific heat") — the heat required to raise the temperature of **1 gram** of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K):

  **c = q / (m · ΔT)**

  It is an **intensive** property (depends only on the type of substance), measured in J/g·°C.

## Calculating Heat Transfer
Rearranging the specific-heat definition gives the central working equation (no reaction or phase change):

**q = c · m · ΔT = c · m · (T_final − T_initial)**

A positive q means the substance gained heat (temperature rose); negative means it lost heat.

## Representative Values (25 °C, 1 bar)
Water has an unusually high specific heat — **4.184 J/g·°C** (liquid) — which is why water moderates temperature and is used as a heat-storage medium. Most metals are far lower: aluminum 0.897, iron 0.449, copper 0.385, gold 0.129 J/g·°C. A measured specific heat can therefore help identify an unknown metal.

## Physics View (College Physics 2e, Ch.14)
Physics writes the same relation as **Q = m·c·ΔT** and typically uses SI specific heat in **J/(kg·K)** (e.g. water 4186, aluminum 900, iron 452 J/(kg·°C)). Water's anomalously high c moderates Earth's climate and underlies its use as a coolant/heat-storage medium. ΔT is numerically identical in kelvin and Celsius. This `Q = mcΔT` is the per-body term used in every [[Calorimetry]] balance and complements the energy of phase change captured by [[LatentHeat]].
