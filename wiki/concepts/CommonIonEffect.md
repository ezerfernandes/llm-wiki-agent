# Common-Ion Effect

The **common-ion effect** is the decrease in solubility of a slightly soluble ionic compound when the solution already contains a **common ion** — one also produced by the compound's dissolution. It is [[LeChatelierPrinciple|Le Châtelier's principle]] applied to the dissolution equilibrium: adding more of a product ion shifts the equilibrium back toward the undissolved solid.

Because the [[SolubilityProduct|solubility product]] [ion₁][ion₂] = Ksp is a fixed constant at a given temperature, raising one ion's concentration forces the other ion's equilibrium concentration (and hence the [[MolarSolubility|molar solubility]]) down proportionally.

## Worked example (CdS)

Dissolving CdS (Ksp = 1.0 × 10⁻²⁸) in 0.010 M CdBr₂ — which already supplies 0.010 M Cd²⁺:

$$\mathrm{CdS(s) \rightleftharpoons Cd^{2+}(aq) + S^{2-}(aq)} \qquad (0.010 + x)(x) = 1.0 \times 10^{-28}$$

With x ≪ 0.010, (0.010)(x) = 1.0 × 10⁻²⁸ ⇒ x = 1.0 × 10⁻²⁶ M. The common Cd²⁺ makes CdS far less soluble than it is in pure water.

(Note: the *same* logic in reverse — removing a product ion via acid or complex formation — *raises* solubility; that is the subject of [[CoupledEquilibria|coupled equilibria]].)
