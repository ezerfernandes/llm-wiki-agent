# Critical Point and Supercritical Fluids

The **critical temperature** is the temperature above which a gas cannot be liquefied no matter how much pressure is applied. The **critical pressure** is the pressure required to liquefy a substance at its critical temperature. Together they define the **critical point**, where the [[PhaseDiagramChemistry|liquid-gas boundary curve]] terminates — for water, 374 °C and ~22,000 kPa (~218 atm).

Above the critical point the distinction between liquid and gas disappears: the substance becomes a **supercritical fluid** whose properties are intermediate between the two.

Selected critical constants:

| Substance | Critical T (°C) | Critical P (kPa) |
|-----------|-----------------|------------------|
| Hydrogen | −240.0 | 1300 |
| Nitrogen | −147.2 | 3400 |
| Oxygen | −118.9 | 5000 |
| Carbon dioxide | 31.1 | 7400 |
| Ammonia | 132.4 | 11,300 |
| Water | 374.0 | 22,000 |

Because ammonia's critical temperature (405.5 K) is above room temperature it can be liquefied by compression; oxygen cannot (critical T below room temperature). A CO₂ fire extinguisher contains sloshing liquid at 18 °C but none at 35 °C — above CO₂'s 31 °C critical temperature.

## Supercritical Fluids

A supercritical fluid expands to fill its container like a gas, yet has near-liquid density and can dissolve nonvolatile solutes; it has essentially no [[LiquidSurfaceTension|surface tension]] and very low [[LiquidViscosity|viscosity]], so it penetrates tiny openings. **Supercritical CO₂** (above 304.2 K, 7376 kPa) decaffeinates coffee (removing 97–99% of caffeine while preserving flavor/aroma), removes fat from potato chips, and extracts citrus flavor/fragrance — nontoxic, cheap, and recoverable by simply releasing the pressure.
