# Hydrogen Bonding

**Hydrogen bonding** is "a particularly strong type of dipole-dipole attraction," the strongest of the [[IntermolecularForces|van der Waals forces]]. It occurs only when a hydrogen atom is bonded to **fluorine, oxygen, or nitrogen** — the three most [[Electronegativity|electronegative]] elements. The requirement is the presence of an **F–H, O–H, or N–H** moiety; the hydrogen of one molecule is then attracted to a lone pair on the F/O/N of another (e.g., H₂O⋯HOH).

The large electronegativity gap (H ≈ 2.1; F 4.0, O 3.5, N 3.0) plus the small sizes of H and of F/O/N concentrate the partial charges, producing the strong attraction.

## Not a Covalent Bond

"Despite use of the word 'bond,' keep in mind that hydrogen bonds are intermolecular attractive forces, not intramolecular attractive forces (covalent bonds)." They are "much weaker than covalent bonds, only about 5 to 10% as strong, but are generally much stronger than other dipole-dipole attractions and dispersion forces."

## Effects on Physical Properties

Hydrogen bonding makes the period-2 binary hydrides boil anomalously high relative to their heavier congeners:

- Group 17: **HF 25 °C** (vs HCl −80, HBr −60, HI −40 °C)
- Group 16: **H₂O 100 °C** (vs H₂S −55, H₂Se −45, H₂Te −3 °C)
- Group 15: **NH₃ −40 °C** (vs PH₃ −80, AsH₃ −55, SbH₃ −10 °C)

Without hydrogen bonding, water would boil near −80 °C, HF near −110 °C, NH₃ near −120 °C. The contrast of dimethylether (−24.8 °C, dipole-dipole) vs ethanol (78.4 °C, –OH hydrogen bonding) at equal molar mass shows the same effect.

## Hydrogen Bonding in DNA

In DNA the complementary base pairs are held by hydrogen bonds — adenine–thymine by 2 and guanine–cytosine by 3. Millions of these bonds hold the double helix together, yet because each is weak the strands "unzip" easily during replication.
