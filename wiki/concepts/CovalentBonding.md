# Covalent Bonding

**Covalent bonding** is a type of [[ChemicalBond|chemical bond]] that results from the mutual attraction of two atoms for a *shared* pair of electrons. It forms between atoms with similar tendencies to attract electrons — similar [[IonizationEnergy|ionization energies]] and [[ElectronAffinity|electron affinities]] — typically two nonmetals. This contrasts with [[IonicBonding|ionic bonding]], which transfers electrons rather than sharing them. (Named *CovalentBonding* to avoid generic-name collisions.)

## Formation and Bond Length

As two atoms approach (e.g., two H atoms with overlapping 1s orbitals), each shared electron is attracted to *both* nuclei, lowering the system's potential energy. If the atoms get too close, the nuclei repel and energy rises. The **bond length** is the internuclear distance at the potential-energy minimum — for H₂, 74 pm with energy −7.24 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Forming the bond releases energy (H₂: 2H → H₂, ΔH = −436 kJ); breaking it requires the same energy ([[BondEnergy|bond energy]]).

## Pure vs. Polar Covalent Bonds

- **Pure (nonpolar) covalent bond**: identical atoms (H₂, Cl₂) share electrons equally.
- **[[BondPolarity|Polar covalent bond]]**: different atoms share unequally; electron density shifts toward the more [[Electronegativity|electronegative]] atom, giving partial charges (δ+ / δ−).

## Physical Properties of Covalent Compounds

Because neutral molecules attract one another more weakly than charged ions do, [[MolecularCompound|covalent compounds]] generally have much lower melting/boiling points than ionic compounds, are often liquids or gases at room temperature, are softer as solids, are usually insoluble in water, and are poor electrical conductors in any state.

This describes **molecular solids** (held together by weak [[IntermolecularForces|intermolecular forces]]). A separate class, **covalent network solids** (diamond, SiO₂, SiC, Si), uses covalent bonds *throughout the entire crystal* rather than within discrete molecules, making them extremely hard with very high melting points (diamond >3500 °C). See [[CrystallineSolid]] for the four solid types.

## Quantum-Mechanical Models

Lewis structures and [[VSEPRTheory|VSEPR]] describe *that* a covalent bond exists and the molecule's shape, but two deeper models explain *why*. [[ValenceBondTheory|Valence bond theory]] treats a bond as the overlap of half-filled [[AtomicOrbital|atomic orbitals]] (giving [[SigmaAndPiBonds|σ and π bonds]] and requiring [[OrbitalHybridization|hybridization]] to match geometry); [[MolecularOrbitalTheory|molecular orbital theory]] combines atomic orbitals into delocalized bonding/antibonding orbitals and yields a [[BondOrder|bond order]]. The energy-vs-distance picture above (the bond length at the potential-energy minimum) is exactly the valence bond account of bond formation.

## Mixed Bonding

A single compound can contain both bond types: polyatomic ions (OH⁻, NO₃⁻, NH₄⁺) are held internally by polar covalent bonds, yet combine with oppositely charged ions through ionic bonds (KNO₃: ionic between K⁺ and NO₃⁻, covalent within NO₃⁻).
