## Definition
A **particle accelerator** uses electric fields to give charged particles very high kinetic energy and magnetic fields to steer them. High-energy collisions then **create matter from energy** (m = ΔE/c²), making accelerators the primary discovery tool of [[ParticlePhysics|particle physics]].

## Key Points
- **Cyclotron** (1930): a fixed-frequency alternating field accelerates particles in expanding circular orbits within a magnetic field.
- **Synchrotron**: voltage frequency and magnetic field rise together so the orbital radius stays constant while energy climbs to TeV scales; it emits **synchrotron radiation** (an energy loss, but also a useful intense radiation source).
- **Linear accelerator (linac)**: a straight series of accelerating tubes; avoids synchrotron-radiation losses (SLAC: 3.2 km, ~50 GeV). A proton gains PE = qV per gap.
- **Colliders / colliding beams**: head-on collisions of opposing beams (often particle–[[Antimatter|antiparticle]] pairs in the same ring) put nearly all energy into making massive particles at rest, rather than wasting it on recoil momentum. Examples: LHC at CERN (7 TeV per beam), Tevatron at Fermilab (proton–antiproton).
- **[[ParticleDetector|Detectors]]** record the resulting particle "spray," measuring mass and charge to identify what was produced.

## Equations
- m = ΔE/c² (matter created from collision energy)
- PE = qV (energy gained per accelerating gap; 1 eV = (1 V)(1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C))
