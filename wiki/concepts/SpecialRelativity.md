## Definition
Special relativity is [[AlbertEinstein|Einstein's]] theory of how space, time, and motion are measured by observers moving at constant velocity relative to one another (inertial observers, no gravity or acceleration). Built from [[EinsteinPostulates|two postulates]], it predicts that time intervals, lengths, simultaneity, momentum, and energy all depend on the observer's [[ReferenceFrame|reference frame]].

## Key Points
- "Special" because it is restricted to inertial frames; accelerating frames and gravity require general relativity.
- The invariance of the [[SpeedOfLightPhysics|speed of light]] c forces space and time to be observer-dependent.
- Every relativistic effect is scaled by the [[LorentzFactor|Lorentz factor]] γ, which is ≈ 1 at everyday speeds and diverges as v → c.
- Classical (Newtonian) mechanics is recovered in the low-speed limit (v ≪ c), so relativity extends rather than overturns it.
- Consequences include [[TimeDilation|time dilation]], [[LengthContraction|length contraction]], [[RelativisticVelocityAddition|relativistic velocity addition]], [[RelativisticMomentum|relativistic momentum]], and [[MassEnergyEquivalence|mass-energy equivalence]] (E = mc²).
- No massive object can reach c, since its energy and momentum would become infinite.

## Equations
- γ = 1 / √(1 − v²/c²)
- E = γ mc² ;  E₀ = mc² ;  E² = (pc)² + (mc²)²
