# Evolution of Atomic Theory

The **evolution of atomic theory** is the sequence of experiments that revealed the internal [[AtomicStructure|structure]] of the [[Atom]], overturning [[AtomicTheory|Dalton's]] assumption that atoms are indivisible.

## Thomson — the Electron (late 1800s)

[[JJThomson]] studied cathode rays in low-pressure gas tubes. The beam deflected toward positive charges and behaved identically regardless of electrode metal. Using simultaneous electric and magnetic deflection he measured the **charge-to-mass ratio** of the beam's particles, concluding they were fundamental, negatively charged constituents of all atoms — the [[Electron|electron]] (mass >1000× smaller than an atom). The term "electron" was coined in 1891 by George Stoney.

## Millikan — the Electron's Charge (1909)

[[RobertMillikan|Robert Millikan's]] oil-drop experiment found droplet charges always to be multiples of **1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C**, the fundamental charge of one electron. Combined with Thomson's charge-to-mass ratio (1.759 × 10¹¹ C/kg), this gave the electron mass: **9.107 × 10⁻³¹ kg**.

## Models Before Rutherford

- **Plum pudding model** (Thomson, 1904): electrons embedded in a positive mass.
- **Saturn model** (Hantaro Nagaoka, 1903): a positive sphere ringed by orbiting electrons.

## Rutherford — the Nucleus (gold-foil experiment)

[[ErnestRutherford]], with Geiger and Marsden, fired positively charged α particles (2 [[Proton|protons]] + 2 [[Neutron|neutrons]]) at thin gold foil. Most passed straight through, but a few deflected sharply backward — "as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you." Conclusions: the atom is mostly empty space, with a tiny, dense, positively charged [[AtomicNucleus|nucleus]] at its center. Rutherford named the [[Proton|proton]] as the nuclear building block.

## Soddy — Isotopes

Frederick Soddy recognized that chemically identical atoms could have different masses — [[Isotope|isotopes]] — earning the 1921 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

## Chadwick — the Neutron (1932)

[[JamesChadwick]] discovered the [[Neutron|neutron]] (uncharged, mass ≈ proton), accounting for the nuclear mass that protons alone could not, and explaining why isotopes differ in mass but not chemistry.
