## Definition
A black hole is a region of spacetime so curved by concentrated mass that nothing, not even light, can escape from within its boundary. That boundary, the **event horizon**, lies at the Schwarzschild radius, where the escape velocity equals the speed of light.

## Key Points
- Predicted by [[GeneralRelativity]]; black holes are extremely compact because G is small and c² is enormous.
- The **event horizon** marks the point of no return; the **Schwarzschild radius** R_S sets its size.
- **Neutron stars** are a related endpoint of stellar collapse, slightly larger than a black hole of comparable mass.
- Indirect evidence: X-ray emission from binary systems, companion-mass measurements, superheated **accretion disks** spiraling inward, and relativistic jets. **Quasars** — extremely luminous young galaxy cores — are likely powered by supermassive black hole accretion.
- **Hawking radiation**: quantum pair production near the horizon lets one particle escape while its partner falls in, draining the hole's mass-energy; the process accelerates as the hole shrinks, ending in a burst of particles and gamma rays.
- Black-hole mergers were confirmed by LIGO's 2015 [[GravitationalWave]] detection.

## Equations
- Schwarzschild radius: R_S = 2GM / c²
