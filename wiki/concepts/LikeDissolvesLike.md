# Like Dissolves Like

**"Like dissolves like"** is the empirical rule that polar solutes dissolve well in polar solvents and nonpolar solutes dissolve well in nonpolar solvents. It follows from the [[DissolutionProcess|dissolution process]]: a solution forms readily when solute-solvent attractions are at least as strong as the solute-solute and solvent-solvent attractions that must be overcome.

## Application to Liquid Miscibility
- **Miscible** liquids mix in any proportion (infinite mutual solubility) and typically share polarity — e.g., ethanol, sulfuric acid, and ethylene glycol with water; two-cycle motor oil with gasoline.
- **Immiscible** liquids form separate layers — e.g., gasoline, oil, benzene, or carbon tetrachloride with water; the weak attractions between polar water and nonpolar liquids cannot overcome water's strong [[HydrogenBonding|hydrogen bonding]].
- **Partially miscible** liquids show moderate mutual solubility and form two saturated layers — e.g., bromine and water. Miscibility vs immiscibility is a matter of degree, not absolute.

## Application to Solids and Gases
The same polarity matching governs whether solids dissolve and how strongly gases dissolve (gas solubility depends only on solute-solvent forces, since gases have no solute-solute interactions). It depends fundamentally on [[MolecularPolarity|molecular polarity]] and the [[IntermolecularForces|intermolecular forces]] available.
