## Definition
The **law of conservation of energy** states that energy can change form or be transferred between systems but is never created or destroyed; the total energy of an isolated system stays constant.

## Key Points
- Cited in College Physics 2e Ch. 1 as a prime example of a unifying [[ScientificModelsTheoriesLaws|physical law]] — one statement that ties together topics as varied as the calories in food and the charge in a battery.
- Illustrates the chapter's theme that a few simple laws explain a vast range of phenomena.
- A concrete instance of how [[Physics]] reveals an underlying order in nature.
- Ch. 7 develops the quantitative form: when only [[ConservativeForce|conservative forces]] act, [[MechanicalEnergy]] (KE + PE) is conserved; a [[NonconservativeForce]] such as friction changes mechanical energy by exactly the nonconservative work W_nc.
- The general statement adds "other energy" (OE) — thermal, chemical, electrical, nuclear, radiant — so the total over all forms is constant.
- Conservation does not mean energy stays useful: most transformations degrade energy into waste heat ([[EnergyEfficiency]]), motivating energy conservation of [[EnergyResources]].

## Equations
- Conceptual form: total energy = constant (E_total stays fixed in an isolated system).
- General: KE_i + PE_i + W_nc + OE_i = KE_f + PE_f + OE_f
- Mechanical only (conservative forces): KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f
