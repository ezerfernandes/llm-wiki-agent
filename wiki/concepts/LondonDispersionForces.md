# London Dispersion Forces

**London dispersion forces** are the weakest of the [[IntermolecularForces|van der Waals forces]], but they are present in *all* condensed phases regardless of composition. Named for physicist Fritz London, who first explained them in 1928.

## Mechanism: Instantaneous and Induced Dipoles

Because electrons are in constant motion, the electron cloud of any atom or molecule can momentarily become asymmetric, producing an **instantaneous dipole**. This temporary dipole distorts the electron cloud of a neighbor, producing an **induced dipole**. The fleeting, correlated dipoles attract weakly — the dispersion force.

## Polarizability and Strength

"The measure of how easy or difficult it is for another electrostatic charge ... to distort a molecule's charge distribution (its electron cloud) is known as **polarizability**." Larger, heavier atoms/molecules have valence electrons farther from the nucleus and more loosely held, so they are more polarizable and have **stronger** dispersion forces — giving higher melting and boiling points.

- Halogens: F₂ (bp 85 K) < Cl₂ (238 K) < Br₂ (332 K) < I₂ (457 K) < At₂ (610 K), tracking molar mass and atomic radius.
- Group-14 hydrides: CH₄ < SiH₄ < GeH₄ < SnH₄ in boiling point.

## Molecular Shape

For equal molar mass, more elongated molecules have more surface contact and stronger dispersion forces. The C₅H₁₂ isomers: n-pentane (36 °C) > isopentane (27 °C) > the compact neopentane (9.5 °C). "The elongated shape of n-pentane provides a greater surface area available for contact between molecules, resulting in correspondingly stronger dispersion forces."
