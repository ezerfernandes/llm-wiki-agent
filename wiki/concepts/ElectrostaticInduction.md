## Definition
Electrostatic induction is the rearrangement of charge in a material caused by a nearby [[ElectricCharge|charge]], without direct contact. When the redistribution of free charges in a conductor stops (net force on them is zero), the conductor is in **electrostatic equilibrium**.

## Key Points
- **Charging by induction:** a nearby charge polarizes a neutral [[ConductorsAndInsulators|conductor]]; separating it (or grounding then disconnecting it) leaves a net charge opposite to the inducer. The inducer loses no charge — consistent with [[ConservationOfCharge]].
- Three properties of a conductor in static equilibrium:
  1. The [[ElectricField]] is zero inside the conductor.
  2. The field just outside is perpendicular to the surface (any parallel component would keep moving charge).
  3. All excess charge sits on the outer surface; far away the external field equals that of a point charge q at the center.
- **Charge concentrates at sharp points** (high curvature) because repulsion spreads charge more effectively on flat regions — the basis of lightning rods.
- A **Faraday cage** (or metal car body) keeps the field zero inside and excess charge on the outside, shielding the interior.
- Earth's fair-weather field is ≈ 150 N/C downward (positive ionosphere, negative surface); air ionizes when the field exceeds ~3 × 10⁶ N/C.

## Equations
- External field of a charged conductor (far field) ≈ k |q| / r²
