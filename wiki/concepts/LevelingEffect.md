# Leveling Effect

The **leveling effect** is the limit a solvent places on the maximum acid or base strength that can be observed in it. In aqueous solution, the strongest acid that can exist is the **hydronium ion, H₃O⁺(aq)**: any acid intrinsically stronger than H₃O⁺ reacts completely with water to generate H₃O⁺, so all such acids appear equally strong — they are "leveled" to the strength of H₃O⁺.

$$\text{any strong acid } \mathrm{HA} + \mathrm{H_2O} \longrightarrow \mathrm{H_3O^+} + \mathrm{A^-}\quad(\text{complete})$$

Symmetrically, the strongest base that can exist in water is the **hydroxide ion, OH⁻(aq)**.

## Consequence

Because HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, HClO₄, and H₂SO₄ (first proton) are all leveled to H₃O⁺ in water, their relative strengths **cannot be distinguished** in aqueous solution — their effective [[AcidIonizationConstant|Ka]] is just that of H₃O⁺. To rank such strong acids one must use a **less basic solvent** than water (e.g. glacial acetic acid), in which they ionize only partially and reveal their differences.
