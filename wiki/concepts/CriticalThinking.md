# Critical Thinking

**Critical thinking** is the practiced skill of **analyzing and assessing arguments** — identifying what is being claimed, on what grounds, and whether those grounds actually support the claim. [[logic-text-v2|Van Cleave]] frames the whole textbook around it and insists it is a **skill, not a body of knowledge**: like learning a language, it is acquired only through practice, not by reading about it.

## The two halves
The book splits the work into two complementary methods, mirroring the deductive/inductive split:

1. **Reconstruction** — put an argument into **standard form** ([[Argument]]): make its premises and conclusion explicit, supply missing premises, and paraphrase charitably (the **principle of charity** — interpret an argument in its strongest plausible form).
2. **Evaluation** — judge the reconstructed argument. For **deductive** arguments this means testing [[Validity|validity]] (informally in Ch 1; formally via [[TruthTable|truth tables]], [[RulesOfInference|proofs]], and [[CategoricalLogic|Venn diagrams]] in Ch 2) and then [[Soundness|soundness]]. For **inductive** arguments it means assessing strength ([[InductiveReasoning]], [[InferenceToBestExplanation]]) and watching for [[LogicalFallacy|fallacies]].

## Why it matters
The book argues these skills are decisive in a world where information arrives "couched within probabilistic and statistical frameworks" — so it devotes Chapter 3 to **probabilistic literacy** and the [[ConjunctionFallacy|conjunction]] / [[BaseRateFallacy|base-rate]] fallacies, and Chapter 4 to a catalog of [[LogicalFallacy|formal and informal fallacies]], deliberately explaining *why each is seductive* (a debt to [[DanielKahneman|Kahneman]]).
