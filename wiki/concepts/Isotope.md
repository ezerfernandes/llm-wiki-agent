# Isotope

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same [[ChemicalElement|element]] that have the same number of [[Proton|protons]] ([[AtomicNumberAndMassNumber|atomic number Z]]) but **different numbers of [[Neutron|neutrons]]**, and therefore different mass numbers A. Because chemistry is governed by electrons/protons, isotopes are chemically nearly indistinguishable; they differ in mass.

## Discovery

Frederick Soddy recognized isotopes in the early 1900s when "mesothorium" (from thorium decay) proved chemically identical to radium despite a different atomic mass; he won the 1921 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The existence of [[Neutron|neutrons]] (discovered by [[JamesChadwick]] in 1932) explained how isotopes can differ in mass while remaining the same element (see [[EvolutionOfAtomicTheory]]).

## Examples

- Magnesium: ²⁴Mg (12 n), ²⁵Mg (13 n), ²⁶Mg (14 n).
- Hydrogen: ¹H (protium), ²H = **deuterium** (D), ³H = **tritium** (T).

## Atomic Mass

Because most elements occur as natural mixtures of isotopes, the tabulated [[AtomicMass|atomic mass]] is a fractional-abundance-weighted average over the isotopes.

## Nuclear Stability and Radioisotopes (College Physics 2e Ch.31)
In nuclear physics a specific isotope is a **nuclide** ($^{A}_{Z}X_{N}$); the neutron-to-proton ratio determines whether it is stable or radioactive. Unstable isotopes (radioisotopes) undergo [[RadioactiveDecay|radioactive decay]] ([[AlphaDecay|alpha]], [[BetaDecay|beta]], or [[GammaDecay|gamma]]), each characterized by a [[HalfLife|half-life]]. Long-lived radioisotopes such as ¹⁴C and ²³⁸U enable [[RadiometricDating|radiometric dating]].

## Nuclear Chemistry View (Chemistry 2e Ch.21)
Unstable isotopes — **[[Radioisotope|radioisotopes]]** — sit off the [[BandOfStability|band of stability]] and undergo [[RadioactiveDecayChemistry|radioactive decay]] with characteristic [[RadioactiveHalfLife|half-lives]]. They are exploited as medical tracers and clocks (e.g. C-14, U-238); see [[NuclearChemistry]].
