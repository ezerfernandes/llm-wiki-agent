# Inductive Reasoning

An **inductive argument** is one *"whose conclusion is supposed to follow from its premises with a high level of probability"* — possible for the conclusion to be false even when the premises are true, but unlikely ([[logic-text-v2|Van Cleave]] §1.8).

## Defining features
- **Aims at probability, not certainty.** Inductive support comes in degrees: **strong** inductive arguments give a good (if not conclusive) reason to accept the conclusion; **weak** ones do not.
- **Defeasible.** This is the key contrast with [[DeductiveReasoning|deduction]]: adding further premises can **defeat** an otherwise strong inductive inference.
  ```
  1. Tweets is a healthy, normally functioning bird
  2. Most healthy, normally functioning birds fly
  3. Therefore, Tweets probably flies        ← strong, but…
  + Tweets is 6 ft tall and can run 30 mph   ← defeats it (Tweets is an ostrich)
  ```
  A valid deductive argument cannot be defeated this way.
- **No single agreed-upon standard of evaluation** — assessing inductive strength "is an area of ongoing research in philosophy," which is why logic texts spend less time on it.

## Forms of inductive argument (Ch 3)
- **Statistical generalization** — from a sample to a population (watch sample size and bias).
- **[[InferenceToBestExplanation|Inference to the best explanation]]** — adopt the hypothesis that best explains the observed facts (graded by seven explanatory virtues; a kind of [[Abduction]]).
- **Analogical arguments** — infer a shared further property from relevant similarities.
- **Causal arguments** — establish causes via [[NecessaryAndSufficientConditions|necessary- and sufficient-condition tests]].
- **Probabilistic reasoning** ([[Probability]]) — and its characteristic failures, the [[ConjunctionFallacy|conjunction]], [[BaseRateFallacy|base-rate]], small-numbers, regression, and gambler's fallacies.
