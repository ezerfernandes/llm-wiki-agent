# Ideal Solution

An **ideal solution** is one that forms "with no accompanying energy change" because "the strengths of the intermolecular forces of attraction between solute and solvent species in a solution are no different than those present in the separated components." Its [[EnthalpyOfSolution|enthalpy of solution]] is essentially zero, so it forms purely on the strength of increased matter dispersal (entropy).

## Examples
- **Ideal gases mixing** — helium and argon experience no significant intermolecular attractions; when their containers are connected they mix spontaneously by diffusion, each gas then occupying twice its original volume (increased dispersal).
- **Structurally similar liquids** — methanol (CH₃OH) + ethanol (C₂H₅OH); pentane (C₅H₁₂) + hexane (C₆H₁₄). The attractive forces between like and unlike molecules are essentially the same, so mixing causes no significant energy change.

These cases demonstrate that **increased matter dispersal alone** can drive spontaneous solution formation, even without any exothermic enthalpy.

## Relation to Raoult's Law
Ideal solutions obey [[RaoultsLaw|Raoult's law]] exactly: each component's vapor pressure is its pure vapor pressure scaled by its mole fraction. Deviations from ideality reflect unequal solute-solute, solvent-solvent, and solute-solvent forces.
