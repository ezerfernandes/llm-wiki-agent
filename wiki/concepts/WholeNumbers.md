# Whole Numbers

The **whole numbers** are the everyday numbers used for counting things, together with zero: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, … and on forever. They are built from the **counting numbers** (also called the **natural numbers**), which start at 1: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, …. The only difference between the two sets is that the whole numbers also include **0**. The ellipsis (…) means the list keeps going without end — there is no largest whole number.

Whole numbers can be pictured on a **number line**: a straight line with the numbers marked in order from left to right, smaller numbers on the left and larger on the right. The point labeled 0 is the **origin**. When a number is matched up with a point on the line, that number is called the **coordinate** of the point. The number line makes it easy to see order ("which is bigger") and is the first model students use before working with fractions and negative numbers later on.

Every whole number is written with **digits** (the symbols 0 through 9) arranged according to [[PlaceValue|place value]], the rule that a digit's worth depends on where it sits. Whole numbers are the starting point for all of arithmetic: see [[WholeNumberArithmetic]] for adding, subtracting, multiplying, and dividing them, [[Rounding]] for approximating them, and [[Divisibility]] for telling when one divides another evenly. The decimal whole-number system is the base-10 case of the more general [[NumberBase|positional number system]].
