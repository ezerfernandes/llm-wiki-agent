## Definition
Maxwell's equations are four mathematical statements (formulated by [[JamesClerkMaxwell]] in the mid-19th century) that unify all classical electric and magnetic phenomena into a single theory of the electromagnetic force. Their combined consequence is the prediction of self-propagating [[ElectromagneticWave|electromagnetic waves]] traveling at the speed of light — establishing that light is an electromagnetic wave and unifying electricity, magnetism, and optics.

## Key Points
- **Gauss's law for electricity** — electric field lines begin on positive charges and end on negative charges; field strength relates to the electric constant ε₀ (permittivity of free space).
- **Gauss's law for magnetism** — magnetic field lines are continuous closed loops; there are no magnetic monopoles.
- **Faraday's law** (see [[FaradaysLaw]]) — a changing magnetic field induces an electric field opposing the change.
- **Ampère–Maxwell law** (extends [[AmperesLaw]]) — currents *and changing electric fields* produce magnetic fields. Maxwell's crucial addition was that a changing [[ElectricField]] alone creates a [[MagneticField]].
- This added symmetry (changing E ↔ changing B) lets the two fields continually regenerate each other and propagate as a wave.
- Verified by [[HeinrichHertz]] (1887), who generated and detected EM waves at the predicted speed.

## Equations
- Predicted wave speed: `c = 1 / sqrt(μ₀ ε₀)`
- Numerical: `c = 1/sqrt[(8.85×10⁻¹² C²/N·m²)(4π×10⁻⁷ T·m/A)] = 3.00×10⁸ m/s`
- Constants: ε₀ = 8.85×10⁻¹² C²/N·m²; μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ T·m/A
