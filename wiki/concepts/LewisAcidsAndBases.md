# Lewis Acids and Bases

The **Lewis definition** of acids and bases (proposed by [[GilbertLewis|G. N. Lewis]] in 1923) is the most general of the acid–base models. It identifies acids and bases by their ability to **accept or donate a pair of electrons** and form a [[CoordinateCovalentBond|coordinate covalent bond]]:

- A **[[LewisAcid|Lewis acid]]** is an electron-pair **acceptor**.
- A **[[LewisBase|Lewis base]]** is an electron-pair **donor**.
- The product is an **acid–base adduct**, joined by the new coordinate covalent bond.

The Lewis lone-pair/bonding-pair picture is the same one drawn in a [[LewisStructure|Lewis structure]], extended to reactivity: a species with an available lone pair can act as a base; a species with an empty (or expandable) valence orbital can act as an acid.

## Relationship to Brønsted-Lowry

[[BronstedLowryAcidsAndBases|Brønsted-Lowry]] reactions are a **subcategory** of Lewis reactions — those in which the Lewis acid is the proton H⁺. For example, H₂O (Lewis base) donates a lone pair to H⁺ (Lewis acid) to form H₃O⁺; NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺. The Lewis model is broader because it covers reactions with **no proton transfer**.

## Examples beyond proton transfer

- **BF₃ + F⁻ → [BF₄]⁻** — BF₃ is a strong Lewis acid (boron has only six valence electrons); fluoride donates a lone pair.
- **Ag⁺ + 2 NH₃ → [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺** — a [[ComplexIon|complex ion]]: the metal cation is the Lewis acid, ammonia molecules the Lewis bases.
- **SO₂ + O²⁻ → [SO₃]²⁻** — nonmetal oxides act as Lewis acids toward oxide ions to form oxyanions.
- **Displacement** — one Lewis base can displace another from an adduct (and likewise for Lewis acids).

Complex-ion formation, the most important application here, is governed by the [[FormationConstant|formation constant Kf]].
