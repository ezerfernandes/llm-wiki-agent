## Definition
Electromotive force (emf) is the potential difference a voltage source produces when it delivers **zero current** — its "open-circuit" voltage. Measured in volts. Despite the name, emf is not a force; it is the energy per unit charge the source can supply. The voltage actually measured at the terminals while current flows is the **terminal voltage**, which is lower because of the source's [[InternalResistance]].

## Key Points
- A real source models as an ideal emf in series with internal resistance r.
- Terminal voltage equals emf only at zero current; under load it falls.
- Heavy loads (small load resistance) and aging batteries (rising r) cause large terminal-voltage drops — a weak battery dims a flashlight.
- Series sources: emfs add (and so do internal resistances).
- Parallel identical sources: emf unchanged but combined internal resistance falls, raising current capacity.
- Electric eels and solar arrays stack cells in series/parallel to reach target voltage and current.

## Equations
- Terminal voltage: `V = emf − I r`
- Loaded current: `I = emf / (R_load + r)`
- Power delivered/dissipated: `P = I² R`
