# Inelastic Collision

## Definition
An inelastic collision is a [[Collision]] that does not conserve internal kinetic energy. Momentum is still conserved, but kinetic energy is transformed — typically lost to heat and sound, or in some cases added from stored energy (e.g. a compressed spring). A **perfectly inelastic collision** is the extreme case where the bodies stick together, giving the maximum kinetic-energy loss still allowed by momentum conservation.

## Key Points
- Momentum conservation always holds (negligible external force); kinetic energy does not.
- When a moving body strikes a stationary one and they stick, the combined final speed is v' = m₁v₁/(m₁+m₂).
- Most everyday collisions are inelastic: a goalie catching a puck converts most of the kinetic energy to heat and sound.
- Not all inelastic collisions lose motion energy — spring-loaded carts can gain kinetic energy from released potential energy.
- The **coefficient of restitution** c = √(h/H) (bounce height over drop height) quantifies how inelastic a bounce is.
- Applications: sports equipment design (heavier bats/racquets transfer more momentum), "sweet spot" physics, and injury reduction.

## Equations
- Momentum: m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v'₁ + m₂v'₂
- Perfectly inelastic (target at rest): v' = m₁v₁ / (m₁ + m₂)
- KE = ½mv²
- Coefficient of restitution: c = √(h/H)
