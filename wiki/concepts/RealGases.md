# Real Gases (Non-Ideal Behavior)

Real gases deviate from the [[IdealGasLawChemistry|ideal gas law]] PV = nRT: their observed P–V–T relationships are not exactly described by the simple gas laws. The deviations arise because two postulates of the [[KineticMolecularTheory|kinetic-molecular theory]] fail for real gases — molecules **do** occupy volume, and they **do** exert intermolecular attractions.

## Compressibility Factor (Z)

The **compressibility factor** quantifies deviation from ideality:

$$Z = \frac{\text{measured molar volume}}{\text{ideal molar volume}} = \frac{PV_m}{RT}$$

- **Z = 1** → ideal behavior.
- **Z ≠ 1** → non-ideal behavior.

In compressibility plots, Z first dips below 1 (attractive forces dominate, making the gas more compressible than ideal) at low-to-moderate pressure, then rises above 1 at very high pressure (finite molecular volume dominates, making the gas less compressible).

## Physical Causes

1. **Finite molecular volume** — at high pressure, molecules are crowded and their own volume becomes a significant fraction of the total, so the gas is less compressible than [[BoylesLaw|Boyle's law]] predicts.
2. **Intermolecular attractions** — at higher pressure, attractive forces pull molecules together, lowering the pressure (at constant V) or volume (at constant P). The effect is stronger at **low temperature**, where molecules have less kinetic energy to overcome the attractions.

## When Gases Behave Ideally

Ideal behavior is approached at **low pressure, high temperature**, and low molecular density (few molecules in a large volume). Deviations are largest at **high pressure and low temperature**. The corrections are made quantitative by the [[VanDerWaalsEquation|van der Waals equation]].
