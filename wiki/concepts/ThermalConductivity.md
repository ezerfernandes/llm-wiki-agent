## Definition
**Thermal conductivity (k)** is a material property that quantifies how readily heat propagates through
a substance by [[HeatConduction|conduction]]. It is the proportionality constant in the conduction-rate
law, with SI units W/(m·K).

## Key Points
- High-k materials (metals: copper, silver, aluminum) transfer heat quickly; low-k materials (wood,
  rubber, plastic, foams, and especially trapped air) act as insulators. Values span several orders of
  magnitude.
- A large temperature gradient can be sustained across a low-k material; a high-k material reaches near-
  uniform temperature quickly (why an aluminum pan transfers stove heat with little temperature drop).
- The **R-value** of an insulating layer is R = d/k — combining a material's conductivity with its
  thickness; thicker, lower-k layers give larger R and better insulation.
- Many practical insulators work by trapping still air (low k) and suppressing [[Convection|convection]]
  within it — e.g. down, wool, fiberglass batts, foam.

## Equations
- Q/t = kA(T2 − T1)/d   (k appears as the conduction-rate constant)
- R = d/k   (R-value)
