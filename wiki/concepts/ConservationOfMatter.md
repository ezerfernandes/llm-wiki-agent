# Conservation of Matter

The **law of conservation of matter** states that there is no detectable change in the total quantity of [[Matter]] present when matter converts from one type to another (a [[ChemicalProperty|chemical change]]) or changes among solid, liquid, or gaseous [[StatesOfMatter|states]] (a [[PhysicalProperty|physical change]]).

In other words, matter is neither created nor destroyed in ordinary chemical and physical processes — it is only rearranged.

Examples from the chapter:
- **Beer brewing:** ingredients (water, yeast, grains, malt, hops, sugar) convert into beer (water, alcohol, carbonation, flavoring) with no net loss of substance; when glucose becomes ethanol and CO₂, total mass is unchanged.
- **Lead-acid battery:** lead, lead oxide, and sulfuric acid convert to lead sulfate and water with no change in total matter.

This law is the conceptual basis for balancing chemical equations and for mass-based stoichiometry in later chapters.

## Application in Chemical Reactions (Ch 4)

Conservation of matter is the rule a [[ChemicalEquation|chemical equation]] must obey: each element must show **equal numbers of atoms** on the reactant and product sides. This is enforced by [[BalancingChemicalEquations|balancing]] (adjusting coefficients only), and it underwrites all of [[ReactionStoichiometry|reaction stoichiometry]] — because atoms are conserved, the balanced coefficients give exact mole ratios between substances.

## Atomic-Theory Basis (Ch 2)

Conservation of matter follows directly from the fifth postulate of [[AtomicTheory|Dalton's atomic theory]]: "[[Atom|atoms]] are neither created nor destroyed during a chemical change, but are instead rearranged." Because atoms only rearrange, the total mass before and after a chemical change is unchanged.
