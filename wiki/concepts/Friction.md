## Definition
Friction is a resistive force that opposes the relative motion (or tendency toward motion) between two surfaces in contact, acting parallel to the contact surfaces (perpendicular to the [[NormalForce]]). Chapter 4 introduces it qualitatively; Chapter 5 treats it quantitatively, splitting it into static and kinetic regimes.

## Key Points
- Friction is the usual hidden net force behind everyday slowing of objects, which is why [[NewtonsFirstLaw]] can seem counterintuitive — on nearly frictionless surfaces, motion persists much longer.
- Two regimes: [[StaticFriction]] (variable, up to a maximum) and [[KineticFriction]] (roughly constant during sliding). Maximum static friction usually exceeds kinetic friction.
- Magnitude depends on material properties and the normal force (via the [[CoefficientOfFriction]]), not on the contact area. At low nonzero speeds kinetic friction is nearly velocity-independent.
- It reduces acceleration down an incline: a = (mg sin θ − f) / m, versus the frictionless a = g sin θ.
- Friction (and the fluid analog, [[DragForce]]) is electromagnetic in origin at the atomic level (see [[FundamentalForces]]).
- Lubrication (e.g. synovial fluid in joints, μ ≈ 0.015) dramatically lowers friction coefficients — relevant to artificial joints.

## Equations
- Static friction: f_s ≤ μ_s N; maximum f_s(max) = μ_s N
- Kinetic friction: f_k = μ_k N
- On an incline: f_k = μ_k m g cos θ
- Constant-velocity sliding on an incline: μ_k = tan θ
- Acceleration down an incline with friction f: a = (mg sin θ − f) / m
- Frictionless incline (for comparison): a = g sin θ
