# Argument

In logic's sense, an **argument** is *"a set of statements, some of which (the premises) attempt to provide a reason for thinking that some other statement (the conclusion) is true"* ([[logic-text-v2|Van Cleave]] §1.1). This is distinct from the everyday sense of a heated quarrel, and distinct from an **explanation** (which tells you *why* a fact obtains, presupposing you already accept *that* it obtains).

## Anatomy
- **Statement** — a sentence that can be true or false (a *declarative* sentence). Questions, commands, and requests are **not** statements. Both premises and conclusions are statements.
- **Premise** — a statement offered as a reason. An argument typically has two or more.
- **Conclusion** — the statement the premises are meant to support.
- **Standard form** — premises numbered, conclusion last and marked by "therefore":
  ```
  1. It is morally wrong to take the life of an innocent human being
  2. A fetus is an innocent human being
  3. Therefore, abortion is morally wrong   (from 1, 2)
  ```

## Identifying arguments
Ask: *is someone trying to establish one statement as true on the basis of another?* **Premise indicators** ("since, because, for, as, given that") and **conclusion indicators** ("therefore, so, hence, thus, it follows that") are reliable signposts. An argument is present independent of whether anyone is persuaded, and independent of whether you agree with it.

## Reconstruction
Putting a real-world passage into standard form often requires **paraphrasing**, applying the **principle of charity** (the strongest plausible reading), and supplying **missing premises** — an argument with an unstated assumption can be made explicit and often turned from invalid to [[Validity|valid]] by adding the weakest premise that does the job. This reconstruction step is the heart of [[CriticalThinking|critical thinking]].
