# Amphoteric (Amphiprotic) Species

An **amphiprotic** species can either donate or accept a proton, acting as a [[BronstedLowryAcidsAndBases|Brønsted-Lowry]] acid in one reaction and as a base in another. **Amphoteric** is the more general term (it also covers acid-base definitions other than Brønsted-Lowry); in proton-transfer contexts the two are used interchangeably.

## Water

The classic example is water, which donates a proton (becoming OH⁻) to a base and accepts one (becoming H₃O⁺) from an acid. Because water is amphiprotic, its molecules react with one another in [[WaterAutoionization|autoionization]].

## Bicarbonate ion

The bicarbonate ion HCO₃⁻ illustrates both roles:

$$\text{as acid:}\quad \mathrm{HCO_3^-(aq) + H_2O(l) \rightleftharpoons CO_3^{2-}(aq) + H_3O^+(aq)}$$
$$\text{as base:}\quad \mathrm{HCO_3^-(aq) + H_2O(l) \rightleftharpoons H_2CO_3(aq) + OH^-(aq)}$$

Both equilibria are established simultaneously; which dominates the solution's pH is decided by comparing the relevant Ka and Kb (see [[SaltHydrolysis]] — e.g. for NaHCO₃, Kb ≫ Ka, so the solution is basic). The intermediate ions of a [[PolyproticAcid|polyprotic acid]] (HCO₃⁻, H₂PO₄⁻, HPO₄²⁻) are generally amphiprotic.

## Amphoteric hydroxides

Some metal hydroxides near the metal-nonmetal diagonal (e.g. aluminum hydroxide) are amphoteric, reacting as acids with strong bases and as bases with strong acids:

$$\mathrm{Al(H_2O)_3(OH)_3 + OH^- \rightleftharpoons H_2O + [Al(H_2O)_2(OH)_4]^-}$$
$$\mathrm{3H_3O^+ + Al(H_2O)_3(OH)_3 \rightleftharpoons [Al(H_2O)_6]^{3+} + 3H_2O}$$
