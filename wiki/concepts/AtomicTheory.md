# Atomic Theory (Dalton's Postulates)

**Dalton's atomic theory**, first published by [[JohnDalton]] in 1807, was the first modern, quantitative statement that matter is made of [[Atom|atoms]]. It revived and made experimentally testable the ancient idea (Leucippus, Democritus) of indivisible particles called *atomos*, displacing Aristotle's four-element, infinitely-divisible view that had dominated for two millennia.

## The Five Postulates

1. **Matter is composed of atoms.** An atom is the smallest unit of a [[ChemicalElement|element]] that can participate in a chemical change.
2. **An element consists of one type of atom**, with a characteristic mass identical for all atoms of that element; all share identical chemical properties.
3. **Atoms of different elements differ** in their properties.
4. **A [[ChemicalCompound|compound]]** consists of atoms of two or more elements combined in a small, whole-number ratio; in a given compound the ratio of each element's atoms is always the same.
5. **Atoms are neither created nor destroyed** during a chemical change, only rearranged into different substances.

## What the Postulates Explain

- Postulate 5 is the atomic-scale basis of the [[ConservationOfMatter|law of conservation of matter]].
- Postulate 4 explains the [[LawOfDefiniteProportions|law of definite proportions]] (constant mass ratios within a compound) and the [[LawOfMultipleProportions|law of multiple proportions]] (small whole-number mass ratios across compounds of the same two elements).

## Later Revision

Dalton's atoms were assumed indivisible. The [[EvolutionOfAtomicTheory|experimental evolution of atomic theory]] (Thomson, Millikan, Rutherford, Chadwick) later showed atoms have internal [[AtomicStructure|structure]] — and that [[Isotope|isotopes]] mean not all atoms of an element share exactly the same mass — but Dalton's framework remains the foundation of modern chemistry.
