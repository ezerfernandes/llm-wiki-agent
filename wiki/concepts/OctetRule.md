# Octet Rule

The **octet rule** is the tendency of main-group atoms to gain, lose, or share electrons until they are surrounded by **eight valence electrons**, matching the configuration of a noble gas. It guides the construction of [[LewisStructure|Lewis structures]].

## Scope

The rule applies most reliably to second-period nonmetals (C, N, O, F). **Hydrogen** is an exception — it is stable with only **two** electrons (a duet). Transition and inner-transition elements do not follow it. Typical bonding patterns: group 14 (C) forms 4 bonds; group 15 (N) forms 3 bonds + 1 lone pair; group 16 (O) forms 2 bonds + 2 lone pairs; group 17 (halogens) form 1 bond + 3 lone pairs.

## Exceptions

1. **Odd-electron molecules (free radicals)** — an odd total of valence electrons leaves an unpaired electron, so not every atom can reach an octet. Example: NO has 5 + 6 = 11 valence electrons; it is reactive.
2. **Electron-deficient molecules** — a central atom from group 2 or 13 has fewer than eight electrons. BeH₂ leaves Be with 4 electrons; BF₃ leaves B with 6. Such molecules readily accept an electron pair (BF₃ + NH₃ → F₃B–NH₃, where N's lone pair forms a dative bond and B completes its octet).
3. **Hypervalent / expanded-octet molecules** — period 3+ elements (n ≥ 3) have empty valence *d* orbitals and can hold more than four electron pairs: PCl₅ (5 pairs), SF₆ (6 pairs), IF₅ (12 e⁻ around I), XeF₄ (12 e⁻ around Xe), XeF₂, XeF₆.

[[FormalCharge|Formal charge]] helps decide whether an expanded-octet structure is preferred.
