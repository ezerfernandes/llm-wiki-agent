# Phase Diagram (Chemistry)

A **phase diagram** combines the pressure-vs-temperature curves for the solid-liquid, liquid-gas, and solid-gas equilibria of a substance. It shows which physical state ([[StatesOfMatter|solid, liquid, or gas]]) exists at any given pressure and temperature, and how transition temperatures depend on pressure. Axes: temperature (x), pressure (y); three regions and three boundary curves.

## The Three Boundary Curves

- **Solid-gas curve** — [[PhaseTransitionChemistry|sublimation/deposition]] equilibria; the basis of freeze-drying (a frozen sample held below the ice vapor pressure sublimes).
- **Solid-liquid curve** — melting/freezing; usually a **positive** slope (melting point rises with pressure). **Water is anomalous**, with a slight **negative** slope (melting point falls as pressure rises), partly explaining why glaciers slide on a thin melted layer.
- **Liquid-gas curve** — the [[VaporPressureChemistry|vapor-pressure]] curve; gives the boiling point at any pressure; terminates at the [[CriticalPointChemistry|critical point]].

## Triple Point

The **triple point** is where all three curves meet and all three phases coexist in equilibrium. For water it is 0.01 °C and 0.6 kPa. Below the triple-point pressure a substance cannot exist as a liquid at any temperature.

## Water vs. Carbon Dioxide

- **Water:** negative-slope solid-liquid line; triple point 0.01 °C / 0.6 kPa; critical point 374 °C / 22,089 kPa.
- **CO₂:** positive-slope solid-liquid line; triple point above 1 atm, so CO₂ has no liquid phase at ambient pressure — solid CO₂ (dry ice) **sublimes** at 1 atm; critical point 31.1 °C / 7400 kPa (pressure axis often logarithmic).
