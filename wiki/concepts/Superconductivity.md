## Definition
Superconductivity is a state in which a material has exactly zero electrical resistivity, allowing electric current to flow indefinitely without dissipating energy as heat. It appears only at or below a material-specific **critical temperature (T_c)**.

## Key Points
- Above T_c a superconductor behaves as a normal conductor; at/below T_c resistance collapses to zero.
- **Meissner effect**: superconductors expel magnetic field lines, causing magnets to levitate above them.
- Discovered by H. Kamerlingh Onnes in 1911 (mercury, T_c = 4.2 K, cooled with liquid helium). The microscopic mechanism — quantum-mechanical electron pairing that lets carriers move collision-free — was explained by conventional (BCS) theory in 1957.
- **High-temperature superconductors**: a 1986 ceramic reached 35 K, and by early 1988 a thallium-calcium-barium-copper-oxide ceramic reached T_c = 125 K. This matters because liquid nitrogen (boils at 77 K, ~$0.30/L) is far cheaper than liquid helium (~$5/L).
- High-T_c superconductivity remains theoretically unexplained: thermal atomic vibrations should disrupt electron motion, yet superconductivity persists.
- Reports of T_c above 200 K (near 270 K) are irreproducible — "USOs" (unidentified superconducting objects) — and samples often fail after thermal cycling; reproducibility is essential.

## Applications
- MRI high-current magnets, lossless power transmission, cell-phone RF filters (first commercial high-T_c use), and thin-film research devices.
