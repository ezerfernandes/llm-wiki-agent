## Definition
Chaos theory is the physics of deterministic systems that exhibit extreme sensitivity to initial conditions: tiny differences in starting state grow into drastically different outcomes, making long-term prediction impossible even though the governing laws are fully deterministic. It is closely tied to the study of **complex systems** and self-organization.

> Disambiguation: this page covers the physical, nonlinear-dynamics sense of "chaos/complexity," not algorithmic/computational complexity (see [[ComplexityTax]], [[ModelComplexity]], [[ConservationOfComplexity]]).

## Key Points
- **Complex systems** are collections of interacting components, governed by ordinary physics and chemistry, that spontaneously self-organize and display patterns absent in simpler systems.
- **Complex adaptive systems** emerge quickly, self-organize, and evolve with their environment — primordial oceans, biology, economies, cultures, languages, and even AI share these mechanisms.
- **Chaos with order**: despite sensitivity to initial conditions, chaotic systems can settle into stable, quantifiable structures, often with **fractal** (self-similar at all scales) geometry.
- Systems tend toward greater organization rather than simple disorder, a cross-disciplinary parallel spanning physics, biology, and the social sciences.

## Examples
- Primordial ocean → life: self-replication arose far faster (~500 Myr) than random chance predicts.
- Jupiter's Great Red Spot: a chaotic vortex stabilized for centuries.
- Pluto's orbit: deterministic yet long-term unpredictable.
- Crystal growth and magnetic domains: spontaneous order at phase transitions.
- Cardiac arrhythmias: chaotic heartbeat patterns aid medical diagnostics.
