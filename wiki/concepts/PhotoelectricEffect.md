# Photoelectric Effect

The **photoelectric effect** is the ejection of electrons from a metal surface struck by light above a **threshold frequency**. It was the experiment that forced the particle (photon) view of light.

## Key observations
- Ejected-electron kinetic energy depends on light **frequency**, not brightness.
- Below the threshold frequency, no electrons are emitted regardless of intensity.
- Above the threshold, brighter light ejects *more* electrons (more photons), but each ejected electron's kinetic energy is unchanged.

Classical wave theory failed here because it predicted energy should depend on intensity (amplitude), not frequency.

## Einstein's explanation (1905)
Light is composed of [[PhotonQuantum|photons]] of energy E = hν. A photon must carry enough energy to overcome an electron's binding energy; any surplus becomes kinetic energy. This is treated in full, with worked values (e.g., aluminum threshold = 394 kJ/mol), in [[PhotonQuantum]].

## Quantitative form (College Physics 2e Ch.29)
The maximum kinetic energy of an ejected electron is the photon energy minus the surface **binding energy** (work function) BE:

> **KE_e = hf − BE**, with the threshold set by **BE = hf₀**

Ejection is essentially instantaneous (no energy accumulation), and brighter light ejects *more* electrons without raising their kinetic energy. Worked example: a 420 nm violet photon (~2.96 eV) on calcium (BE = 2.71 eV) ejects electrons of only ~0.25 eV, since the photon energy barely exceeds threshold. Applications: light meters / camera irises and solar cells.
