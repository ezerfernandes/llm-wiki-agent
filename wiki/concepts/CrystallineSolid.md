# Crystalline Solid

In a **crystalline solid** "the atoms, ions, or molecules are arranged in a definite repeating pattern" (its [[UnitCell|unit cell]] repeated through space). Over 90% of solids are crystalline. Because every particle of a given type is held by forces of equal strength, crystalline solids have a **precise melting point**. This contrasts with the [[AmorphousSolid|amorphous solid]], which lacks long-range order and softens over a temperature range.

## Four Types of Crystalline Solids

| Type | Particles | Attractions | Properties | Examples |
|------|-----------|-------------|-----------|----------|
| **Ionic** | Ions | [[IonicBonding|Ionic bonds]] | Hard, brittle; high–very high mp; conduct only when molten/dissolved | NaCl, NiO, Al₂O₃ |
| **Metallic** | Atoms of electropositive elements | Metallic bonding (a "sea" of delocalized electrons) | Shiny, malleable, ductile; conduct heat & electricity; variable hardness/mp | Cu, Fe, Ti, Pb, U |
| **Covalent network** | Atoms of electronegative elements | [[CovalentBonding|Covalent bonds]] throughout | Very hard; very high mp (diamond >3500 °C); not conductive | C(diamond), SiO₂, SiC, Si |
| **Molecular** | Molecules or atoms | [[IntermolecularForces|IMFs]] | Variable hardness; not conductive; low mp (depends on size/polarity) | H₂O, CO₂, I₂, sugar |

Molecular solids span a wide melting range: small nonpolar molecules (H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂) melt below −200 °C, while ice melts at 0 °C, CO₂ at −78 °C, iodine at 114 °C, and table sugar at 185 °C. **Graphite** is an atypical covalent solid — planar sheets held by weaker forces, making it soft and electrically conductive.

## Crystal Defects

Real crystals contain **vacancies** (missing particles), **interstitial sites** (particles squeezed between regular positions), and **impurity defects** (foreign species). **Doping** — deliberately adding trace impurities — tunes properties; e.g., doped silicon for semiconductors and computer chips.
