# Valence Bond Theory

**Valence bond theory** describes a [[CovalentBonding|covalent bond]] as the **overlap of half-filled [[AtomicOrbital|atomic orbitals]]** — each containing a single electron — that yields a pair of electrons shared between the two bonded atoms. It supplies the quantum-mechanical explanation of *why* bonds form that [[VSEPRTheory|VSEPR theory]] (which only predicts shapes) lacks. (Named *ValenceBondTheory* to avoid generic-name collisions.)

A bond results when (1) an orbital on one atom overlaps an orbital on a second atom and (2) the single electrons in each orbital combine into an electron pair. The mutual attraction between this negative pair and the two positive nuclei is the covalent bond.

## Overlap, Energy, and Bond Distance

Bond strength depends on the **extent of overlap** — orbitals that overlap more extensively form stronger bonds. As two atoms approach:

- At long range there is no overlap; energy is defined as zero.
- As they near, attractions exceed repulsions and the system energy falls.
- At the energy **minimum** the atoms sit at the **bond distance** (most stable separation).
- Closer than this, nuclear and electron repulsions dominate and energy rises.

The depth of the minimum is the [[BondEnergy|bond energy]] — the energy released on bond formation (H₂: bond distance 74 pm, 436 kJ/mol). Overlap quality also depends on **orientation**: orbitals overlap best along the direct line between nuclei (except spherical *s* orbitals, which overlap equally in all directions).

## σ and π Bonds; Multiple Bonds

Overlap produces two bond types — see [[SigmaAndPiBonds]]:

- **σ bond**: electron density along the internuclear axis (s–s, s–p, end-to-end p–p).
- **π bond**: side-by-side p-orbital overlap, density above and below the axis.

All single bonds are σ; a double bond is one σ + one π, a triple bond is one σ + two π. The first bond between two atoms is always the σ bond.

## Limitations

Valence bond theory does not easily handle **delocalization** (e.g., the [[ResonanceStructure|resonance]] of benzene or the paramagnetism of O₂); those are better described by [[MolecularOrbitalTheory|molecular orbital theory]]. To match molecular geometries it must invoke [[OrbitalHybridization|hybridization]] of atomic orbitals.
