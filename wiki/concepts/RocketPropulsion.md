# Rocket Propulsion

## Definition
Rocket propulsion is a variable-mass application of [[ConservationOfMomentum]] and [[NewtonsThirdLaw]]: a rocket ejects exhaust gas backward and, by reaction, the remaining system gains forward momentum. Because the rocket's mass changes as fuel burns, it is a canonical variable-mass system requiring the momentum form of [[NewtonsSecondLaw]].

## Key Points
- Thrust arises from recoil: every backward ejection of mass produces an equal, opposite forward force on the rocket (same physics as gun recoil and squid jetting).
- Gravity applies a negative impulse (−mg·Δt), reducing momentum even while mass is expelled.
- Acceleration depends on three factors: higher exhaust velocity (practical limit ~2,500 m/s for conventional engines), faster burn rate, and smaller current mass. As fuel burns the mass drops, so acceleration rises continuously during a burn.
- **Multi-stage rockets** are necessary because a single stage would need roughly 98.9% of its mass to be fuel to reach escape velocity; the space shuttle used reusable boosters and orbiter with an expendable external tank.

## Equations
- Acceleration: a = (v_e/m)·(Δm/Δt) − g
  - v_e = exhaust velocity relative to rocket; m = current mass; Δm/Δt = burn rate; g = gravitational acceleration
- Single-stage final velocity: v = v_e · ln(m₀/m_r)  (m₀ = initial mass, m_r = remaining mass)
