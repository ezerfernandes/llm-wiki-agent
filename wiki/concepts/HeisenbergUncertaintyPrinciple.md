# Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

The **Heisenberg uncertainty principle** (Werner Heisenberg) states that it is fundamentally impossible to determine simultaneously and exactly both the **position** and the **momentum** of a particle. The more precisely one is known, the less precisely the other can be:

> **Δx · Δpₓ = (Δx)(mΔv) ≥ ℏ/2**, where ℏ = h/(2π)

An analogous relation links energy and time:

> **ΔE · Δt ≥ ℏ/2**

## Worked value
For an electron localized to Δx = 1 pm (10⁻¹² m):
Δp = ℏ/(2Δx) = (1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ kg·m²/s)/(2 × 10⁻¹² m) = 5 × 10⁻²³ kg·m/s.

## Measurement and probability (College Physics 2e Ch.29)
Because matter is wavelike (see [[DeBroglieWavelength]]), a single particle's position is not predictable; repeated measurements form a diffraction-like **probability distribution** showing where it is *likely* to be. The principle arises because **measurement disturbs the system**: a probe used to locate a particle transfers momentum unpredictably. A short-wavelength probe pins down position but spoils momentum, and vice versa. College Physics 2e writes the limits with h directly:

> **Δx · Δp ≥ h/(4π)** and **ΔE · Δt ≥ h/(4π)**

Worked illustrations: localizing an electron to 0.01 nm forces a velocity spread ~5.79 × 10⁶ m/s (KE ~95.5 eV, exceeding atomic level spacings — orbits can't be tracked); a 10⁻¹⁰ s excited state has ~3.3 µeV energy uncertainty, while a 10⁻²⁵ s particle shows GeV-scale energy spread (affecting mass measurements). The energy-time relation also permits brief, "virtual" violations of energy conservation underlying quantum field processes.

## Significance
The principle is a direct consequence of [[WaveParticleDuality|wave-particle duality]] and is what forbids the precise classical orbits of the [[BohrModel|Bohr model]]. Instead of definite paths, the electron is described by a probability distribution — the [[AtomicOrbital|atomic orbital]] from |ψ|². It is a defining feature separating quantum theory from classical mechanics.
