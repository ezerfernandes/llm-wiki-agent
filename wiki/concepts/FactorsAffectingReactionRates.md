# Factors Affecting Reaction Rates

Reactions occur when molecules collide and undergo chemical transformation, so anything that changes the **frequency or effectiveness of collisions** changes the [[ReactionRate|reaction rate]]. OpenStax identifies five factors:

1. **Chemical nature of the reactants.** Different substances react at vastly different intrinsic rates. Sodium reacts with air overnight while iron barely changes; calcium reacts moderately with water while sodium reacts explosively.
2. **Physical state and surface area.** Reactants in different phases react only at the interface between phases, so finely divided solids (more surface area) react faster. Iron powder reacts rapidly with dilute HCl while a nail reacts slowly; sawdust can burn explosively while a log only smolders.
3. **Temperature.** Higher [[Temperature|temperature]] speeds reactions — roughly doubling the rate per +10 °C — because more molecules have enough energy to react (see [[KineticMolecularTheory]] and [[ActivationEnergy]]). Refrigeration slows food spoilage for this reason.
4. **Concentration.** Higher reactant [[Molarity|concentration]] usually raises the rate by increasing collision frequency. Phosphorus burns faster in pure O₂ than in air; carbonate stone deteriorates faster in SO₂-rich air. The quantitative dependence is captured by the [[RateLaw]].
5. **Catalyst.** A [[Catalysis|catalyst]] increases the rate without being consumed, by providing a lower-[[ActivationEnergy|activation-energy]] pathway. Dilute H₂O₂ decomposes slowly until biological catalysts in tissue make it foam.

These factors are explained microscopically by [[CollisionTheory]].
