## Overview
**Michael Faraday** (1791–1867) was an English experimental physicist and chemist who discovered **electromagnetic induction** — that a changing [[MagneticFlux|magnetic flux]] induces an [[ElectromotiveForce|emf]] in a conductor. This finding, the converse of Oersted's observation that currents produce magnetic fields, established the deep symmetry between electricity and magnetism and underlies essentially all modern electrical power technology. He also founded much of **[[Electrochemistry|electrochemistry]]**, giving the quantitative laws of electrolysis.

## Key Contributions
- **[[FaradaysLaw|Faraday's law of induction]]**: induced emf is proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux and the number of coil turns — the quantitative basis of [[ElectricGenerator|generators]], [[ElectricalTransformer|transformers]], and [[Inductance|inductors]].
- The **iron-ring experiment**: switching current in a primary coil induced transient currents in a secondary coil, demonstrating that only *changing* flux induces an emf.
- Showed induction depends on *relative* motion between magnet and coil, and that faster change yields larger emf.

## In Electrochemistry
- **[[FaradaysLawsOfElectrolysis|Faraday's laws of electrolysis]]:** the amount of substance produced at an electrode is proportional to the electric charge passed (Q = It = nF).
- The **Faraday constant** F = **96,485 C/mol of electrons** is named for him; it appears both in electrolysis stoichiometry and in the thermodynamic bridge ΔG° = −nF·E°cell (see [[CellPotential]], [[GibbsFreeEnergy]]).
