## Summary
MAD (Michigan Algorithm Decoder) is an imperative, procedural programming language developed in 1959-1960 at the University of Michigan by Bernard Galler, Bruce Arden, and Robert Graham as a faster-compiling alternative to IBM's IT and the early ALGOL dialects. Statically and explicitly typed with integer, floating-point, and Boolean variable modes, it ran on IBM 704/709/7090 mainframes and supported user-defined operators, functions, and a macro-like definition facility unusual for its era. MAD saw heavy use in academic computing and systems work during the 1960s -- notably in the development of the MTS (Michigan Terminal System) and the CTSS time-sharing environment -- before fading as FORTRAN and ALGOL descendants dominated. Variants such as MAD/I and GOM (Good Old MAD) extended the original for newer hardware.
