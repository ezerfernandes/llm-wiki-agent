## Summary
Lingo is the dynamically typed, interpreted scripting language created by John H. Thompson in 1989 for Macromedia (originally MacroMind) Director, the dominant authoring tool for interactive multimedia and CD-ROM titles throughout the 1990s and early 2000s. It is primarily imperative and event-driven, with verbose, English-like syntax and a message-passing model in which scripts (movie, behavior, parent, and cast-member scripts) respond to handlers triggered by frame, mouse, and timer events; later versions added object-oriented features through parent scripts and a more conventional dot-notation syntax. Lingo manipulates the "Score" timeline, sprites, and cast members on the Stage, and was widely used for game development, kiosks, educational software, and Shockwave content delivered in web browsers. With the decline of Director and Shockwave (Adobe ended Shockwave support in 2019), Lingo is now largely a legacy language, though it remains influential as one of the first scripting languages aimed at non-programmer multimedia authors.
