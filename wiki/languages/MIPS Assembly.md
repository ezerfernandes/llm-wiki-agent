## Summary
MIPS Assembly is the low-level, imperative programming language for the MIPS instruction set architecture, a classic RISC design originated by John Hennessy's team at Stanford in the early 1980s and commercialized by MIPS Computer Systems. As an assembly language it is untyped, operating directly on a fixed bank of 32 general-purpose registers and memory addresses, with each mnemonic mapping closely to a single machine instruction; programmers manage the calling convention, register allocation, and load/store memory access by hand. Its clean, regular, fixed-width encoding made MIPS a longtime staple of computer-architecture education (notably the SPIM and MARS simulators) while also powering real-world embedded systems, networking gear, and game consoles such as the PlayStation and Nintendo 64. Typical use is for performance-critical routines, systems and firmware programming, and teaching how processors execute code at the hardware level.
