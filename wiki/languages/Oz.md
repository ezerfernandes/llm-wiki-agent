## Summary
Oz is a multi-paradigm programming language developed in the early 1990s by Gert Smolka and his group, designed to unify functional, logic, constraint, object-oriented, imperative, and concurrent programming within a single coherent model. It is dynamically typed and built around dataflow variables and lightweight threads, making concurrency and declarative concurrent programming central rather than bolted on. Its most widely used implementation is the Mozart Programming System, and the language is famous as the vehicle for the textbook "Concepts, Techniques, and Models of Computer Programming" by Van Roy and Haridi. Oz is used primarily in teaching and research, especially for exploring constraint satisfaction, distributed computing, and the relationships between programming paradigms.
