## Summary

Chapter 6 (book pp. 172–224) of [[mml-book|Mathematics for Machine Learning]] is the probability foundation of the book — the substrate for probabilistic modeling (§8.4), [[BayesianLinearRegression]] (Ch 9), [[GaussianMixtureModel|GMMs]] (Ch 11), and most uncertainty-aware ML. It is deliberately "slightly hand-wavy" (it avoids measure theory) to keep the intuition visible. The arc runs: **build a probability space** $(\Omega, \mathcal{A}, P)$ and the [[RandomVariable|random variable]] as a *function* $X:\Omega\to\mathcal{T}$ (§6.1); **describe distributions** via [[ProbabilityMassFunction|pmf]] / [[ProbabilityDensityFunction|pdf]] / [[CumulativeDistributionFunction|cdf]] (§6.2); reduce all of probability to the **[[SumRule|sum rule]] + [[ProductRule|product rule]] + [[BayesTheorem|Bayes' theorem]]** (§6.3); summarize and compare random variables via **[[ExpectedValue|expectation]], [[Mean|mean]], [[Median|median]], [[Mode|mode]], [[Variance|variance]], [[Covariance|covariance]], [[CovarianceMatrix|covariance matrix]], [[Correlation|correlation]], [[EmpiricalMean|empirical statistics]], [[StatisticalIndependence|independence]], [[ConditionalIndependence|conditional independence]]**, and the **random-variables-as-an-inner-product-space** view (§6.4); develop the **[[GaussianDistribution|Gaussian]]** and its closure under marginalization / conditioning / products / linear transforms / sampling (§6.5); introduce **[[ConjugatePrior|conjugacy]], [[SufficientStatistics|sufficient statistics]], and the [[ExponentialFamily|exponential family]] with [[NaturalParameters|natural parameters]]** (§6.6); derive **[[ChangeOfVariables|change of variables / inverse transform]]** with the Jacobian determinant (§6.7); and close with further reading (§6.8). The opening frame (citing Jaynes 2003) is that **probability generalizes Boolean logic** ([[CoxJaynesTheorem]]), and the book is *agnostic* between the Bayesian and frequentist interpretations.

## Key Claims

- **Probability is a generalization of Boolean logic / logical reasoning** (§6.1, §6.1.1, p. 172–174, citing Jaynes 2003 and Pearl 1988). Classical Boolean logic cannot express plausible reasoning: observing "$A$ is false" makes $B$ "less plausible" though no conclusion is licensed; observing "$B$ is true" makes $A$ "more plausible." The "waiting for a late friend" scenario (H1 on time / H2 traffic / H3 abducted by aliens, p. 173) shows everyday reasoning ruling H1 out and ranking H2 over H3 without logical compulsion. *"For plausible reasoning it is necessary to extend the discrete true and false values of truth to continuous plausibilities."* (Jaynes 2003, marginal, p. 173). The [[CoxJaynesTheorem|Cox–Jaynes theorem]] proves Jaynes's three desiderata (plausibilities are real numbers; based on common sense; consistent — with the three senses *non-contradiction*, *honesty*, *reproducibility*, p. 174) are **sufficient to fix the universal rules of plausibility up to a monotonic transform — and those rules *are* the rules of probability**.
- **The book is agnostic between Bayesian and frequentist interpretations** (§6.1, Remark, p. 174). Bayesian = degree of belief / "subjective probability" (Bishop 2006); frequentist = limiting relative frequency in the limit of infinite data (Efron & Hastie 2016). MML uses both interpretations throughout without committing.
- **A probability space is the triple $(\Omega, \mathcal{A}, P)$** (§6.1.2, p. 174–176, axioms due to Kolmogorov; Grinstead & Snell 1997, Jaynes 2003). **Sample space $\Omega$** = set of all outcomes (e.g. two coin tosses → $\{hh, tt, ht, th\}$); **event space $\mathcal{A}$** = the observable subsets of $\Omega$ (for discrete $\Omega$ usually the power set $2^\Omega$); **probability $P:\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]$** with $P(\Omega)=1$. $\Omega$ goes by many names across books — "state space" (Jacod & Protter 2004), "sample description space," "possibility space," "event space" (p. 175–176 Remark).
- **A random variable is a *function*, not a variable** (§6.1.2, p. 175). $X:\Omega\to\mathcal{T}$ maps outcomes to a convenient **target space** $\mathcal{T}$ (the book calls its elements *states*); for finite $\Omega, \mathcal{T}$ it is "essentially a lookup table." The induced **law / distribution** is $P_X(S) = P(X\in S) = P(X^{-1}(S)) = P(\{\omega\in\Omega : X(\omega)\in S\})$ (Eq. 6.8, p. 177); equivalently $P_X = P\circ X^{-1}$. The biased-two-coin example (Example 6.1, p. 176, $X$ = number of \$ drawn with replacement, $P(\$)=0.3$) gives $P(X{=}2){=}0.09$, $P(X{=}1){=}0.42$, $P(X{=}0){=}0.49$ (Eqs. 6.5–6.7).
- **Probability vs. statistics, and where ML sits** (§6.1.3, p. 177–178). Probability: given a model/process, derive what happens; statistics: given observations, infer the underlying process. ML is close to statistics — it constructs a "best-fitting" model for data — but additionally cares about *generalization error* (Ch 8) on future, non-identical instances.
- **Discrete distributions use a pmf; continuous use pdf + cdf** (§6.2, p. 178–183). [[ProbabilityMassFunction|pmf]] $P(X{=}x)$ for discrete (point probability); [[ProbabilityDensityFunction|pdf]] $f(x)$ for continuous (Def. 6.1: $f\ge 0$ and $\int_{\mathbb{R}^D} f(\mathbf x)\,d\mathbf x = 1$, Eq. 6.15) with $P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ (Eq. 6.16); [[CumulativeDistributionFunction|cdf]] $F_X(\mathbf x)=P(X_1\le x_1,\dots,X_D\le x_D)$ (Def. 6.2, Eqs. 6.17–6.18). **For a continuous RV, $P(X{=}x)=0$** ("a set of measure zero," p. 181). Densities *can exceed 1* (only the integral is constrained, Fig. 6.3 / Eq. 6.19). Joint pmf of two discrete RVs is a 2-D table $P(X{=}x_i,Y{=}y_j)=n_{ij}/N$ (Eq. 6.9); marginals are row/column sums (Eqs. 6.10–6.11); conditionals are cell÷row-or-column (Eqs. 6.13–6.14). Nomenclature is summarized in **Table 6.1** (Discrete: pmf / N.A.; Continuous: pdf / cdf).
- **All of probability reduces to two rules + Bayes** (§6.3, p. 183–186). **[[SumRule|Sum rule]] / marginalization** $p(\mathbf x)=\sum_{\mathbf y} p(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)$ (discrete) or $\int p(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\,d\mathbf y$ (continuous) (Eq. 6.20); $p(x_i)=\int p(x_1,\dots,x_D)\,d\mathbf x_{\setminus i}$ (Eq. 6.21, "$\setminus i$" = all but $i$). High-dimensional sums/integrals are generally computationally hard (no known poly-time exact algorithm — p. 184 Remark). **[[ProductRule|Product rule]]** $p(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)=p(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x)\,p(\mathbf x)$ (Eq. 6.22) — every joint factorizes. **[[BayesTheorem|Bayes' theorem]]** $p(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf y)=\dfrac{p(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x)\,p(\mathbf x)}{p(\mathbf y)}$ (Eq. 6.23): $\underbrace{p(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf y)}_{\text{posterior}}, \underbrace{p(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x)}_{\text{likelihood}}, \underbrace{p(\mathbf x)}_{\text{prior}}, \underbrace{p(\mathbf y)}_{\text{evidence}}$. The **prior** must put nonzero mass on all plausible $\mathbf x$; the **likelihood** $p(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x)$ ("measurement model," MacKay 2003) is a distribution in $\mathbf y$ only — *"never the likelihood of $\mathbf y$"*; the **evidence / marginal likelihood** $p(\mathbf y)=\int p(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x)p(\mathbf x)\,d\mathbf x = \mathbb E_X[p(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x)]$ (Eq. 6.27) normalizes the posterior and drives model selection (§8.6) but is often hard to compute. Bayes' theorem is the *probabilistic inverse* (p. 186).
- **The expected value is foundational; mean is its special case** (§6.4.1, p. 187–189). $\mathbb E_X[g(x)]=\int_{\mathcal X} g(x)p(x)\,dx$ (continuous, Eq. 6.28) or $\sum_{x\in\mathcal X} g(x)p(x)$ (discrete, Eq. 6.29) — "law of the unconscious statistician" (Casella & Berger 2002). Whittle (2000): foundational probability can be *derived from* expectation. **[[Mean|Mean]]** (Def. 6.4) = $\mathbb E_X[x]$ with $g$ the identity (Eqs. 6.31–6.32), computed element-wise for vectors. **[[Median|Median]]** = the "middle" value (50/50 split / cdf $=0.5$); more robust to outliers and long tails than the mean; hard to generalize to >1 dimension (no canonical ordering, p. 188). **[[Mode|Mode]]** = most frequent value (discrete) / a peak of $p(\mathbf x)$ (continuous); a density can be multimodal (Example 6.4 / Fig. 6.4 is a bimodal Gaussian mixture). **Expectation is a linear operator** (Eq. 6.34: $\mathbb E[ag+bh]=a\mathbb E[g]+b\mathbb E[h]$).
- **(Co)variance, covariance matrix, correlation** (§6.4.1, p. 189–191). **[[Covariance|Covariance]]** (univariate, Def. 6.5) $\mathrm{Cov}_{X,Y}[x,y]=\mathbb E_{X,Y}[(x-\mathbb E_X[x])(y-\mathbb E_Y[y])]$ (Eq. 6.35) $=\mathbb E[xy]-\mathbb E[x]\mathbb E[y]$ (Eq. 6.36). Multivariate (Def. 6.6) $\mathrm{Cov}[\mathbf x,\mathbf y]=\mathbb E[\mathbf x\mathbf y^\top]-\mathbb E[\mathbf x]\mathbb E[\mathbf y]^\top=\mathrm{Cov}[\mathbf y,\mathbf x]^\top\in\mathbb R^{D\times E}$ (Eq. 6.37) — the cross-covariance. **[[Variance|Variance]]** (Def. 6.7) $\mathbb V_X[x]=\mathrm{Cov}_X[\mathbf x,\mathbf x]=\mathbb E[(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top]=\mathbb E[\mathbf x\mathbf x^\top]-\mathbb E[\mathbf x]\mathbb E[\mathbf x]^\top$ (Eq. 6.38). Its matrix form is the **[[CovarianceMatrix|covariance matrix]]** (Eq. 6.38c): symmetric PSD, diagonal = marginal variances, off-diagonal = cross-covariances $\mathrm{Cov}[x_i,x_j]$ (Eq. 6.39); the book assumes PD to "enable better intuition." **Standard deviation** $\sigma(x)=\sqrt{\mathbb V[x]}$. **[[Correlation|Correlation]]** (Def. 6.8) $\mathrm{corr}[x,y]=\dfrac{\mathrm{Cov}[x,y]}{\sqrt{\mathbb V[x]\mathbb V[y]}}\in[-1,1]$ (Eq. 6.40) — the normalized covariance; the correlation matrix is the covariance matrix of standardized RVs $x/\sigma(x)$. Fig. 6.5: two datasets with identical means and per-axis variances but opposite-sign covariance.
- **Empirical mean and covariance estimate the population statistics from data** (§6.4.2, p. 191–192). Definitions 6.4–6.8 are *population* statistics. Given a dataset of size $N$ with realizations $\mathbf x_1,\dots,\mathbf x_N$: **empirical / sample mean** $\bar{\mathbf x}=\frac1N\sum_n \mathbf x_n$ (Def. 6.9, Eq. 6.41); **empirical covariance** $\boldsymbol\Sigma=\frac1N\sum_n(\mathbf x_n-\bar{\mathbf x})(\mathbf x_n-\bar{\mathbf x})^\top$ (Eq. 6.42) — symmetric PSD, *biased* (the unbiased / corrected version divides by $N-1$, Bessel's correction; the book uses the biased $1/N$ form throughout). See [[EmpiricalMean]], [[DataCovarianceMatrix]].
- **Three expressions for the variance** (§6.4.3, p. 192–193). (1) Standard definition $\mathbb V_X[x]=\mathbb E_X[(x-\mu)^2]$ (Eq. 6.43) — needs a *two-pass* algorithm (mean first, then squared deviations). (2) **Raw-score formula** $\mathbb V_X[x]=\mathbb E_X[x^2]-(\mathbb E_X[x])^2$ (Eq. 6.44) = "mean of the square minus square of the mean" — one-pass but numerically unstable when the two terms are large and nearly equal (catastrophic cancellation in floating point, p. 193 marginal); used to derive the [[BiasVarianceDecomposition|bias–variance decomposition]]. (3) **Sum of pairwise differences** $\frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{i,j}(x_i-x_j)^2 = 2\big[\frac1N\sum x_i^2-(\frac1N\sum x_i)^2\big]$ (Eq. 6.45) = twice the raw-score expression — geometrically, pairwise distances ($N^2$ terms) equal distances from the center ($N$ terms).
- **Sums and affine transformations of random variables** (§6.4.4, p. 193–194). $\mathbb E[\mathbf x\pm\mathbf y]=\mathbb E[\mathbf x]\pm\mathbb E[\mathbf y]$ (Eqs. 6.46–6.47); $\mathbb V[\mathbf x\pm\mathbf y]=\mathbb V[\mathbf x]+\mathbb V[\mathbf y]\pm\mathrm{Cov}[\mathbf x,\mathbf y]\pm\mathrm{Cov}[\mathbf y,\mathbf x]$ (Eqs. 6.48–6.49). For affine $\mathbf y=\mathbf A\mathbf x+\mathbf b$: $\mathbb E_Y[\mathbf y]=\mathbf A\boldsymbol\mu+\mathbf b$ (Eq. 6.50), $\mathbb V_Y[\mathbf y]=\mathbf A\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf A^\top$ (Eq. 6.51), $\mathrm{Cov}[\mathbf x,\mathbf y]=\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf A^\top$ (Eq. 6.52).
- **Statistical independence and conditional independence** (§6.4.5, p. 194–195). $X\perp Y$ iff $p(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)=p(\mathbf x)p(\mathbf y)$ (Def. 6.10, Eq. 6.53), equivalently $p(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x)=p(\mathbf y)$. Then $\mathbb V_{X,Y}[\mathbf x+\mathbf y]=\mathbb V_X[\mathbf x]+\mathbb V_Y[\mathbf y]$ and $\mathrm{Cov}_{X,Y}[\mathbf x,\mathbf y]=\mathbf 0$. **Zero covariance does *not* imply independence** — covariance measures only *linear* dependence; Example 6.5 ($X$ zero-mean with $\mathbb E[x^3]=0$, $Y=x^2$ dependent on $X$) has $\mathrm{Cov}[x,y]=\mathbb E[x^3]=0$ despite dependence. **[[ConditionalIndependence|Conditional independence]]** $X\perp Y\mid Z$ iff $p(\mathbf x,\mathbf y\mid\mathbf z)=p(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)\,p(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf z)$ for all $\mathbf z$ (Def. 6.11, Eq. 6.55), equivalently $p(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf y,\mathbf z)=p(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)$ (Eq. 6.57); plain independence is the special case $X\perp Y\mid\emptyset$. **i.i.d.** = mutually independent (all subsets independent) + identically distributed (p. 195).
- **Random variables form an inner-product space** (§6.4.6, p. 195–197). For zero-mean RVs, $\langle X,Y\rangle := \mathrm{Cov}[x,y]$ is a valid [[InnerProduct|inner product]] (symmetric, positive definite — $\mathrm{Cov}[x,x]=0\iff x=0$ — bilinear) (Eq. 6.59). Then **length** $\|X\|=\sqrt{\mathrm{Cov}[x,x]}=\sqrt{\mathbb V[x]}=\sigma[x]$ (Eq. 6.60) — longer = more uncertain; length 0 = deterministic. The **angle** satisfies $\cos\theta=\frac{\langle X,Y\rangle}{\|X\|\,\|Y\|}=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}[x,y]}{\sqrt{\mathbb V[x]\mathbb V[y]}}=\mathrm{corr}[x,y]$ (Eq. 6.61) — **correlation is the cosine of the angle between two random variables**, and $X\perp Y$ (orthogonal) iff $\mathrm{Cov}[x,y]=0$ (uncorrelated). For uncorrelated $X,Y$, $\mathbb V[x+y]=\mathbb V[x]+\mathbb V[y]$ (Eq. 6.58) is literally the **Pythagorean theorem** $c^2=a^2+b^2$ with side lengths $\sigma$ (Fig. 6.6). Remark (p. 196–197): Euclidean distance is *not* the right metric for comparing distributions — they live on a *statistical manifold* studied by *information geometry*; use [[KullbackLeiblerDivergence|KL divergence]] (a Bregman / $f$-divergence; Amari 2016) instead.
- **The Gaussian is the central continuous distribution** (§6.5, p. 197–204). Univariate $p(x\mid\mu,\sigma^2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2})$ (Eq. 6.62); multivariate $p(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma)=(2\pi)^{-D/2}|\boldsymbol\Sigma|^{-1/2}\exp(-\frac12(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu))$ (Eq. 6.63), fully characterized by mean vector $\boldsymbol\mu$ + covariance matrix $\boldsymbol\Sigma$. $\boldsymbol\mu=\mathbf 0, \boldsymbol\Sigma=\mathbf I$ = **standard normal**. It arises from the central limit theorem (sums of i.i.d. RVs, marginal p. 197). Used for likelihood + prior in linear regression (Ch 9), GMMs (Ch 11), Gaussian processes, variational inference, RL, Kalman filters, LQR, hypothesis testing.
  - **Marginals and conditionals of a Gaussian are Gaussian** (§6.5.1, p. 198–200). Writing the joint $p(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)=\mathcal N\!\big(\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol\mu_x\\\boldsymbol\mu_y\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{xx}&\boldsymbol\Sigma_{xy}\\\boldsymbol\Sigma_{yx}&\boldsymbol\Sigma_{yy}\end{bmatrix}\big)$ (Eq. 6.64): **conditional** $p(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf y)=\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_{x\mid y},\boldsymbol\Sigma_{x\mid y})$ with $\boldsymbol\mu_{x\mid y}=\boldsymbol\mu_x+\boldsymbol\Sigma_{xy}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{yy}^{-1}(\mathbf y-\boldsymbol\mu_y)$ (Eq. 6.66) and $\boldsymbol\Sigma_{x\mid y}=\boldsymbol\Sigma_{xx}-\boldsymbol\Sigma_{xy}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{yy}^{-1}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{yx}$ (Eq. 6.67); **marginal** $p(\mathbf x)=\int p(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\,d\mathbf y=\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu_x,\boldsymbol\Sigma_{xx})$ (Eq. 6.68, just read off the relevant block). The Kalman filter is "nothing but computing Gaussian conditionals" (p. 199). Example 6.6 conditions a bivariate Gaussian on $x_2=-1$ to get $\mathcal N(0.6,0.1)$, and marginalizes to $p(x_1)=\mathcal N(0,0.3)$.
  - **Product of two Gaussian densities is a scaled Gaussian** (§6.5.2, p. 201). $\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf a,\mathbf A)\,\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf b,\mathbf B)=c\,\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf c,\mathbf C)$ with $\mathbf C=(\mathbf A^{-1}+\mathbf B^{-1})^{-1}$ (Eq. 6.74), $\mathbf c=\mathbf C(\mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf a+\mathbf B^{-1}\mathbf b)$ (Eq. 6.75), and the scaling $c=\mathcal N(\mathbf a\mid\mathbf b,\mathbf A+\mathbf B)=\mathcal N(\mathbf b\mid\mathbf a,\mathbf A+\mathbf B)$ (Eqs. 6.76–6.77). This is exactly the likelihood×prior computation behind Gaussian conjugacy in linear regression.
  - **Sums and linear transforms of Gaussians stay Gaussian** (§6.5.3, p. 201–204). For independent $X,Y$ Gaussian, $p(\mathbf x+\mathbf y)=\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_x+\boldsymbol\mu_y,\boldsymbol\Sigma_x+\boldsymbol\Sigma_y)$ (Eq. 6.78); weighted sums $p(a\mathbf x+b\mathbf y)=\mathcal N(a\boldsymbol\mu_x+b\boldsymbol\mu_y, a^2\boldsymbol\Sigma_x+b^2\boldsymbol\Sigma_y)$ (Eq. 6.79). Theorem 6.12 gives the mean (Eq. 6.81) and variance (Eq. 6.82) of a *mixture* $p(x)=\alpha p_1(x)+(1-\alpha)p_2(x)$ — note the *law of total variance* connection (Eq. 6.85c). **Any linear/affine transform of a Gaussian is Gaussian**: $\mathbf y=\mathbf A\mathbf x\Rightarrow p(\mathbf y)=\mathcal N(\mathbf A\boldsymbol\mu,\mathbf A\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf A^\top)$ (Eqs. 6.86–6.88); the reverse problem uses the pseudo-inverse $(\mathbf A^\top\mathbf A)^{-1}\mathbf A^\top$ when $\mathbf A$ is not invertible (Eqs. 6.89–6.91).
  - **Sampling from a multivariate Gaussian** (§6.5.4, p. 204). Three stages: (1) a uniform PRNG on $[0,1]$; (2) Box–Müller (Devroye 1986) to get a univariate Gaussian; (3) collate into $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$. For a general $\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma)$: factor $\boldsymbol\Sigma=\mathbf A\mathbf A^\top$ via [[CholeskyDecomposition|Cholesky]] (triangular $\mathbf A$ → efficient), sample $\mathbf x\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$, return $\mathbf y=\mathbf A\mathbf x+\boldsymbol\mu$.
- **Conjugacy and the exponential family** (§6.6, p. 205–214). Three desiderata for ML-friendly distributions (p. 205): (1) a "closure property" under probability operations (apply Bayes → get same type); (2) more data should not require more parameters; (3) parameter estimation should behave nicely. The exponential family hits all three.
  - **Three named distributions first** (Examples 6.8–6.10): **Bernoulli** $\mathrm{Ber}(\mu)$: $p(x\mid\mu)=\mu^x(1-\mu)^{1-x}$, $\mathbb E[x]=\mu$, $\mathbb V[x]=\mu(1-\mu)$ (Eqs. 6.92–6.94). **Binomial** $\mathrm{Bin}(N,\mu)$: $p(m\mid N,\mu)=\binom{N}{m}\mu^m(1-\mu)^{N-m}$, $\mathbb E[m]=N\mu$, $\mathbb V[m]=N\mu(1-\mu)$ (Eqs. 6.95–6.97). **Beta** $\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)$: $p(\mu\mid\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\mu^{\alpha-1}(1-\mu)^{\beta-1}$, $\mathbb E[\mu]=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}$ (Eqs. 6.98–6.101); $\alpha$ pushes mass toward 1, $\beta$ toward 0; special cases include $\alpha=\beta=1\Rightarrow\mathcal U[0,1]$.
  - **[[ConjugatePrior|Conjugacy]]** (§6.6.1, Def. 6.13, p. 208): a prior is *conjugate* to a likelihood if the posterior is of the same family as the prior, so updating is "just" updating parameters. Example 6.11 (Beta–Binomial) and Example 6.12 (Beta–Bernoulli) show the Beta is conjugate to the Bernoulli/Binomial parameter $\mu$, with posterior $\mathrm{Beta}(h+\alpha, N-h+\beta)$ (Eq. 6.104d). **Table 6.2** lists conjugate pairs (Bernoulli→Beta; Binomial→Beta; Gaussian→Gaussian/inverse-Gamma; Gaussian→Gaussian/inverse-Wishart; Multinomial→Dirichlet). Univariate Gaussian variance ↔ inverse-Gamma; multivariate covariance/precision matrix ↔ inverse-Wishart.
  - **[[SufficientStatistics|Sufficient statistics]]** (§6.6.2, p. 210). A statistic = a deterministic function of a RV. Sir Ronald Fisher's idea: sufficient statistics $\phi(x)$ "contain all the information that can be inferred from data about the population." **Fisher–Neyman theorem** (Theorem 6.14, Lehmann & Casella 1998): $\phi(x)$ is sufficient for $\theta$ iff $p(x\mid\theta)=h(x)\,g_\theta(\phi(x))$ (Eq. 6.106) — the density factors into a $\theta$-free part $h(x)$ and a part that depends on $\theta$ *only through* $\phi(x)$. As more data is collected, do we need more parameters? In general yes (studied in nonparametric statistics, Wasserman 2007) — *except* for exponential-family distributions, which have **finite-dimensional sufficient statistics**.
  - **[[ExponentialFamily|Exponential family]]** (§6.6.3, p. 210–214). Three levels of abstraction: a named distribution with fixed params; a fixed parametric form with params to be learned; a *family* of distributions. An exponential family has the form $p(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\theta)=h(\mathbf x)\exp(\langle\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)\rangle - A(\boldsymbol\theta))$ (Eq. 6.107), where $\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)$ = **sufficient statistics**, $\boldsymbol\theta$ = **[[NaturalParameters|natural parameters]]**, $A(\boldsymbol\theta)$ = log-partition (normalization) function; $h(\mathbf x)$ can be absorbed into the dot product. Stripped down: $p(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\theta)\propto\exp(\boldsymbol\theta^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x))$ (Eq. 6.108). The Gaussian (Example 6.13) is exponential-family with $\boldsymbol\phi(x)=[x, x^2]^\top$ and $\boldsymbol\theta=[\mu/\sigma^2, -1/(2\sigma^2)]^\top$ (Eqs. 6.109–6.111). The Bernoulli (Example 6.14) has natural parameter $\theta=\log\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}$, $\phi(x)=x$, $A(\theta)=\log(1+\exp\theta)$, $h(x)=1$ (Eqs. 6.113–6.117); inverting gives $\mu=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\theta)}$ — **the sigmoid / logistic function** (Eq. 6.118), the same nonlinearity used in logistic regression and neural-net activations. **Every exponential-family member has a conjugate prior** (Brown 1986; Eq. 6.120) whose sufficient statistics are $[\boldsymbol\theta, -A(\boldsymbol\theta)]^\top$; Example 6.15 *derives* the Beta as the canonical conjugate prior of the Bernoulli. Historical note: exponential families were discovered independently 1935–1936 by Pitman, Darmois, and Koopman — the only families with finite-dimensional sufficient statistics under repeated sampling. ML payoffs: finite-dimensional sufficient statistics, easy-to-write conjugates, MLE-of-sufficient-statistics is optimal, and a **concave log-likelihood** → efficient optimization (Ch 7).
- **Change of variables / inverse transform** (§6.7, p. 214–220). Since the set of *named* distributions is small, we need to know how *transformed* RVs are distributed (e.g. distribution of $X^2$ or $\frac12(X_1+X_2)$ for standard normals). **Discrete case** (§6.7, Eq. 6.125): for invertible $U$, $P(Y{=}y)=P(U(X){=}y)=P(X=U^{-1}(y))$ — transformations directly relabel events. **Two approaches for continuous**: (a) **distribution-function / cdf technique** (§6.7.1) — find $F_Y(y)=P(Y\le y)$ then differentiate $f(y)=\frac{d}{dy}F_Y(y)$ (Eqs. 6.126–6.127; Example 6.16 finds the pdf of $Y=X^2$ for $f(x)=3x^2$); a special case is the **probability integral transform** (Theorem 6.15, Casella & Berger 2002): if $X$ has a strictly monotonic cdf $F_X$, then $Y:=F_X(X)$ is *uniform* — the basis of inverse-cdf sampling, hypothesis testing, and copulas (Nelsen 2006). (b) **change-of-variables technique** (§6.7.2), built on the calculus substitution rule $\int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=\int f(u)\,du$ (Eq. 6.133, $u=g(x)$). For univariate invertible $U$: $f(y)=f_x(U^{-1}(y))\cdot\big|\frac{d}{dy}U^{-1}(y)\big|$ (Eq. 6.143) — the absolute value handles both increasing and decreasing $U$. The extra factor (vs. the discrete case) reflects that $P(Y{=}y)=0$ for continuous RVs (p. 219 Remark). **Multivariate** (Theorem 6.16, Billingsley 1995): $f(\mathbf y)=f_{\mathbf x}(U^{-1}(\mathbf y))\cdot\big|\det\big(\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf y}U^{-1}(\mathbf y)\big)\big|$ (Eq. 6.144) — the **absolute value of the [[Jacobian]] determinant** replaces $|dU^{-1}/dy|$, exactly the volume-scaling reading from §4.1 / §5.3. Example 6.17 transforms a standard bivariate Gaussian by $\mathbf y=\mathbf A\mathbf x$ and recovers covariance $\boldsymbol\Sigma=\mathbf A\mathbf A^\top$ (Eqs. 6.145–6.151).
- **Further reading** (§6.8, p. 221). The chapter is "rather terse." Gentler self-study: Grinstead & Snell (1997), Walpole et al. (2011). Philosophical: Hacking (2001). The measure-theoretic details that were avoided (a precise $p(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x)$ needs $\mathbb E_y[f(y)\mid\sigma(x)]$ conditioned on a $\sigma$-algebra) are in Billingsley (1995), Jacod & Protter (2004), Shiryayev (1984), etc. **Normalizing flows** (Jimenez Rezende & Mohamed 2015) "rel[y] on change of variables for transforming random variables" — the modern neural-network payoff of §6.7. Probabilistic-ML texts: MacKay (2003), Bishop (2006), Rasmussen & Williams (2006), Barber (2012), Murphy (2012).

> "The name 'random variable' is a great source of misunderstanding as it is neither random nor it is a variable. It is a function." — §6.1.2 marginal, p. 175

Cuts through the foundational naming confusion: a [[RandomVariable]] is the function $X:\Omega\to\mathcal{T}$.

> "Using this mathematical structure of probability, the goal is to perform automated reasoning, and in this sense, probability generalizes logical reasoning (Jaynes, 2003)." — §6.1, p. 172

The framing that probability *extends* Boolean logic — the [[CoxJaynesTheorem]] thesis.

> "For plausible reasoning it is necessary to extend the discrete true and false values of truth to continuous plausibilities." — Jaynes (2003), marginal, p. 173

Why $\{0,1\}$ truth values are not enough; plausibilities live in $[0,1]$.

> "The Cox–Jaynes theorem proves these plausibilities to be sufficient to define the universal mathematical rules that apply to plausibility $p$, up to transformation by an arbitrary monotonic function. Crucially, these rules are the rules of probability." — §6.1.1, p. 174

> "In machine learning and statistics, there are two major interpretations of probability: the Bayesian and frequentist interpretations (Bishop, 2006; Efron and Hastie, 2016). The Bayesian interpretation uses probability to specify the degree of uncertainty that the user has about an event… The frequentist interpretation considers the relative frequencies of events of interest to the total number of events that occurred." — §6.1, Remark, p. 174

The explicit Bayesian-vs-frequentist agnosticism.

> "Some machine learning texts on probabilistic models use lazy notation and jargon, which is confusing. This text is no exception. Multiple distinct concepts are all referred to as 'probability distribution', and the reader has to often disentangle the meaning from the context. One trick to help make sense of probability distributions is to check whether we are trying to model something categorical (a discrete random variable) or something continuous (a continuous random variable)." — §6.1.1, p. 174

The "abuse of notation" / lazy-notation admission, repeated and sharpened in §6.2.3 (the pmf $P(X{=}x)$, the pdf $p(x)$, and the cdf $P(X\le x)$ are all loosely called the "distribution").

> "We will be using the expression 'probability distribution' not only for discrete probability mass functions but also for continuous probability density functions, although this is technically incorrect. In line with most machine learning literature, we also rely on context to distinguish the different uses of the phrase probability distribution." — §6.2.3, Remark, p. 183

> "We think of the outcome $x$ as the argument that results in the probability $p(x)$." — §6.2.3 marginal, p. 183

> "The likelihood is sometimes also called the 'measurement model'." — §6.3 marginal, p. 185. And: "Note that the likelihood is not a distribution in $\mathbf x$, but only in $\mathbf y$. We call $p(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x)$ either the 'likelihood of $\mathbf x$ (given $\mathbf y$)' or the 'probability of $\mathbf y$ given $\mathbf x$' but never the likelihood of $\mathbf y$ (MacKay, 2003)." — §6.3, p. 185

The precise definition of *likelihood* and the prior/likelihood/posterior/evidence decomposition of [[BayesTheorem]].

> "The concept of the expected value is central to machine learning, and the foundational concepts of probability itself can be derived from the expected value (Whittle, 2000)." — §6.4.1, p. 187

> "The last point may not hold in converse, i.e., two random variables can have covariance zero but are not statistically independent. To understand why, recall that covariance measures only linear dependence. Therefore, random variables that are nonlinearly dependent could have covariance zero." — §6.4.5, p. 194

The zero-covariance ≠ independence caveat (Example 6.5).

> "Random variables can be considered vectors in a vector space, and we can define inner products to obtain geometric properties of random variables (Eaton, 2007)." — §6.4.6, p. 196

The inner-product-space view: correlation = $\cos\theta$, $\sigma$ = length, uncorrelated = orthogonal, Pythagoras for variances.

> "'Computers' used to be a job description." — §6.6 marginal, p. 205

## Contradictions / notational quirks

- **Self-admitted "lazy notation."** The book repeatedly flags (§6.1.1, §6.2.3) that "probability distribution" is overloaded across the pmf $P(X{=}x)$, the pdf $p(x)$, and the cdf $P(X\le x)$, and that ML literature hides the distinction between $\Omega$, $\mathcal{T}$, and $X$. It uses $X$ for *both* univariate and multivariate RVs, denoting states $x$ (scalar) vs $\mathbf x$ (vector). The "abuse of notation" on $p(x)$ is explicit, not a contradiction — but cross-referencing other corpora (e.g. [[d2l-preliminaries]], which uses $\mathcal{S}$ for the sample space and $f(x)/F(x)$ for pdf/cdf) requires translation.
- **Sample-space naming drift.** $\Omega$ is variously "sample space," "state space" (Jacod & Protter 2004 — but "state space" is also used for dynamical systems, Hasselblatt & Katok 2003), "sample description space," "possibility space," or even "event space" across books (§6.1.2 Remark, p. 175–176). MML standardizes on *sample space* $\Omega$ + *target space* $\mathcal{T}$ (states).
- **Biased empirical covariance by default.** MML divides the empirical covariance by $N$ (the biased MLE, Eq. 6.42), not $N-1$ (the unbiased / Bessel-corrected estimator), and says so explicitly (margin, p. 192). Cross-references to statistics texts and to [[DataCovarianceMatrix]] must note this. This is consistent with the existing [[DataCovarianceMatrix]] page.
- **Covariance ↔ cross-covariance terminology.** The multivariate $\mathrm{Cov}[\mathbf x,\mathbf y]$ is "sometimes referred to as cross-covariance, with covariance referring to $\mathrm{Cov}[\mathbf x,\mathbf x]$" (margin, p. 190) — a genuine terminology fork to be aware of.
- **Zero covariance ≠ independence** (Example 6.5, §6.4.5) — a common student error the book pre-empts; the converse (independence ⇒ zero covariance) *does* hold. Covariance captures only *linear* dependence.
- **Subscript suppression on expectation.** $\mathbb E_X[x]$ is "often written" $\mathbb E[x]$ when the RV is clear from context (margin, p. 190) — and the covariance subscript is similarly dropped. Not a contradiction, but a notational convenience to track.
- **Bayesian/frequentist agnosticism** (§6.1 Remark) is consistent with [[probability]] and [[ProbabilitySpace]] already in the wiki, and with [[d2l-preliminaries]]; no conflict.
- **No contradictions with prior wiki content** were found. The chapter *confirms and extends* the existing pages; all enrichments are additive.
