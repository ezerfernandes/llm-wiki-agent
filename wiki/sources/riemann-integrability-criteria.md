## Summary
The Riemann integral is built to measure the net area under a bounded [[Functions|function]] on a [[intervals|closed interval]] by approximating it with rectangles. The subtle point is not computing the integral once it exists, but deciding when the [[Limits|limiting]] process is well-defined. This page collects the most useful criteria for Riemann integrability, in a form that is easy to apply when you meet a function that is not obviously [[ContinuousFunctions|continuous]].

## Key Claims
- **Partitions, upper sums, lower sums** — Let \\( f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R} \\) be bounded.
- **The Darboux criterion** — Before stating the criterion, it helps to fix two numbers that summarize all possible upper and lower sums at once.
- **Common sufficient conditions** — The Darboux criterion is the foundation, but in practice most functions you encounter fall into one of three categories that guarantee integrability without any direct computation of sums.
- **The discontinuity-set criterion** — A bounded function \\( f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R} \\) is Riemann integrable if and only if its set of discontinuities has Lebesgue measure zero.
