## Summary
Kaplan, McCandlish et al. (OpenAI + Johns Hopkins) study empirical scaling laws for autoregressive Transformer language models on the cross-entropy loss. Across more than seven orders of magnitude in compute, six in model size, and two in dataset size, the test loss obeys clean **power laws** in three scale factors — non-embedding parameters N, dataset size D in tokens, and training compute C — when each is varied while the other two are not bottlenecks. Architecture details such as depth/width ratio, attention-head count, or feed-forward ratio matter only weakly. From these laws the authors derive an **optimal allocation of a fixed compute budget**: train **very large models** on **a relatively modest amount of data** and **stop significantly before convergence** — the opposite of the prevailing practice of training small models to convergence. Larger models are also more *sample-efficient*. This paper is the foundational quantitative argument for the scale-up trajectory that produced GPT-3 and successor LLMs.

## Key Claims

- **Three smooth power laws.** When not bottlenecked by the other two factors:
  - $L(N) = (N_c/N)^{\alpha_N}$ with $\alpha_N \approx 0.076$, $N_c \approx 8.8 \times 10^{13}$ non-embedding params.
  - $L(D) = (D_c/D)^{\alpha_D}$ with $\alpha_D \approx 0.095$, $D_c \approx 5.4 \times 10^{13}$ tokens.
  - $L(C_\min) = (C_c^\min/C_\min)^{\alpha_C^\min}$ with $\alpha_C^\min \approx 0.050$, $C_c^\min \approx 3.1 \times 10^8$ PF-days.
  - Trends show no deviation at the upper end across the studied range.
- **Performance depends strongly on scale, weakly on shape.** Holding total non-embedding parameter count $N$ fixed, varying depth-vs-width, feed-forward ratio, and number of attention heads moves loss by only a few percent. A `(n_layer, d_model) = (6, 4288)` Transformer reaches within 3% of the loss of the `(48, 1600)` model used in GPT-2 ([RWC+19]).
- **Joint $L(N, D)$ formula.**
  $$L(N, D) = \big[ (N_c/N)^{\alpha_N/\alpha_D} + D_c/D \big]^{\alpha_D}$$
  satisfies the limit conditions and predicts overfitting from the ratio $N^{0.74}/D$ — an 8× model needs only ~5× the data to keep the overfitting penalty constant.
- **Joint $L(N, S)$ formula** for finite training steps S in the infinite-data limit:
  $$L(N, S) = (N_c/N)^{\alpha_N} + (S_c / S_\min(S))^{\alpha_S}$$
  with $\alpha_S \approx 0.76$, $S_c \approx 2.1 \times 10^3$. Power-law learning curves are roughly *universal* across model sizes.
- **Critical batch size.** The critical batch size for data-parallel training follows its own power law in the loss: $B_\mathrm{crit}(L) \approx B_*/L^{1/\alpha_B}$ with $B_* \approx 2 \times 10^8$ tokens and $\alpha_B \approx 0.21$. It is **independent of model size** — depends only on the achieved loss — and approximately matches the gradient noise scale of [MKAT18]. At convergence the critical batch size for the largest models is ~1–2M tokens.
- **Optimal compute allocation.** Within a fixed compute budget $C$, the optimal allocation scales as
  $$N \propto C^{0.73}, \quad B \propto C^{0.24}, \quad S \propto C^{0.03}, \quad D = B \cdot S.$$
  Most additional compute should go into **larger models**, with batch size scaled up via critical batch size and the *number of serial training steps barely increasing*. A 10× compute increase calls for ~5× model size and ~2× data; a 1000× compute increase still implies <10× more serial steps.
- **Convergence is inefficient.** Compute-efficient training stops *significantly before* convergence. Data requirements grow very slowly, $D \sim C^{0.27}$, so optimal-compute training is far more sample-efficient than training small models to convergence.
- **Sample efficiency rises with model size.** Larger models reach a given loss using fewer optimizer steps and fewer data points.
- **Transfer adds a constant offset.** When models trained on WebText2 are evaluated on Books, Wikipedia, Common Crawl, or Internet Books, the loss correlates linearly with WebText2 validation loss with a roughly constant offset; transfer to a new distribution costs a fixed penalty but otherwise tracks training performance — and depends only on training-distribution loss, not on the duration of training.
- **Transformers asymptotically dominate LSTMs.** With matched non-embedding parameter counts, LSTMs match Transformers on early tokens of a context but plateau after ~100 tokens, whereas Transformers keep improving across the full 1024-token context. Per-token loss obeys a power law in context position with larger exponents for larger models.
- **Embedding parameters obscure the trend.** Trends are clean only when N excludes vocabulary and positional embedding matrices; the embedding matrix can be shrunk substantially with little performance loss ([LCG+19]).
- **Scaling laws must eventually break.** Extrapolating $L(C_\min)$ and $L(D \mid C_\min)$ produces a contradiction at $C^* \sim 10^4$ PF-days, $N^* \sim 10^{12}$ params, $D^* \sim 10^{12}$ tokens, $L^* \sim 1.7$ nats/token. The authors conjecture this intersection may estimate the **entropy of natural language** and the point at which Transformer LMs approach maximum performance — many orders of magnitude beyond what they trained.

| $S_\mathrm{min}(C_\min)$ | $p_S = 0.03$ | $S_e = 5.4 \times 10^3$ steps |
| $D_\mathrm{opt}(C_\min)$ | $p_D = 0.27$ | $D_e = 2 \times 10^{10}$ tokens |

The optimal allocation also satisfies $\alpha_C^\min = 1/(1/\alpha_S + 1/\alpha_B + 1/\alpha_N) \approx 0.054$, consistent with the empirical $\alpha_C^\min \approx 0.050$.

## Key Quotes

> "Performance depends strongly on scale, weakly on model shape. Model performance depends most strongly on scale, which consists of three factors: the number of model parameters N (excluding embeddings), the size of the dataset D, and the amount of compute C used for training. Within reasonable limits, performance depends very weakly on other architectural hyperparameters such as depth vs. width."

> "Larger models are significantly more sample-efficient, such that optimally compute-efficient training involves training very large models on a relatively modest amount of data and stopping significantly before convergence."

> "These results strongly suggest that larger language models will continue to perform better, and will also be much more sample efficient than has been previously appreciated. Big models may be more important than big data."

> "One might interpret these relations as analogues to the ideal gas law, which relates the macroscopic properties of a gas in a universal way, independent of most of the details of its microscopic constituents."

## Methodology Notes
- Decoder-only Transformers ([[1706.03762-attention-is-all-you-need]], LSP+18) trained on **WebText2** (extended WebText: 20.3M documents, 96 GB text, $2.29 \times 10^{10}$ training tokens; $6.6 \times 10^8$ held-out tokens). Tokenizer: byte-pair encoding, vocab 50257.
- N denotes **non-embedding** parameter count: $N \approx 2 d_\mathrm{model} n_\mathrm{layer} (2 d_\mathrm{attn} + d_\mathrm{ff}) \approx 12 n_\mathrm{layer} d_\mathrm{model}^2$ with $d_\mathrm{attn} = d_\mathrm{ff}/4 = d_\mathrm{model}$.
- Compute estimate: $C \approx 6 N B S$ floating-point operations per token (forward+backward). One PF-day = $8.64 \times 10^{19}$ FLOPs.
- Default context length 1024 tokens; default batch size $2^{19}$ tokens.
- Adam optimizer, $2.5 \times 10^5$ steps, 3000-step linear warmup + cosine decay to zero. Adafactor for models >1B params.
- Model size range: 768 → 1.5B non-embedding params. Dataset size range: 22M → 23B tokens.

## Contradictions
- **No contradictions with existing wiki sources.** The paper *predates* every 2026 LLM paper in the corpus and supplies the quantitative justification their training budgets implicitly assume.
- **An internal contradiction is acknowledged** within the paper itself (Section 6.3): naïve extrapolation of $L(C_\min)$ and the data-bound $L(D)$ predicts an eventual crossover where $L(D(C_\min)) < L(C_\min)$, implying compute-efficient training will exhaust the data-overfitting frontier. The authors conjecture the crossover ($C^* \sim 10^4$ PF-days, $L^* \sim 1.7$ nats/token) marks where the scaling laws themselves break down.

## Caveats
- Power-law fits are clean over the range studied but **must eventually break** before reaching the entropy of natural language ($L > 0$).
- Constants $N_c, D_c, C_c$ depend on the tokenizer and vocabulary; they have **no fundamental meaning**, only the exponents do.
- Architecture independence holds only within the studied range (1–207 layers, aspect ratio variation up to ~40×). 1-layer models and extreme depth-to-width ratios deviate.
- Generalization claim relies on the four held-out distributions (Books, Wikipedia, Common Crawl, Internet Books) which all overlap with WebText2 in genre. Behavior on out-of-distribution text (code, math, multilingual) is not measured here.
- The Chinchilla-style claim that **data should scale faster** than N suggests this paper's optimal allocation may itself be re-revised; that revision happens in later work and is not present in this 2020 paper.
