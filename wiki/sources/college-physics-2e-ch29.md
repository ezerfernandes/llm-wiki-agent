## Summary
OpenStax College Physics 2e Chapter 29 introduces quantum physics through the historical sequence that broke classical theory: Planck's quantization of blackbody-oscillator energy, Einstein's photon explanation of the photoelectric effect, the photon energy/momentum (Compton) relations, and the symmetric extension to matter via de Broglie wavelengths confirmed by Davisson-Germer. It culminates in particle-wave duality as a universal property of all particles and in the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, which replaces deterministic orbits with probability distributions. The unifying thread is **quantization** — energy, charge, and momentum come in discrete packets — with the correspondence principle explaining why classical physics still works macroscopically.

## Key Claims
- Energy is quantized: oscillating atoms emit/absorb radiation only in discrete steps E = (n + ½)hf, which Planck (c. 1900) used to resolve blackbody radiation and the ultraviolet catastrophe.
- A photon is a discrete quantum of EM radiation with energy E = hf = hc/λ; light intensity is the number of photons, not the energy per photon.
- The photoelectric effect requires a threshold frequency and obeys KE_e = hf − BE; Einstein (1905) explained it by treating EM radiation itself as quantized.
- Photon energy scales with frequency across the EM spectrum (handy: hc = 1240 eV·nm), determining whether radiation can ionize, break bonds, or merely heat.
- Photons carry momentum p = h/λ = E/c despite being massless; the Compton effect (Compton, ~1923) confirmed this through X-ray energy loss on scattering.
- Particle-wave duality: EM radiation shows wave (interference, diffraction) and particle (discrete energy/momentum) behavior, but only one in any single observation.
- Matter has wave character too: de Broglie (1923) proposed λ = h/p; Davisson-Germer (1925–26) confirmed it via electron diffraction obeying the Bragg condition nλ = 2d sin θ.
- The Heisenberg uncertainty principle sets Δx·Δp ≥ h/(4π) and ΔE·Δt ≥ h/(4π); localizing an electron to atomic dimensions forces a velocity spread too large for classical orbits.
- Quantum positions are probabilistic — repeated measurements form diffraction-like distributions — which underlies atomic orbitals replacing definite electron paths.
- Duality is universal: all particles, massless or massive, have wave properties; observability is mass- and scale-dependent (correspondence principle).

## Key Quotes (paraphrased)
> Any single quantum-level observation yields either particle-like or wave-like properties, never both at once. — §29.5
> Measurement unavoidably disturbs the system: a probe used to pin down position transfers momentum unpredictably. — §29.7
> By nature's symmetry, if radiation has both particle and wave character, matter should too. — §29.6
