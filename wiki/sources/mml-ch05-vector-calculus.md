## Summary

Chapter 5 of [[mml-book|Mathematics for Machine Learning]] (Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong; book pp. 139–171) is the **gradient-machinery chapter**: it builds, layer by layer, the differential-calculus tooling that every gradient-based ML optimizer consumes. The chapter opens (p. 139) by motivating differentiation through three ML problems — (i) linear regression curve-fitting (Ch 9), (ii) neural-network auto-encoders trained by *"repeated application of the chain rule,"* and (iii) Gaussian mixture models (Ch 11) — all solved by *"optimization algorithms that exploit gradient information"* (§7.1). It then proceeds: §5.1 revisits univariate differentiation (the [[DifferenceQuotient|difference quotient]] → [[derivatives|derivative]] as its limit; [[TaylorSeries|Taylor series]] / [[TaylorPolynomial|Taylor polynomial]] / Maclaurin series; the product/quotient/sum/[[ChainRule|chain]] [[DifferentiationRules|differentiation rules]]); §5.2 generalizes to [[PartialDerivative|partial derivatives]] collected into the **[[Gradient|gradient as a row vector]]** (Eq. 5.40, the deliberate row-vector convention) and gives the multivariate [[ChainRule|chain rule]]; §5.3 extends to vector-valued functions $\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ whose gradient is the $m\times n$ **[[Jacobian]]** matrix (Def. 5.6, numerator layout), tying the [[Jacobian]] determinant to change-of-variables and volume scaling; §5.4 takes gradients of matrices, producing higher-order [[Tensor|tensors]] (with a "flatten-to-vector" shortcut); §5.5 tabulates the [[UsefulGradientIdentities|useful gradient identities]] (from Petersen & Pedersen 2012); §5.6 presents [[Backpropagation]] as a special case of [[AutomaticDifferentiation|automatic differentiation]], distinguishing [[ReverseModeAutodiff|reverse mode]] (= backprop, cheap when inputs ≫ outputs) from [[ForwardModeAutodiff|forward mode]] over a [[ComputationalGraph|computation graph]] of intermediate variables; §5.7 introduces higher-order derivatives and the symmetric **[[Hessian]]** (Eq. 5.147); §5.8 gives [[Linearization]] (first-order Taylor, Eq. 5.148) and the full **multivariate Taylor series** (Def. 5.7, Eq. 5.151) built from $k$-fold outer-product tensors $D_\mathbf{x}^k f(\mathbf{x}_0)\boldsymbol\delta^k$; §5.9 (further reading) connects Taylor linearization to the extended Kalman filter, the unscented transform, and the [[LaplaceApproximation|Laplace approximation]]. The chapter mind map (Fig. 5.2, p. 140): Difference quotient *defines* Partial derivatives, which are *collected in* the Jacobian/Hessian and *used in* Taylor series — and which feed Ch 6 (Probability), Ch 7 (Optimization), Ch 9 (Regression), Ch 10 (Dim-reduction), Ch 11 (Density estimation), Ch 12 (Classification).

## Key Claims

### 5.0 Opening / mind map (pp. 139–140)

- **Vector calculus exists to compute gradients for optimization** (p. 139): *"Many algorithms in machine learning optimize an objective function with respect to a set of desired model parameters... we typically solve by using optimization algorithms that exploit gradient information (Section 7.1)."* Three motivating problems: (i) linear regression / curve fitting (Ch 9, maximize likelihood over weights); (ii) **neural-network auto-encoders** for dimensionality reduction / data compression, where parameters are layer weights and biases and *"we minimize a reconstruction error by repeated application of the chain rule"*; (iii) Gaussian mixture models (Ch 11). See [[VectorCalculus]].
- **A function $f$ relates two quantities** (p. 139): inputs $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^D$ (the *domain*) to real-valued function values $f(\mathbf{x})$ (the *image/codomain*), written $f:\mathbb{R}^D\to\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbf{x}\mapsto f(\mathbf{x})$ (Eqs. 5.1a–5.1b). Example 5.1 (p. 140): the dot product $f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x}$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ is $f:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbf{x}\mapsto x_1^2+x_2^2$ (Eqs. 5.2a–5.2b).
- **The gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent** (p. 140): *"the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent."* Vector calculus is *"one of the fundamental mathematical tools we need in machine learning,"* with functions assumed differentiable throughout (sub-differentials for non-differentiable points are mentioned but not covered; constrained extensions deferred to Ch 7).
- **Mind map (Fig. 5.2, p. 140):** [[DifferenceQuotient|Difference quotient]] —*defines*→ [[PartialDerivative|Partial derivatives]] —*collected in*→ [[Jacobian]]/[[Hessian]] —*used in*→ [[TaylorSeries|Taylor series]]; Partial derivatives are *used in* Ch 7 (Optimization), Ch 9 (Regression), Ch 10 (Dim-reduction), Ch 11 (Density estimation), Ch 12 (Classification); Jacobian/Hessian are *used in* Ch 6 (Probability).

### 5.1 Differentiation of Univariate Functions (pp. 141–146)

- **Definition 5.1 ([[DifferenceQuotient|Difference Quotient]])** (Eq. 5.3, p. 141, marginal "difference quotient"): for $y=f(x)$, $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$, the *difference quotient* $\dfrac{\delta y}{\delta x}:=\dfrac{f(x+\delta x)-f(x)}{\delta x}$ computes the slope of the **secant line** through the two points $(x_0,f(x_0))$ and $(x_0+\delta x, f(x_0+\delta x))$ on the graph of $f$ (Fig. 5.3). It can also be read as the *average slope* of $f$ over $[x, x+\delta x]$ if $f$ is assumed linear. In the limit $\delta x\to 0$ the secant becomes the tangent — *"The tangent is then the derivative of $f$ at $x$"* (if $f$ is differentiable).
- **Definition 5.2 ([[derivatives|Derivative]])** (Eq. 5.4, p. 141, marginal "derivative"): for $h>0$, $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}x}:=\lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$ — the secant in Fig. 5.3 becomes a tangent. *"The derivative of $f$ points in the direction of steepest ascent of $f$."*
- **Example 5.2 (Derivative of a Polynomial via the limit)** (Eqs. 5.5a–5.6d, p. 142): derives $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}x^n=nx^{n-1}$ ($n\in\mathbb{N}$) directly from the difference-quotient definition, expanding $(x+h)^n=\sum_{i=0}^n\binom{n}{i}x^{n-i}h^i$ via the binomial theorem; the $x^n=\binom{n}{0}x^{n-0}h^0$ term cancels, and all terms with $i\geq 2$ vanish as $h\to 0$, leaving $\binom{n}{1}x^{n-1}=\frac{n!}{1!(n-1)!}x^{n-1}=nx^{n-1}$.
- **Definition 5.3 ([[TaylorPolynomial|Taylor Polynomial]])** (Eq. 5.7, p. 142, marginals "Taylor polynomial", "$t^0:=1$ for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$"): the *Taylor polynomial* of degree $n$ of $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ at $x_0$ is $T_n(x):=\sum_{k=0}^n\dfrac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x-x_0)^k$, where $f^{(k)}(x_0)$ is the $k$th derivative of $f$ at $x_0$ (assumed to exist) and $\frac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}$ are the polynomial coefficients.
- **Definition 5.4 ([[TaylorSeries|Taylor Series]])** (Eq. 5.8, p. 143, marginals "Taylor series", "$f\in\mathcal{C}^\infty$ means $f$ is continuously infinitely many times differentiable", "Maclaurin series", "analytic"): for a smooth $f\in\mathcal{C}^\infty$, $T_\infty(x)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\dfrac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x-x_0)^k$. For $x_0=0$ this is the **Maclaurin series**. If $f=T_\infty$, $f$ is called **analytic**.
- **Remark — polynomials are exact Taylor** (p. 143): in general a degree-$n$ Taylor polynomial is an *approximation* of a function (which need not be a polynomial), accurate in a neighborhood of $x_0$. *However*, a degree-$n$ Taylor polynomial is an **exact** representation of a polynomial $f$ of degree $k\leq n$ since all derivatives $f^{(i)}$, $i>k$, vanish.
- **Example 5.3 (Taylor Polynomial)** (Eqs. 5.9–5.18b, p. 143): for $f(x)=x^4$ the degree-6 Taylor polynomial at $x_0=1$ (coefficients $f(1)=1$, $f'(1)=4$, $f''(1)=12$, $f^{(3)}(1)=24$, $f^{(4)}(1)=24$, $f^{(5)}=f^{(6)}=0$) re-expands to $T_6(x)=1+4(x-1)+6(x-1)^2+4(x-1)^3+(x-1)^4 = x^4 = f(x)$ — an *exact* representation.
- **Example 5.4 (Taylor Series of $\sin x+\cos x$)** (Eqs. 5.19–5.27, pp. 144–145): the Maclaurin series of $f(x)=\sin(x)+\cos(x)\in\mathcal{C}^\infty$ regroups into $\cos(x)+\sin(x)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty(-1)^k\frac{1}{(2k)!}x^{2k}+\sum_{k=0}^\infty(-1)^k\frac{1}{(2k+1)!}x^{2k+1}$, recovering the **power-series representations** $\cos(x)=\sum(-1)^k\frac{x^{2k}}{(2k)!}$ (Eq. 5.26) and $\sin(x)=\sum(-1)^k\frac{x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}$ (Eq. 5.27). Fig. 5.4 shows $T_0,T_1,T_5,T_{10}$: *"Higher-order Taylor polynomials approximate the function $f$ better and more globally; $T_{10}$ is already similar to $f$ in $[-4,4]$."*
- **Remark — Taylor is a special power series** (Eq. 5.28, p. 145): a general power series $f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k(x-c)^k$ reduces to the Taylor series of Def. 5.4 for the special coefficient form.

#### 5.1.2 Differentiation Rules (pp. 145–146)

- **The four [[DifferentiationRules|differentiation rules]]** (Eqs. 5.29–5.32, p. 145; denoting $f'$ for $\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}x}$): **Product rule** $(f(x)g(x))'=f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x)$; **Quotient rule** $\left(\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right)'=\dfrac{f'(x)g(x)-f(x)g'(x)}{(g(x))^2}$; **Sum rule** $(f(x)+g(x))'=f'(x)+g'(x)$; **[[ChainRule|Chain rule]]** $(g(f(x)))'=(g\circ f)'(x)=g'(f(x))f'(x)$, where $g\circ f$ denotes the composition $x\mapsto f(x)\mapsto g(x)$.
- **Example 5.5 (Chain Rule)** (Eqs. 5.33–5.38, pp. 145–146): for $h(x)=(2x+1)^4=g(f(x))$ with $f(x)=2x+1$, $g(f)=f^4$, the chain rule gives $h'(x)=g'(f)f'(x)=(4f^3)\cdot 2=4(2x+1)^3\cdot 2=8(2x+1)^3$.

### 5.2 Partial Differentiation and Gradients (pp. 146–149)

- **The gradient generalizes the derivative to several variables** (p. 146): for $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ we find the gradient *"by varying one variable at a time and keeping the others constant. The gradient is then the collection of these partial derivatives."*
- **Definition 5.5 ([[PartialDerivative|Partial Derivative]])** (Eq. 5.39, p. 146, marginal "partial derivative"): for $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbf{x}\mapsto f(\mathbf{x})$ of $n$ variables, $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}=\lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac{f(x_1+h,x_2,\dots,x_n)-f(\mathbf{x})}{h}$, …, $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}=\lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac{f(x_1,\dots,x_{n-1},x_n+h)-f(\mathbf{x})}{h}$. Marginal note: *"We can use results from scalar differentiation: Each partial derivative is a derivative with respect to a scalar."*
- **The gradient is a ROW vector** (Eq. 5.40, p. 146, marginals "gradient", "Jacobian"): collecting the partials gives $\nabla_\mathbf{x} f=\operatorname{grad} f=\dfrac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}=\left[\dfrac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_1}\;\dfrac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_2}\;\cdots\;\dfrac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_n}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}$, where $n$ is the number of variables and $1$ the dimension of the image; the input $\mathbf{x}=[x_1,\dots,x_n]^\top\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is a *column* vector. **The row vector in (5.40) is called the gradient of $f$ or the Jacobian** and is the generalization of the §5.1 derivative. *Remark*: this Jacobian definition is a special case of the general vector-valued Jacobian (§5.3). See [[Gradient]], [[Jacobian]].
- **Example 5.6 (Partial Derivatives Using the Chain Rule)** (Eqs. 5.41–5.42, pp. 146–147): for $f(x,y)=(x+2y^3)^2$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=2(x+2y^3)$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=2(x+2y^3)\cdot 6y^2=12(x+2y^3)y^2$, using the (scalar) chain rule (5.32).
- **Remark (Gradient as a Row Vector)** (p. 147): *"It is not uncommon in the literature to define the gradient vector as a column vector, following the convention that vectors are generally column vectors. The reason why we define the gradient vector as a row vector is twofold: First, we can consistently generalize the gradient to vector-valued functions $\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ (then the gradient becomes a matrix). Second, we can immediately apply the multi-variate chain rule without paying attention to the dimension of the gradient."* — The deliberate justification for the [[Gradient|row-vector convention]] (see Contradictions).
- **Example 5.7 (Gradient)** (Eqs. 5.43–5.45, p. 147): for $f(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2 x_2+x_1 x_2^3$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}=2x_1 x_2+x_2^3$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}=x_1^2+3x_1 x_2^2$, so $\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}=[2x_1 x_2+x_2^3\quad x_1^2+3x_1 x_2^2]\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times 2}$.

#### 5.2.1 Basic Rules of Partial Differentiation (pp. 147–148)

- **Vector/matrix differentiation rules — order matters** (p. 147): the school sum/product/chain rules still apply, *"However, when we compute derivatives with respect to vectors $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ we need to pay attention: Our gradients now involve vectors and matrices, and matrix multiplication is not commutative (Section 2.2.1), i.e., the order matters."* General **Product rule** (Eq. 5.46): $\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{x}}(f(\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{x}))=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{x}}g(\mathbf{x})+f(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial g}{\partial\mathbf{x}}$; **Sum rule** (Eq. 5.47): $\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{x}}(f(\mathbf{x})+g(\mathbf{x}))=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{x}}+\frac{\partial g}{\partial\mathbf{x}}$; **Chain rule** (Eq. 5.48): $\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{x}}(g\circ f)(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{x}}(g(f(\mathbf{x})))=\frac{\partial g}{\partial f}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{x}}$.
- **The chain rule resembles matrix-multiplication dimension matching** (p. 148, marginal "This is only an intuition, but not mathematically correct since the partial derivative is not a fraction."): reading (5.48) left to right, $\partial f$ shows up in the "denominator" of the first factor and the "numerator" of the second; the dimensions of $\partial f$ match so it "cancels," leaving $\partial g/\partial\mathbf{x}$ — mirroring the rule that neighboring dimensions must match for matrix multiplication (§2.2.1).

#### 5.2.2 Chain Rule (pp. 148–149)

- **Multivariate chain rule, single outer parameter** (Eq. 5.49, p. 148): for $f:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}$ of $x_1(t),x_2(t)$, $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}t}=\left[\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}\;\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}\right]\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial x_1(t)}{\partial t}\\\frac{\partial x_2(t)}{\partial t}\end{bmatrix}=\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}\dfrac{\partial x_1}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}\dfrac{\partial x_2}{\partial t}$ (where $\mathrm{d}$ denotes the gradient and $\partial$ partial derivatives).
- **Example 5.8** (Eqs. 5.50a–5.50c, p. 148): for $f(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2+2x_2$ with $x_1=\sin t$, $x_2=\cos t$, $\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}t}=2\sin t\cos t-2\sin t=2\sin t(\cos t-1)$.
- **Multivariate chain rule, two outer parameters** (Eqs. 5.51–5.53, pp. 148–149): for $x_1(s,t),x_2(s,t)$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial s}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}\frac{\partial x_1}{\partial s}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}\frac{\partial x_2}{\partial s}$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}\frac{\partial x_1}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}\frac{\partial x_2}{\partial t}$, expressed as the **matrix multiplication** $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}(s,t)}=\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{x}}{\partial(s,t)}=\underbrace{\left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}\;\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}\right]}_{=\partial f/\partial\mathbf{x}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial x_1}{\partial s}&\frac{\partial x_1}{\partial t}\\\frac{\partial x_2}{\partial s}&\frac{\partial x_2}{\partial t}\end{bmatrix}}_{=\partial\mathbf{x}/\partial(s,t)}$ (Eq. 5.53).
- **Why the gradient is a row vector — payoff** (p. 149, marginal "The chain rule can be written as a matrix multiplication."): *"This compact way of writing the chain rule as a matrix multiplication only makes sense if the gradient is defined as a row vector. Otherwise, we will need to start transposing gradients for the matrix dimensions to match. This may still be straightforward as long as the gradient is a vector or a matrix; however, when the gradient becomes a tensor... the transpose is no longer a triviality."*
- **Remark (Gradient checking via finite differences)** (p. 149, marginal "Gradient checking"): the difference-quotient definition (5.39) lets us numerically verify gradient implementations — pick small $h$ (e.g. $h=10^{-4}$), compare the finite-difference approximation against the analytic gradient; if $\sqrt{\frac{\sum_i(dh_i-df_i)^2}{\sum_i(dh_i+df_i)^2}}<10^{-6}$ the implementation is probably correct.

### 5.3 Gradients of Vector-Valued Functions (pp. 149–153)

- **Generalize to vector fields** (p. 149): for $\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ ($n\geq 1$, $m>1$), $\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=[f_1(\mathbf{x}),\dots,f_m(\mathbf{x})]^\top\in\mathbb{R}^m$ (Eq. 5.54) is viewed as a stack of scalar functions $f_i:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$, each differentiated by the §5.2 rules.
- **Partial derivative of a vector-valued function is a column vector** (Eq. 5.55, p. 150): $\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial x_i}=\left[\frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_i}\;\cdots\;\frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_i}\right]^\top\in\mathbb{R}^m$ (each entry a difference-quotient limit). Collecting all $n$ such column vectors as columns gives the gradient $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}=\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial x_1}\;\cdots\;\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial x_n}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ (Eqs. 5.56a–5.56b).
- **Definition 5.6 ([[Jacobian]])** (Eqs. 5.57–5.59, p. 150, marginal "The gradient of a function $\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ is a matrix of size $m\times n$"): the *Jacobian* $\mathbf{J}$ is the $m\times n$ matrix of all first-order partial derivatives of $\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$, $\mathbf{J}=\nabla_\mathbf{x}\mathbf{f}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1}&\cdots&\frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n}\\\vdots&&\vdots\\\frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1}&\cdots&\frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n}\end{bmatrix}$, with entry $J(i,j)=\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}$. The scalar case $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^1$ (e.g. $f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_i x_i$) has a Jacobian that is a **row vector of dimension $1\times n$** — recovering (5.40).
- **Remark — numerator layout** (pp. 150–151, marginals "numerator layout", "denominator layout"): *"In this book, we use the numerator layout of the derivative, i.e., the derivative $\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}/\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}$ of $\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^m$ with respect to $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is an $m\times n$ matrix, where the elements of $\mathbf{f}$ define the rows and the elements of $\mathbf{x}$ define the columns. There exists also the denominator layout, which is the transpose of the numerator layout."*
- **Jacobian determinant = local volume-scaling factor** (Eqs. 5.60–5.66, pp. 151–152, Figs. 5.5–5.6, marginals "The determinant of the Jacobian of $\mathbf{f}$ can be used to compute the magnifier between the blue and orange area", "Geometrically, the Jacobian determinant gives the magnification/scaling factor when we transform an area or volume", "Jacobian determinant"): the unit square $(\mathbf{b}_1,\mathbf{b}_2)$ has area $|\det[\mathbf{b}_1\,\mathbf{b}_2]|=1$ (Eq. 5.60); a parallelogram $(\mathbf{c}_1,\mathbf{c}_2)$ with $\mathbf{c}_1=[-2,1]^\top$, $\mathbf{c}_2=[1,1]^\top$ has area $|\det\begin{bmatrix}-2&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}|=|-3|=3$ (Eq. 5.61) — *three times* the unit square. **Approach 1** (linear algebra): the basis-change matrix is $\mathbf{J}=\begin{bmatrix}-2&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$ with $|\det\mathbf{J}|=3$ (Eq. 5.62). **Approach 2** (partial derivatives): treating $\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2$ as a variable transformation $y_1=-2x_1+x_2$, $y_2=x_1+x_2$ (Eqs. 5.63–5.64) recovers the same Jacobian $\mathbf{J}=\begin{bmatrix}\partial y_1/\partial x_1&\partial y_1/\partial x_2\\\partial y_2/\partial x_1&\partial y_2/\partial x_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-2&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$ (Eqs. 5.65–5.66). The Jacobian is *exact* for linear transformations and *approximates* nonlinear ones locally; $|\det(\mathbf{J})|$ is the area/volume-scaling factor. This underlies the **change-of-variables** for probability distributions (§6.7) and is *"extremely relevant in machine learning in the context of training deep neural networks using the reparametrization trick, also called infinite perturbation analysis."* See [[Jacobian]], [[Determinant]].
- **Dimensions of gradients summary** (Fig. 5.6, p. 152): $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ → gradient is a scalar; $f:\mathbb{R}^D\to\mathbb{R}$ → $1\times D$ row vector; $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^E$ → $E\times 1$ column vector; $\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^D\to\mathbb{R}^E$ → $E\times D$ matrix.
- **Example 5.9 (Gradient of a Vector-Valued Function $\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$)** (Eqs. 5.67–5.68, p. 153): for $\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$, $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^N$, since $f_i=\sum_{j=1}^N A_{ij}x_j$ we get $\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}=A_{ij}$, so $\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$ (the gradient of a linear map is the matrix itself).
- **Example 5.10 (Chain Rule for $h=f\circ g$)** (Eqs. 5.69–5.74c, p. 153): for $h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ with $f:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}$, $g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^2$, $f(\mathbf{x})=\exp(x_1 x_2^2)$, $\mathbf{x}=g(t)=[t\cos t,\,t\sin t]^\top$: $\frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}}{\partial t}=[\exp(x_1 x_2^2)x_2^2\quad 2\exp(x_1 x_2^2)x_1 x_2]\begin{bmatrix}\cos t-t\sin t\\\sin t+t\cos t\end{bmatrix}$, dimensions $\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times 2}$, $\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 1}$.
- **Example 5.11 (Gradient of a Least-Squares Loss in a Linear Model)** (Eqs. 5.75–5.84, p. 154, marginals foreshadowing Ch 9; `dLdtheta = np.einsum('n,nd', dLde,dedtheta)`): the central worked example. For $\mathbf{y}=\boldsymbol\Phi\boldsymbol\theta$, $\boldsymbol\theta\in\mathbb{R}^D$, $\boldsymbol\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$, $\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^N$, with $L(\mathbf{e}):=\|\mathbf{e}\|^2$ and $\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol\theta):=\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\Phi\boldsymbol\theta$, the gradient $\frac{\partial L}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D}$ via the chain rule $\frac{\partial L}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{e}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{e}}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta}$. Since $\|\mathbf{e}\|^2=\mathbf{e}^\top\mathbf{e}$, $\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{e}}=2\mathbf{e}^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times N}$ (Eq. 5.81) and $\frac{\partial\mathbf{e}}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta}=-\boldsymbol\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$ (Eq. 5.82), giving $\frac{\partial L}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta}=-2\mathbf{e}^\top\boldsymbol\Phi=-2(\mathbf{y}^\top-\boldsymbol\theta^\top\boldsymbol\Phi^\top)\boldsymbol\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D}$ (Eq. 5.83). *Remark*: the same result follows from differentiating $L_2(\boldsymbol\theta):=\|\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\Phi\boldsymbol\theta\|^2$ directly, *"but becomes impractical for deep function compositions"* (Eq. 5.84) — the motivation for the chain rule / backprop.

### 5.4 Gradients of Matrices (pp. 155–158)

- **Gradients of matrices are higher-order tensors** (p. 155, marginals "We can think of a tensor as a multidimensional array", "Matrices can be transformed into vectors by stacking the columns of the matrix ('flattening')."): differentiating an $m\times n$ matrix $\mathbf{A}$ with respect to a $p\times q$ matrix $\mathbf{B}$ yields a **four-dimensional tensor** $\mathbf{J}$ of shape $(m\times n)\times(p\times q)$ with entries $J_{ijkl}=\partial A_{ij}/\partial B_{kl}$ (Fig. 5.7 visualizes a $4\times 2$ matrix differentiated w.r.t. a 3-vector → $4\times 2\times 3$ tensor). See [[Tensor]].
- **Flattening shortcut (vector-space isomorphism)** (p. 156): because $\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\cong\mathbb{R}^{mn}$, one can re-shape matrices into $mn$- and $pq$-vectors; the gradient then becomes an ordinary $mn\times pq$ Jacobian *matrix*. **Approach 1** collates partial derivatives into a Jacobian tensor; **Approach 2** flattens the matrix into a vector, computes a Jacobian matrix, then re-shapes (Fig. 5.7). *"In practical applications, it is often desirable to re-shape the matrix into a vector and continue working with this Jacobian matrix: The chain rule (5.48) boils down to simple matrix multiplication, whereas in the case of a Jacobian tensor, we will need to pay more attention to what dimensions we need to sum out."*
- **Example 5.12 (Gradient of Vectors with Respect to Matrices)** (Eqs. 5.85–5.92, pp. 156–157): for $\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^M$, $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$, $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^N$, the gradient $\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times(M\times N)}$ (Eq. 5.86). Since $f_i=\sum_{j=1}^N A_{ij}x_j$, $\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial A_{iq}}=x_q$ (Eq. 5.89); the partial of $f_i$ w.r.t. its own row $\mathbf{A}_{i,:}$ is $\mathbf{x}^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times 1\times N}$ (Eq. 5.90) and w.r.t. other rows is $\mathbf{0}^\top$ (Eq. 5.91), so $\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial\mathbf{A}}$ stacks zeros with $\mathbf{x}^\top$ in the $i$th slot (Eq. 5.92).
- **Example 5.13 (Gradient of Matrices with Respect to Matrices)** (Eqs. 5.93–5.98, pp. 157–158): for $\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{R})=\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=:\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}$ with $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$, the gradient $\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{K}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N\times N)\times(M\times N)}$ is a tensor. Since $K_{pq}=\mathbf{r}_p^\top\mathbf{r}_q=\sum_{m=1}^M R_{mp}R_{mq}$ (Eq. 5.96), the partial $\frac{\partial K_{pq}}{\partial R_{ij}}=\partial_{pqij}$ is a piecewise expression (Eq. 5.98): $R_{iq}$ if $j=p, p\neq q$; $R_{ip}$ if $j=q, p\neq q$; $2R_{iq}$ if $j=p, p=q$; $0$ otherwise.

### 5.5 Useful Identities for Computing Gradients (pp. 158–159)

- **A table of [[UsefulGradientIdentities|frequently required gradients]]** (Eqs. 5.99–5.108, pp. 158; from Petersen & Pedersen, 2012; using $\operatorname{tr}(\cdot)$, $\det(\cdot)$, $\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})^{-1}$):
  - $\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})^\top=\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\right)^\top$ (Eq. 5.99)
  - $\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X}))=\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\right)$ (Eq. 5.100)
  - $\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\det(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X}))=\det(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X}))\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\right)$ (Eq. 5.101)
  - $\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})^{-1}=-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X})^{-1}$ (Eq. 5.102)
  - $\frac{\partial\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{X}^{-1}\mathbf{b}}{\partial\mathbf{X}}=-(\mathbf{X}^{-1})^\top\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top(\mathbf{X}^{-1})^\top$ (Eq. 5.103)
  - $\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{a}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{a}^\top$ (Eq. 5.104); $\frac{\partial\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{x}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{a}^\top$ (Eq. 5.105)
  - $\frac{\partial\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b}}{\partial\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top$ (Eq. 5.106)
  - $\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}^\top(\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}^\top)$ (Eq. 5.107)
  - $\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{s}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s})^\top\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s})=-2(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s})^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}$ for symmetric $\mathbf{W}$ (Eq. 5.108).
- **Remark — trace/transpose on tensors** (pp. 158–159): the book *"only cover[s] traces and transposes of matrices,"* but derivatives can be higher-dimensional tensors where trace/transpose aren't defined: the trace of a $D\times D\times E\times F$ tensor is an $E\times F$ matrix (a special case of *tensor contraction*); "transposing" a tensor means swapping the first two dimensions.

### 5.6 Backpropagation and Automatic Differentiation (pp. 159–164)

- **Explicit gradients are impractical for compositions** (Eqs. 5.109–5.110, p. 159): for $f(x)=\sqrt{x^2+\exp(x^2)}+\cos(x^2+\exp(x^2))$ the analytic derivative (5.110) is a long expression; *"if we are not careful, the implementation of the gradient could be significantly more expensive than computing the function."* The **[[Backpropagation|backpropagation]]** algorithm (Kelley 1960; Bryson 1961; Dreyfus 1962; Rumelhart et al. 1986) is *"an efficient way to compute the gradient of an error function with respect to the parameters of the model."* Marginal: a Tim Vieira blog (`tinyurl.com/ycfm2yrw`) is recommended.

#### 5.6.1 Gradients in a Deep Network (pp. 159–160)

- **A deep network is a many-level composition** (Eq. 5.111, p. 159): $\mathbf{y}=(f_K\circ f_{K-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_1)(\mathbf{x})=f_K(f_{K-1}(\cdots(f_1(\mathbf{x}))\cdots))$, where $\mathbf{x}$ are inputs (e.g. images), $\mathbf{y}$ observations (e.g. class labels), each $f_i$ has its own parameters. *"An area where the chain rule is used to an extreme is deep learning."*
- **Layer structure and squared loss** (Eqs. 5.112–5.114, p. 160, Fig. 5.8): each layer $\mathbf{f}_i=\sigma_i(\mathbf{A}_{i-1}\mathbf{f}_{i-1}+\mathbf{b}_{i-1})$ with activation $\sigma$ (logistic sigmoid $\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$, $\tanh$, or ReLU); $\mathbf{f}_0:=\mathbf{x}$; loss $L(\boldsymbol\theta)=\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{f}_K(\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf{x})\|^2$ minimized over $\boldsymbol\theta=\{\mathbf{A}_0,\mathbf{b}_0,\dots,\mathbf{A}_{K-1},\mathbf{b}_{K-1}\}$.
- **Backward recursion reuses computation** (Eqs. 5.115–5.118, p. 160; marginal recommends Justin Domke's lecture notes `tinyurl.com/yalcxgtv`): the chain rule gives $\frac{\partial L}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta_{K-1}}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{f}_K}\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_K}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta_{K-1}}$, $\frac{\partial L}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta_{K-2}}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{f}_K}\boxed{\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_K}{\partial\mathbf{f}_{K-1}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_{K-1}}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta_{K-2}}}$, …, $\frac{\partial L}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta_i}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{f}_K}\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_K}{\partial\mathbf{f}_{K-1}}\cdots\boxed{\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_{i+2}}{\partial\mathbf{f}_{i+1}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_{i+1}}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta_i}}$. The **orange** terms are layer-output-w.r.t.-input partials; the **blue** terms are layer-output-w.r.t.-parameters partials. *"Assuming we have already computed the partial derivatives $\partial L/\partial\boldsymbol\theta_{i+1}$, then most of the computation can be reused to compute $\partial L/\partial\boldsymbol\theta_i$."* Fig. 5.9 visualizes gradients passed *backward* through the network.

#### 5.6.2 Automatic Differentiation (pp. 161–164)

- **Backprop is a special case of [[AutomaticDifferentiation|automatic differentiation]]** (p. 161, marginals "automatic differentiation", "Automatic differentiation is different from symbolic differentiation and numerical approximations of the gradient, e.g., by using finite differences", "In the general case, we work with Jacobians, which can be vectors, matrices, or tensors"): AD is *"a set of techniques to numerically (in contrast to symbolically) evaluate the exact (up to machine precision) gradient of a function by working with intermediate variables and applying the chain rule."* It applies a series of elementary arithmetic operations (+, ×) and elementary functions ($\sin,\cos,\exp,\log$); has **forward and reverse modes** (Baydin et al. 2018 for an ML overview). See [[ComputationalGraph]].
- **Forward vs reverse mode = order of multiplication** (Eqs. 5.119–5.121, p. 161, marginals "reverse mode", "forward mode"): for $x\to a\to b\to y$ (Fig. 5.10), $\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}b}\frac{\mathrm{d}b}{\mathrm{d}a}\frac{\mathrm{d}a}{\mathrm{d}x}$ (Eq. 5.119). By associativity of matrix multiplication: $\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}=\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}b}\frac{\mathrm{d}b}{\mathrm{d}a}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}a}{\mathrm{d}x}$ is **[[ReverseModeAutodiff|reverse mode]]** (gradients propagated backward, reverse to data flow, Eq. 5.120); $\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}b}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}b}{\mathrm{d}a}\frac{\mathrm{d}a}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)$ is **[[ForwardModeAutodiff|forward mode]]** (gradients flow with the data, left to right, Eq. 5.121).
- **Reverse mode is cheaper when inputs ≫ outputs** (p. 162): *"In the context of neural networks, where the input dimensionality is often much higher than the dimensionality of the labels, the reverse mode is computationally significantly cheaper than the forward mode."* The chapter focuses on **reverse mode = backpropagation**.
- **Example 5.14 (Computation graph with intermediate variables)** (Eqs. 5.122–5.142, pp. 162–163, Fig. 5.11, marginal "intermediate variables"): the $f(x)$ from (5.109) is implemented with intermediate variables $a=x^2$, $b=\exp(a)$, $c=a+b$, $d=\sqrt{c}$, $e=\cos(c)$, $f=d+e$ (Eqs. 5.123–5.128) — fewer operations than a direct implementation; the **[[ComputationalGraph|computation graph]]** (Fig. 5.11) shows the data flow. Elementary-function derivatives: $\frac{\partial a}{\partial x}=2x$, $\frac{\partial b}{\partial a}=\exp(a)$, $\frac{\partial c}{\partial a}=1=\frac{\partial c}{\partial b}$, $\frac{\partial d}{\partial c}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{c}}$, $\frac{\partial e}{\partial c}=-\sin(c)$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial d}=1=\frac{\partial f}{\partial e}$ (Eqs. 5.129–5.134). Working **backward** from the output: $\frac{\partial f}{\partial c}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial d}\frac{\partial d}{\partial c}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial e}\frac{\partial e}{\partial c}$, … , $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial a}\frac{\partial a}{\partial x}$ (Eqs. 5.135–5.142). **Key observation**: *"the computation required for calculating the derivative is of similar complexity as the computation of the function itself"* — counter-intuitive since the explicit derivative (5.110) is far more complicated than the function (5.109).
- **AD formalized over a computation graph** (Eqs. 5.143–5.145, pp. 163–164, marginal "Auto-differentiation in reverse mode requires a parse tree"): with input variables $x_1,\dots,x_d$, intermediate variables $x_{d+1},\dots,x_{D-1}$, and output $x_D$, the graph is $x_i=g_i(x_{\mathrm{Pa}(x_i)})$ for $i=d+1,\dots,D$ (Eq. 5.143, elementary $g_i$, parents $\mathrm{Pa}(x_i)$). Since $f=x_D$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_D}=1$ (Eq. 5.144); for other variables the chain rule gives $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}=\sum_{x_j:x_i\in\mathrm{Pa}(x_j)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}\frac{\partial x_j}{\partial x_i}=\sum_{x_j:x_i\in\mathrm{Pa}(x_j)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}\frac{\partial g_j}{\partial x_i}$ (Eq. 5.145). Eq. 5.143 is **forward propagation**; Eq. 5.145 is **backpropagation** of the gradient through the graph.
- **AD applies to programs, not just math functions** (p. 164): AD works whenever the function is expressible as a computation graph of differentiable elementary functions — *"the function may not even be a mathematical function but a computer program."* Caveats: not all programs are auto-differentiable (e.g. no differentiable elementary form); control structures (`for` loops, `if` statements) require more care.

### 5.7 Higher-Order Derivatives (pp. 164–165)

- **Higher-order derivatives for Newton's method etc.** (p. 164): second-order derivatives are needed for Newton's Method for optimization (Nocedal & Wright 2006); §5.7 extends Taylor approximation to the multivariate case.
- **Notation for higher-order partials** (p. 164): for $f:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}$ of $x,y$: $\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}$ (second partial w.r.t. $x$); $\frac{\partial^n f}{\partial x^n}$ ($n$th partial w.r.t. $x$); $\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\partial x}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)$ (first w.r.t. $x$, then $y$); $\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\partial y}$ (first $y$, then $x$).
- **Definition — the [[Hessian]]** (Eqs. 5.146–5.147, p. 165, marginal "Hessian matrix"): *"The Hessian is the collection of all second-order partial derivatives."* If $f(x,y)$ is twice continuously differentiable, the order of differentiation does not matter: $\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\partial y}=\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\partial x}$ (Eq. 5.146, **Schwarz/Clairaut symmetry**), so the Hessian $\mathbf{H}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}&\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\partial y}\\\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\partial y}&\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}\end{bmatrix}$ (Eq. 5.147) is **symmetric**. Denoted $\nabla^2_{x,y}f(x,y)$; for $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n$, $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ the Hessian is an $n\times n$ matrix that **measures the local curvature** of $f$ around $(x,y)$.
- **Remark (Hessian of a Vector Field)** (p. 165): if $\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ is a vector field, the Hessian is an $(m\times n\times n)$-tensor.

### 5.8 Linearization and Multivariate Taylor Series (pp. 165–169)

- **[[Linearization]] = first-order Taylor** (Eq. 5.148, p. 165, Fig. 5.12): the gradient locally linearizes $f$ around $\mathbf{x}_0$: $f(\mathbf{x})\approx f(\mathbf{x}_0)+(\nabla_\mathbf{x}f)(\mathbf{x}_0)(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)$, approximating the function by a straight line (Fig. 5.12 shows $f$ linearized at $x_0=-2$). *"This approximation is locally accurate, but the farther we move away from $\mathbf{x}_0$ the worse the approximation gets."* It is the special case of the multivariate Taylor series keeping only the first two terms.
- **Definition 5.7 ([[TaylorSeries|Multivariate Taylor Series]])** (Eqs. 5.149–5.151, p. 166, marginal "multivariate Taylor series"): for $f:\mathbb{R}^D\to\mathbb{R}$ smooth at $\mathbf{x}_0$, with difference vector $\boldsymbol\delta:=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0$, the series is $f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\dfrac{D_\mathbf{x}^k f(\mathbf{x}_0)}{k!}\boldsymbol\delta^k$ (Eq. 5.151), where $D_\mathbf{x}^k f(\mathbf{x}_0)$ is the $k$th **(total) derivative** of $f$ w.r.t. $\mathbf{x}$ at $\mathbf{x}_0$.
- **Definition 5.8 (Multivariate [[TaylorPolynomial|Taylor Polynomial]])** (Eq. 5.152, p. 166, marginal "Taylor polynomial"): the degree-$n$ Taylor polynomial keeps the first $n+1$ terms: $T_n(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=0}^n\dfrac{D_\mathbf{x}^k f(\mathbf{x}_0)}{k!}\boldsymbol\delta^k$.
- **The $\boldsymbol\delta^k$ are outer-product [[Tensor|tensors]]** (Eqs. 5.153–5.155, pp. 166–167, Fig. 5.13, marginal "A vector can be implemented as a one-dimensional array, a matrix as a two-dimensional array"): both $D_\mathbf{x}^k f$ and $\boldsymbol\delta^k$ are $k$th-order tensors ($k$-dimensional arrays). $\boldsymbol\delta^k\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D\times\cdots\times D}$ ($k$ times) is the $k$-fold **outer product** $\otimes$: $\boldsymbol\delta^2:=\boldsymbol\delta\otimes\boldsymbol\delta=\boldsymbol\delta\boldsymbol\delta^\top$, $\boldsymbol\delta^2[i,j]=\delta[i]\delta[j]$ (Eq. 5.153); $\boldsymbol\delta^3:=\boldsymbol\delta\otimes\boldsymbol\delta\otimes\boldsymbol\delta$, $\boldsymbol\delta^3[i,j,k]=\delta[i]\delta[j]\delta[k]$ (Eq. 5.154). The general Taylor term is $D_\mathbf{x}^k f(\mathbf{x}_0)\boldsymbol\delta^k=\sum_{i_1=1}^D\cdots\sum_{i_k=1}^D D_\mathbf{x}^k f(\mathbf{x}_0)[i_1,\dots,i_k]\delta[i_1]\cdots\delta[i_k]$ (Eq. 5.155) — *$k$th-order polynomials*. (Marginals show numpy `einsum` implementations, e.g. `np.einsum('i,i',Df1,d)`, `np.einsum('ij,i,j',Df2,d,d)`, `np.einsum('ijk,i,j,k',Df3,d,d,d)`.)
- **First terms of the multivariate Taylor expansion** (Eqs. 5.156–5.160, p. 167): $k=0$: $D_\mathbf{x}^0 f(\mathbf{x}_0)\boldsymbol\delta^0=f(\mathbf{x}_0)$; $k=1$: $D_\mathbf{x}^1 f(\mathbf{x}_0)\boldsymbol\delta=\nabla_\mathbf{x}f(\mathbf{x}_0)\boldsymbol\delta=\sum_{i=1}^D\nabla_\mathbf{x}f(\mathbf{x}_0)[i]\delta[i]$ (gradient · $\boldsymbol\delta$); $k=2$: $D_\mathbf{x}^2 f(\mathbf{x}_0)\boldsymbol\delta^2=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}_0)\boldsymbol\delta\boldsymbol\delta^\top)=\boldsymbol\delta^\top\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}_0)\boldsymbol\delta=\sum_{i,j}H[i,j]\delta[i]\delta[j]$ (the [[Hessian]] quadratic form, Eqs. 5.158–5.159); $k=3$: $D_\mathbf{x}^3 f(\mathbf{x}_0)\boldsymbol\delta^3=\sum_{i,j,k}D_\mathbf{x}^3 f(\mathbf{x}_0)[i,j,k]\delta[i]\delta[j]\delta[k]$ (Eq. 5.160).
- **Example 5.15 (Taylor Series of a Two-Variable Function)** (Eqs. 5.161–5.180c, pp. 167–169): for $f(x,y)=x^2+2xy+y^3$ (degree-3 polynomial) expanded at $(x_0,y_0)=(1,2)$. $f(1,2)=13$ (Eq. 5.162); gradient $D^1_{x,y}f(1,2)=[6\;\;14]$ (Eqs. 5.163–5.165), giving the first-order term $6(x-1)+14(y-2)$ (Eq. 5.166). Hessian $\mathbf{H}=\begin{bmatrix}2&2\\2&6y\end{bmatrix}$, $\mathbf{H}(1,2)=\begin{bmatrix}2&2\\2&12\end{bmatrix}$ (Eqs. 5.167–5.172), giving the second-order term $\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol\delta^\top\mathbf{H}(1,2)\boldsymbol\delta=(x-1)^2+2(x-1)(y-2)+6(y-2)^2$ (Eqs. 5.173a–5.173c). The only nonzero third-order derivative is $\frac{\partial^3 f}{\partial y^3}=6$ (Eq. 5.177), giving the third-order term $(y-2)^3$ (Eq. 5.179). The **exact** Taylor expansion (Eqs. 5.180a–5.180c) reassembles to $13+6(x-1)+14(y-2)+(x-1)^2+6(y-2)^2+2(x-1)(y-2)+(y-2)^3$, identical to the original polynomial (5.161) — unsurprising since a degree-3 polynomial is exactly captured by terms up to order 3.

### 5.9 Further Reading (p. 170)

- **References** (p. 170): matrix differentials — Magnus & Neudecker (2007); automatic differentiation history — Griewank & Walther (2003, 2008), Elliott (2009).
- **Taylor linearization for intractable expectations** (Eq. 5.181, p. 170, marginals "extended Kalman filter", "unscented transform", "Laplace approximation"): ML often needs $\mathbb{E}_\mathbf{x}[f(\mathbf{x})]=\int f(\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}$, which generally has no closed form even for Gaussian $p$. A first-order Taylor expansion of $f$ around $\boldsymbol\mu$ locally linearizes a nonlinear $f$; for linear functions the mean and covariance are exact if $p$ is Gaussian (§6.5). This is exploited by the **extended Kalman filter** (Maybeck 1979) for online state estimation in nonlinear dynamical systems. Other deterministic integral approximations: the **unscented transform** (Julier & Uhlmann 1997, gradient-free) and the **[[LaplaceApproximation|Laplace approximation]]** (MacKay 2003; Bishop 2006; Murphy 2012), which uses a *second-order* Taylor expansion (requiring the Hessian) for a local Gaussian approximation of $p(\mathbf{x})$ around its mode.

> "The Hessian is the collection of all second-order partial derivatives. ... The Hessian measures the curvature of the function locally around $(x,y)$." — §5.7, pp. 164–165

## Contradictions

- **Row-vector gradient convention (the central notational quirk).** [[mml-book]] §5.2 (Eq. 5.40, p. 146) and §5.3 (Def 5.6, numerator layout, p. 150) define the gradient $\nabla_\mathbf{x}f$ as a **row vector** $\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}$ and the Jacobian as an $m\times n$ matrix whose rows index the output $\mathbf{f}$ and columns index the input $\mathbf{x}$ (numerator layout). The book justifies this *deliberately* (Remark, p. 147): it (i) generalizes cleanly to vector-valued functions and (ii) makes the multivariate chain rule a plain left-to-right matrix multiplication $\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}(s,t)}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}}{\partial(s,t)}$ (Eq. 5.53) with no transposes. **This differs from the column-vector gradient convention** used by **Murphy** (*Probabilistic ML* / *PML*, where $\nabla f\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is a column and the chain rule reads $J_{g\circ f}=J_g J_f$ only after transposing), **Goodfellow–Bengio–Courville** (*Deep Learning*, column-vector gradients, denominator-ish layout), and **D2L** ([[d2l-preliminaries]] / [[d2l-appendix-mathematics]], gradient is a column vector and the multivariate chain rule is stated as $\nabla_\mathbf{x}y=\mathbf{A}^\top\nabla_\mathbf{u}y$ with an explicit transpose). The *math is identical* — every disagreement is a transpose — but **the matrix shapes printed on the page differ**, so a reader cross-referencing MML §5.3 against Murphy/Goodfellow/D2L must mentally transpose Jacobians and remember which side of the product the gradient sits on. This is already flagged on [[Gradient]] / [[Jacobian]] / [[PartialDerivative]] and is reinforced here. Parr & Howard's [[matrix-calculus-for-deep-learning]] adopt the **same numerator layout** as MML and *explicitly warn* readers that "papers mix the two" — so MML and Parr & Howard agree with each other and disagree with the column-vector camp.
- **"Total derivative" $D_\mathbf{x}^k f$ notation (§5.8) vs the elementwise Hessian/Jacobian notation (§5.3, §5.7).** §5.8 introduces $D_\mathbf{x}^k f(\mathbf{x}_0)$ as the $k$th *total* derivative tensor and the "slightly sloppy" $\boldsymbol\delta^k$ outer-product notation (the book itself flags it as sloppy, p. 166: "$\boldsymbol\delta^k$ is not defined for $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^D$, $D>1$, and $k>1$"). For $k=1,2$ these reduce to the familiar gradient and Hessian, but the general tensor notation is non-standard and differs from the index-heavy notation used elsewhere; readers should map $D_\mathbf{x}^1=\nabla_\mathbf{x}f$ and $D_\mathbf{x}^2=\mathbf{H}$.
- **No conflict on backprop framing.** MML's "backprop = reverse-mode AD" (§5.6.2) is fully consistent with the wiki's [[Backpropagation]] / [[AutomaticDifferentiation]] pages and with [[d2l-multilayer-perceptrons]]; MML simply gives the cleanest one-sentence statement of it (quoted above).
