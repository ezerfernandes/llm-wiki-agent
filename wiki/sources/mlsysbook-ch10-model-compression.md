# Machine Learning Systems (mlsysbook Vol 1) — Ch 10: Model Compression

## Summary

Chapter 10 of [[VijayJanapaReddi|Vijay Janapa Reddi]]'s *Introduction to Machine Learning Systems* ([[Harvard]], mlsysbook.ai, 2026) is the **"A" of the [[DAMTaxonomy|D·A·M taxonomy]]** seen from the model side — the systematic discipline of [[ModelCompression|model compression]]. Its thesis: the models that win benchmarks rarely run in production because *"trained models are vastly over-specified for most production tasks,"* and that surplus (precision, connections, capacity) can be removed without destroying what the deployment requires. A 7-billion-parameter LLM needs ~14 GB in FP16 but a smartphone offers ~8 GB and a microcontroller ~256 KB — *"the math does not work,"* and no engineering changes the arithmetic. Compression renegotiates the **silicon contract** ([[IronLawOfMLSystems|iron law]]) for a new execution context, attacking $D_{\text{vol}}$ (data moved), $O$ (operations), and energy. The chapter organizes every technique along **three complementary dimensions**: *structural optimization* (what to compute — [[Pruning|pruning]], [[KnowledgeDistillation|knowledge distillation]], [[NeuralArchitectureSearch|NAS]], [[LowRankFactorization|low-rank factorization]]), *precision optimization* (how precisely — [[Quantization|quantization]] from FP32 to INT8 and below), and *architectural efficiency* (how efficiently it executes — [[OperatorFusion|operator fusion]], [[Sparsity|sparsity exploitation]], [[AdaptiveInference|adaptive computation]]). These are "layers in an optimization stack," not alternatives.

A unifying meta-law governs the whole chapter — the **[[ConservationOfComplexity|conservation of complexity]]**: like energy, complexity cannot be destroyed, only relocated between data, algorithm, and machine, which is why *"no compression technique achieves a free lunch."* Pruning relocates complexity from parameters to the hardware's burden of irregular sparse access; distillation moves it from inference compute to training compute; NAS moves it from human design to automated search budget. The second through-line is the **theory-practice gap**: a model pruned 50% and quantized to INT8 has a back-of-envelope 6× speedup target but often measures only ~1.5× (~25% of paper) on commodity hardware, because *"theoretical compression does not translate into proportional speedup"* unless the optimization aligns with the hardware's execution model. The chapter grounds techniques in recurring Lighthouse Models ([[ResNet50|ResNet-50]], [[MobileNetV2]]/MobileNetV3, transformer LMs, the [[DSCNN|DS-CNN]] keyword spotter, the [[DLRM]] recommender), closes with a deployment-constraint decision framework, profiling/Amdahl caveats, framework + compiler tooling ([[PyTorch]], [[TensorFlow]] TF-MOT, [[TVM]], [[ONNX]], [[TensorRTLLM|TensorRT]]), and seven fallacies/pitfalls. Its headline case: BERT compressed **440 MB → 28 MB (16×)** via sequential structural pruning + distillation + INT8 QAT, with only ~0.6% accuracy loss when sequenced correctly.

## Key Claims

- **The deployment gap spans ~6 orders of magnitude.** Cloud (tens of GB, 10–100 ms, flexible power) → Mobile/Edge (hundreds of MB–GB, 10–50 ms, watt-scale) → [[TinyML]] (KB–MB, 1–10 ms, milliwatt). Even MobileNetV2 quantized to INT8 *exceeds* a ~512 KB TinyML envelope by ~7×; only the purpose-built ~500 KB DS-CNN keyword spotter fits. GPT-175B at FP16 = ~350 GB; INT8 halves that to ~175 GB.
- **The three-dimension optimization stack.** Top = efficient *model representation* (pruning, distillation, NAS); middle = efficient *numerics* (quantization, mixed precision); bottom = efficient *hardware implementation* (fusion, sparsity, scheduling). Most effective strategies combine all three: prune 50% of ResNet-50 filters → INT8 → fuse batchnorm into convolution.
- **Bits are energy — the energy hierarchy.** Normalized to an 8-bit integer add (1×): a 32-bit float add costs ~30×, a single 32-bit DRAM read costs >10,000× (more than four orders of magnitude above on-chip compute). Moving FP32→INT8 saves 4× memory but can cut energy-per-inference by up to ~20× on hardware with INT8 units — *"a battery lasting one hour or twenty hours."* This is the chapter's central physics argument (Horowitz 2014 energy constants).
- **Networks are ~10× overparameterized.** Zhang et al. (2017) showed ImageNet-capable nets can memorize random labels — far more capacity than any task needs. ResNet-50's 25.6M params prune by 90% with <2% accuracy loss, implying <3M carry the task-relevant information. This redundancy is "what makes the entire compression discipline viable."
- **Pruning = sparsifying the parameter space; quantization = reducing bit-depth of every parameter.** Pruning frames as $\min_{\hat W}\mathcal{L}(\hat W)$ s.t. $\|\hat W\|_0 \le k$ (NP-hard), so magnitude-based heuristics + iterative prune-retrain dominate. **Optimal Brain Damage** (LeCun 1990) used Hessian info for 4× reduction but costs $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$; magnitude pruning won at scale despite "theoretical inferiority."
- **Unstructured vs structured vs dynamic pruning trade regularity against compression.** Unstructured (individual weights) gives highest compression but irregular sparsity GPUs/TPUs can't exploit without sparse kernels — primarily a *storage* win. Structured (neurons/channels/filters/layers) yields dense sub-networks that run fast on commodity hardware — a *latency* win. **N:M structured sparsity (2:4, NVIDIA A100, 2020)** gives up to 2× speedup when the pattern matches the Sparse Tensor Core path.
- **Iterative pruning beats one-shot.** On a 22-channel CNN removing 6 channels: iterative (2/cycle × 3) ends at 0.991 accuracy (−0.4% from 0.995); one-shot drops to 0.914, recovering only to 0.943 (−5%). Same final structure, very different accuracy.
- **Lottery Ticket Hypothesis** (Frankle & Carbin): sparse "winning ticket" subnetworks exist at initialization; ResNet-18 subnets at 10–20% size reach 93.2% vs 94.1% full accuracy. Found by prune → *reset surviving weights to original init* → repeat. Implication: training the other 80–90% of params may be pure overhead.
- **Knowledge distillation transfers "dark knowledge."** A teacher's soft probability $[0.85,0.10,0.05]$ reveals inter-class similarity a hard label $[1,0,0]$ cannot. Loss = $(1-\gamma)\mathcal{L}_{CE} + \gamma T^2 \mathcal{D}_{KL}(\text{teacher}^{(T)}\,\|\,\text{student}^{(T)})$ with temperature $T\approx 3$–$5$; the $T^2$ factor keeps gradient scales consistent. Distillation produces a **dense** student (no sparse kernels needed). **[[DistilBERT]]**: 97% of BERT-Base accuracy, 40% fewer params (66M vs 110M), 60% faster (85 ms → 34 ms, 1.35 GB → 0.81 GB).
- **Low-rank factorization trades compute for bandwidth.** A 4096×4096 FP32 matrix (67 MB) factored at rank-128 → two matrices totaling ~4 MB (16× data-movement reduction); per-vector compute drops $\mathcal{O}(mn)\to\mathcal{O}(k(m+n))$. SVD is optimal (Eckart-Young). *Never materialize $UV$ explicitly* — that adds $\mathcal{O}(mkn)$ and defeats the purpose. Tensor decompositions (CP, Tucker, Tensor-Train) extend this to conv filters/attention.
- **NAS automates architecture design but is expensive.** Zoph & Le (2017, Google Brain) RL-NAS cost **22,400 GPU-days (537,600 GPU-hours = 800 GPUs × 28 days, ~$50K–$100K)**. Weight-sharing later cut this ~1000×. Strategies: RL (400–1,000 GPU-days), evolutionary/AmoebaNet (200–500 GPU-days, 3,150 to reach 83.9% ImageNet), **gradient-based DARTS (1–4 GPU-days)**. Hardware-aware NAS (MnasNet, reward = accuracy × (target_latency/latency)^β) found nets 1.8× faster than MobileNetV2. **EfficientNet** discovered compound scaling; **MobileNetV3** found inverted residuals + squeeze-excitation + h-swish.
- **The "Free Lunch / Cliff" of quantization.** CNNs/transformers hold accuracy flat from FP32 down to INT8 (<1% loss), then collapse at a "quantization cliff" typically at 3–4 bits. FP32→INT8 collapses 4 billion representable values to 256; INT8 ≈ <1% drop, INT4 ≈ 1–3% for standard tasks — but *not universal* (must validate per-model/per-task; vision tolerates better than language generation).
- **The "Energy Dividend":** FP32→INT8 pays a 4× price in bits but receives ~30× return in arithmetic energy. **SIMD multiplier:** a 512-bit AVX-512 register packs 4× more INT8 elements than FP32 → up to 4× compute throughput from vector packing alone. **A100:** ~624 TOPS INT8 vs ~312 TFLOP/s FP16 = 2× peak compute throughput; LLM decode bandwidth-bound case gets ~2–4× from halving bytes moved.
- **The 4× MobileNet win.** Unoptimized MobileNetV3 (FP32) runs 8 FPS on mid-tier Android — too slow to ship; INT8 quantization → 35 FPS, 3× lower energy/frame. *"Without INT8 quantization, the product could not exist for the target market."*
- **AlexNet's two-GPU split (2012) was a memory budget forced into architecture.** A single GTX 580 (3 GB) couldn't hold the 60M-param net + activations, so Krizhevsky/Sutskever/Hinton split it across two GPUs. *"Every model that runs on real silicon carries the fingerprints of the memory hierarchy it had to fit on."*
- **PTQ vs QAT vs mixed precision — a complexity ladder.** PTQ: no retraining, needs only a small calibration set, ~1–2% loss, free in TF Lite/ONNX Runtime/PyTorch. QAT: simulates low precision in training via **fake-quantization nodes + the Straight-Through Estimator (STE)** (treats rounding's derivative as 1), 20–50% extra training time, <1% loss; BERT-Base keeps 99.1% GLUE (QAT) vs 96.8% (PTQ). Decision rule: *"start with PTQ and measure; if it meets the threshold, ship it."*
- **Calibration and granularity govern PTQ accuracy.** Affine quant: $s=(\beta-\alpha)/(2^b-1)$, $z=\text{round}(-\alpha/s)$. Calibration methods: **Max** (outlier-prone), **Entropy** (TensorRT default, minimizes KL), **Percentile** (clip at 99%). Granularity: layerwise → groupwise (Q-BERT) → **channelwise (current standard)** → sub-channelwise. **AWQ** protects ~1% of salient weights (by *activation* magnitude) to enable INT4 LLM weights, cutting a 7B model 14 GB → ~3.5 GB.
- **Extreme quantization (binary/ternary).** Binarization (±1) and ternarization (−1,0,+1) give 16–32× size reduction but severe accuracy loss; need custom hardware + QAT/STE. Lives in the keyword-spotting regime where ~256 KB SRAM demands it.
- **Architectural efficiency closes the theory-practice gap.** Depthwise separable convolutions (MobileNet) give 5–10× FLOP reduction; MobileNetV2 takes a 4-point ImageNet drop for **13.7× fewer ops vs ResNet-50**. SqueezeNet's 1×1 convs gave **50× fewer params than AlexNet (240 MB → <5 MB)**. **Operator fusion** (Conv-BN-ReLU) cuts 6 memory transfers to 2 (3×), saving ~50% bandwidth and kernel-launch overhead (ResNet-50: 159 → 53 kernels); **FlashAttention** tiles attention to SRAM, $\mathcal{O}(S^2)\to\mathcal{O}(S)$ HBM traffic, 2–4× and 64K context.
- **Adaptive computation spends compute proportional to input difficulty.** Early exit (BranchyNet, multi-exit ViTs) emits predictions at intermediate layers on confidence threshold; conditional computation (SkipNet gating); **Mixture-of-Experts / Switch Transformer**: 1.6T params but only ~2B activated per token (0.13%), 7× faster pretraining than dense T5 at equal FLOPs. Costs: irregular execution hurts hardware utilization, training instability, variable latency.
- **Sparsity exploitation reduces what we *compute* (vs pruning, which reduces what we *store*).** Sparse-aware GEMM skips zeros; block-sparse (cuSPARSE, Monarch) and N:M (2:4) earn hardware speedup; **unstructured sparsity typically needs >90–95% zeros to beat dense kernels** because SIMD lanes still load zeros (a 16-wide register may yield 1–2 nonzeros). CSR breaks even ~50% density, COO ~33%, before index overhead.
- **Sequencing matters.** BERT-Base mobile pipeline: structured prune (75% params, 76.2%→75.1%) → distill (→75.9%) → INT8 QAT (→75.6%) = **16× memory (440→28 MB), 12× CPU speedup, 0.6% loss** — vs 2.1% loss if quantization preceded pruning. Pruning first concentrates important weights into tighter ranges, making quantization more effective.
- **Amdahl's Law caps end-to-end gains.** If model inference is only 20% of pipeline latency, even perfect model optimization yields ≤1.25× overall. *"FLOPs reduction ≠ proportional speedup."* Profile on target hardware.

## Key Quotes

> "Why do the models that win benchmarks rarely become the models that run in production?" — chapter opening; the discipline "is not about making models smaller but about *making the right models possible* for their physical environment."

> "The math does not work. No amount of clever engineering changes this arithmetic: 14 GB *cannot* fit in 8 GB." — on the 7B-on-smartphone deployment gap

> "An ML system *cannot* destroy complexity but only relocate it between data, algorithm, and machine. This principle constrains all possible optimizations and explains why no compression technique achieves a free lunch." — the conservation of complexity meta-law

> "Compression is not a finishing step layered onto a finished model; it is the same battle the field has been fighting since AlexNet." — AlexNet two-GPU war story

> "We call this the 'Dividend' because the system pays a 4× 'price' in bits but receives a 30× 'return' in energy efficiency." — the INT8-vs-FP32 energy dividend

> "Theoretical compression does not translate into proportional speedup. Optimization must extend beyond the model itself to how computations execute on physical hardware." — the theory-practice gap (6× target, ~1.5× measured)

> "The most important lesson of this chapter is that these dimensions compose multiplicatively... The second lesson is equally important: theoretical compression ratios lie." — Summary

## Contradictions

- **No direct contradiction with [[dmls-ch07-model-deployment|DMLS Ch 7]] or [[ai-engineering-ch09-inference-optimization|AI Engineering Ch 9]].** All three share the four-family taxonomy (quantization/pruning/distillation/low-rank) and the DistilBERT 97%/40%/60% figures. This chapter *adds* the energy-physics framing (Horowitz constants, the ~30× energy dividend, the >10,000× DRAM-vs-INT8-add gap), the conservation-of-complexity meta-law, and the explicit three-dimension stack — none of which the LLM-era books foreground.
- **Reframes "quantization is the easiest/best lever."** DMLS Ch 7 and AI Engineering Ch 9 call quantization the most general, out-of-the-box win. This chapter agrees ("single most impactful") but is sharper that the benefit is **hardware-conditional**: INT8 on hardware without INT8 units "achieves even less" than nothing (dequant overhead can *increase* latency 15%), and the fallacy section explicitly debunks "compression ratios translate directly into proportional deployment gains."
- **Tension on pruning value.** Where DMLS cites the lottery ticket / pruned-architecture-matters-more debate as accuracy-neutral, this chapter stresses unstructured pruning is "primarily a *storage* win" that needs >90–95% sparsity to beat dense kernels on commodity hardware — a more pessimistic systems read than the parameter-count-centric framing.
- **"Higher precision (FP32) is always safer" is labeled a fallacy.** Counter to a common practitioner default, the chapter argues FP32 "often hurts performance without improving convergence" since extra mantissa bits model random variance; FP16/BF16 should be the default starting point, FP32 reserved for LayerNorm/Softmax/accumulators.
