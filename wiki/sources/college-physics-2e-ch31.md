## Summary
Chapter 31 of OpenStax *College Physics 2e* introduces nuclear physics and radioactivity: the substructure of the nucleus (nucleons, atomic number, isotopes, nuclide notation), the three radiation types (alpha, beta, gamma) and how they ionize matter, how radiation is detected, the conservation laws governing nuclear decay, the statistics of half-life and activity (with radiometric dating), nuclear binding energy and the binding-energy-per-nucleon curve, and the quantum-tunneling mechanism behind alpha decay. The unifying thread is mass–energy equivalence: small mass deficits release enormous energies in nuclear processes.

## Key Claims
- Radioactivity is the spontaneous emission of energy from unstable nuclei, with mass converted to energy via E = mc².
- Three primary radiations differ in charge, identity, and penetration: alpha (⁴He nucleus, least penetrating), beta (electron/positron), gamma (high-energy photon, most penetrating).
- The nucleus is made of nucleons (protons + neutrons); atomic number Z identifies the element and the nuclear radius follows r = r₀A^(1/3) with r₀ ≈ 1.2 fm, giving nearly constant, enormous nuclear density.
- Nuclear decay conserves charge, nucleon number, momentum (linear and angular), mass–energy, and electron family number.
- Beta decay required postulating the neutrino (Pauli, 1930) to conserve energy and momentum; it interacts only via the weak force and was not detected until 1953.
- Decay is statistical: each nucleus has a fixed decay probability, giving N = N₀e^(−λt) with λ = 0.693/t₁/₂; activity R = λN, measured in becquerel (Bq) or curie (Ci, = 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq).
- Carbon-14 dating works for organic material up to ~50,000–60,000 years; uranium-238 (t₁/₂ = 4.5 × 10⁹ yr) dates ancient rocks.
- Binding energy equals the mass defect times c²; BE/A peaks near iron-56, explaining why fusion of light nuclei and fission of heavy nuclei both release energy.
- The strong nuclear force is ~100× stronger than Coulomb repulsion but very short-ranged, which shapes the BE/A curve and nuclear stability.
- Alpha decay occurs by quantum tunneling: the alpha particle escapes the Coulomb barrier despite being ~8 MeV short of the classical escape energy (Gamow, 1928).
- Radiation is detected via photographic film, Geiger tubes, scintillator + photomultiplier systems, and solid-state semiconductor detectors.

## Key Quotes (paraphrased)
> Half-life is the time for half the original nuclei to decay; each surviving nucleus still has a 50% chance of decaying in the next half-life, regardless of how long it has already survived. — §31.5

> A bound nucleus weighs less than its separated nucleons; that "missing" mass is the binding energy via E = (Δm)c². — §31.6

> An alpha particle lacks the classical energy to escape the nucleus, yet quantum mechanics gives its wave function a nonzero value beyond the Coulomb barrier, so it tunnels out. — §31.7
