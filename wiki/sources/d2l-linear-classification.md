## Summary

The second modeling chapter of [[d2l-preface|*Dive into Deep Learning*]]. Seven sections (`softmax-regression.md`, `image-classification-dataset.md`, `classification.md`, `softmax-regression-scratch.md`, `softmax-regression-concise.md`, `generalization-classification.md`, `environment-and-distribution-shift.md`) develop [[Softmax|softmax regression]] as the canonical multiclass classifier: one-hot labels, an affine output of dimension equal to the number of classes, [[Softmax|softmax]] normalization that "squishes" logits into a probability simplex, and [[CrossEntropyLoss|cross-entropy loss]] derived as the negative log-likelihood under a categorical likelihood. The chapter then introduces [[FashionMNIST|Fashion-MNIST]] as the running benchmark, builds a `Classifier` base class with `accuracy` and `validation_step`, walks through from-scratch and concise framework implementations, addresses numerical stability via the [[LogSumExp|LogSumExp trick]] (combining softmax and cross-entropy into a single fused operator), and closes with two foundational discussions: **statistical-learning-theory** for classification ([[HoeffdingsInequality|Hoeffding bounds]], [[UniformConvergence|uniform convergence]], [[VCDimension|VC dimension]], adaptive overfitting via [[TestSetReuse|test-set reuse]]) and **environment and distribution shift** ([[CovariateShift]], [[LabelShift]], [[ConceptShift]], importance-weighted [[EmpiricalRiskMinimization|ERM]] correction, and a taxonomy of batch / online / bandit / control / RL learning settings). The chapter is the conceptual bridge between linear models and deep classifiers and the wiki's anchor for distribution-shift terminology.

## Key Claims

- **Softmax = exponential-family normalization.** To get a valid probability vector from logits $\mathbf{o}$, exponentiate then normalize: $\hat y_i = \exp(o_i)/\sum_j\exp(o_j)$. The function dates to Gibbs (1902), adapted from Boltzmann's $\exp(-E/kT)$ distribution over thermodynamic energy states; "temperature" in modern ML is the same $T$. Softmax preserves order ($\arg\max_j\hat y_j = \arg\max_j o_j$), is differentiable, and is exactly the maximum-entropy distribution on $K$ categories with linear constraints — the natural choice from the [[ExponentialFamily|exponential-family]] perspective.

- **Cross-entropy loss = negative log-likelihood under a categorical likelihood.** Modeling $P(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})$ as categorical with softmax probabilities and taking $-\log\prod_i P(\mathbf{y}^{(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}^{(i)})$ yields $l(\mathbf{y},\hat{\mathbf{y}}) = -\sum_j y_j\log\hat y_j$ for one-hot $\mathbf{y}$. Equivalently $l = \log\sum_k\exp(o_k) - \sum_j y_j o_j$ (the log-partition minus the true-class logit). Bounded below by 0; reaches 0 only at certainty, which requires logits at $\pm\infty$ — so any finite parametrization keeps the loss strictly positive.

- **The softmax-cross-entropy gradient is prediction minus target.** $\partial_{o_j} l = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{o})_j - y_j$ — exactly the regression-style "error" signal, and a *general* property of any [[ExponentialFamily|exponential-family]] log-likelihood (the gradient equals the difference between expected and observed sufficient statistics). This is what makes the softmax+cross-entropy pairing the canonical loss for classification: it produces the cleanest backprop signal of any classification loss.

- **Numerical stability requires the [[LogSumExp|LogSumExp trick]].** Naive softmax exponentiates logits in FP32, which overflows when any $o_i$ exceeds ≈90 and underflows when all $o_i$ are very negative. The fix is to subtract $\bar o = \max_k o_k$ from every logit before exponentiating — mathematically a no-op (numerator and denominator factors of $\exp(\bar o)$ cancel), but it bounds the numerator at 1 and the denominator in $[1, q]$. Even better: combine softmax and cross-entropy into a single fused operator $\log\hat y_j = o_j - \bar o - \log\sum_k\exp(o_k - \bar o)$ — avoiding overflow *and* the $\log 0$ underflow on the loss side. Every modern framework's `cross_entropy(logits, target)` does this; passing pre-softmax logits (not probabilities) is the correct interface.

- **[[FashionMNIST|Fashion-MNIST]] is the chapter's running benchmark.** [[MNIST]] (60k 28×28 grayscale digits) was the canonical CV benchmark from the 1990s through the 2010s, but simple models routinely exceed 95% accuracy, leaving no signal to distinguish strong models from weak ones; today "MNIST serves as more of a sanity check than as a benchmark." Fashion-MNIST (Xiao, Rasul & Vollgraf 2017, released by [[Zalando]] Research) is a drop-in replacement: 10 clothing categories (t-shirt, trouser, pullover, dress, coat, sandal, shirt, sneaker, bag, ankle boot), same 28×28 grayscale resolution, same 60k/10k train/test split — but with enough variation to discriminate model quality. The chapter uses it as the running dataset for every subsequent classifier through Part 2.

- **Classification accuracy is *the* benchmark metric but not the training objective.** Accuracy = fraction of predictions where $\arg\max_j\hat y_j = y$. Not differentiable, so not used as a loss; nearly always reported as the primary performance number nonetheless. The `Classifier` base class subclasses `d2l.Module`, adds a `validation_step` that reports both loss and accuracy, and is the parent of every subsequent classifier in the book — from softmax regression through CNNs, RNNs, and Transformer classifier heads.

- **[[TestSetReuse|Repeated test-set use]] silently leaks information** — every reuse of the *same* test set on a *new* model trained after seeing prior test performance erodes its status as an unbiased estimator of the population error. Two compounding problems: (i) **multiple hypothesis testing** — evaluating $k$ classifiers on the same test set blows up the per-classifier 5% false-discovery probability, and with $k=20$ classifiers there may be *no* power to rule out at least one misleading result; (ii) **adaptive overfitting** (Dwork et al. 2015 (*Preserving statistical validity in adaptive data analysis*)) — once test performance has been observed, subsequent model choices are no longer independent of the test set, and theoretical worst-case guarantees become bleak. Practical mitigation: curate multiple test sets, consult them as infrequently as possible, demote old test sets to validation sets after each benchmark round.

- **Hoeffding's inequality gives finite-sample bounds on test error.** The central limit theorem gives an asymptotic $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt n)$ rate (so to halve the standard deviation of the test-error estimate one needs $4\times$ the test data); Hoeffding's inequality $P(\epsilon_{\mathcal D}(f) - \epsilon(f) \geq t) < \exp(-2nt^2)$ tightens this to a valid *finite-sample* bound. Concretely: to be 95% confident the test error is within ±0.01 of the true error requires ≈10,000 samples by CLT, ≈15,000 by Hoeffding. The ~10k test-set size of most ML benchmarks is *not* a coincidence; it is the smallest size at which a 0.01 accuracy difference is statistically credible.

- **[[UniformConvergence|Uniform convergence]] and the Vapnik–Chervonenkis program.** For a *fixed* classifier, the empirical error is an unbiased estimate of the population error. The hard problem is when the classifier is *chosen* from a class $\mathcal{F}$ based on training data: we need empirical error to converge to true error *simultaneously* for every $f \in \mathcal F$. Vapnik & Chervonenkis (1964–1991) introduced the **VC dimension** — the largest set size on which a class can produce every possible binary labeling — and proved the bound $P(R[p,f] - R_{\mathrm{emp}}[\mathbf X,\mathbf Y,f] < \alpha) \geq 1-\delta$ for $\alpha \geq c\sqrt{(\mathrm{VC} - \log\delta)/n}$. Linear models on $\mathbb R^d$ have VC dimension $d+1$. The bound is profound theoretically but vacuous for deep networks (which often have VC dimension exceeding $n$ yet generalize anyway) — foreshadowing the [[DoubleDescent|double-descent]] phenomenon.

- **[[CovariateShift|Covariate shift]]: $P(\mathbf{x})$ changes, $P(y\mid\mathbf{x})$ does not.** The natural assumption when $\mathbf x$ *causes* $y$ (image of an animal causes the species label). Classic example: train on photos, test on cartoons of cats and dogs. Correctable by **importance-weighted ERM**: reweight each training example by $\beta_i = p(\mathbf x_i)/q(\mathbf x_i)$, where $q$ is the source distribution and $p$ the target. The ratio is estimated by training a [[LogisticRegression|logistic-regression]] domain classifier to distinguish source from target samples — closing the loop: a binary special case of softmax regression is used to correct softmax regression's distribution-shift bias.

- **[[LabelShift|Label shift]]: $P(y)$ changes, $P(\mathbf{x}\mid y)$ does not.** Natural when $y$ *causes* $\mathbf x$ (disease causes symptoms; the disease prevalence changes but each disease produces the same symptom distribution). Importance weights are $\beta_i = p(y_i)/q(y_i)$ — operating on the low-dimensional label space rather than the high-dimensional input space, which is a major advantage in deep learning. Estimable by inverting the validation-set confusion matrix: $\mathbf C\, p(\mathbf y) = \mu(\hat{\mathbf y})$ where $\mu(\hat{\mathbf y})$ is the average test-set prediction vector.

- **[[ConceptShift|Concept shift]]: the labeling function $P(y\mid\mathbf{x})$ itself changes.** Most commonly slow drift — soft-drink naming conventions across US geography (pop / soda / coke), diagnostic criteria for mental illness over decades, fashion labels, job titles. Rarely correctable principally; usual remedy is to fine-tune the existing model on small batches of fresh labels rather than retrain from scratch. **Nonstationary distributions** (slow drift unaccounted for in training) are concept shift's silent failure mode — Christmas-recommender, post-iPad-launch ad-tech, evolving spam are the chapter's running anecdotes.

- **Empirical risk vs. risk; weighted ERM.** True risk is $\mathbb E_{p(\mathbf x,y)}[l(f(\mathbf x), y)]$, unobservable. **Empirical risk** averages the loss over the training sample — what we actually minimize. Under distribution shift, plain ERM is biased; **weighted ERM** corrects this: $\min_f \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \beta_i\, l(f(\mathbf x_i), y_i)$ with $\beta_i$ chosen to undo the source/target mismatch. **Important caveat**: covariate-shift correction only works when each test-time point had nonzero training-time probability ($q(\mathbf x) > 0$ wherever $p(\mathbf x) > 0$); otherwise the importance weight is infinite and the correction breaks.

- **A taxonomy of learning beyond batch.** The chapter closes with a taxonomy that frames what's later in the book: **batch learning** (train once, deploy, never update — the default assumption), **online learning** (sample-by-sample arrival; predict then receive label), **bandits** (online learning with a finite action set, e.g. ads, A/B testing — special case of [[reinforcementlearning|RL]] with stronger theoretical guarantees), **control** (environment has memory — PID controllers, recommender systems whose users react to recommendations), and **[[reinforcementlearning|reinforcement learning]]** (full game-theoretic setting; chess, Go, autonomous driving). The classifier we trained in this chapter assumes batch learning; the rest of the book progressively relaxes that.

- **Ethics caveat: classifiers in deployment are decision-makers, not predictors.** The chapter's *Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency* closing pivots from technical to ethical: cost-sensitive prediction (some errors cost more), demographic-subgroup welfare, runaway feedback loops in predictive policing, the Oxford-shoes/loan example (introducing a model-based decision changes the data distribution that justified it). Accuracy is "seldom the right measure" once a classifier is deployed as a decision-making tool; the section foreshadows fairness-aware ML treated elsewhere in the wiki ([[islr-seventh-printing]] doesn't cover this; [[pml1-murphy|Murphy]] gives it a chapter).

## Key Quotes

> "Note that the largest coordinate of $\mathbf{o}$ corresponds to the most likely class according to $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$. Moreover, because the softmax operation preserves the ordering among its arguments, we do not need to compute the softmax to determine which class has been assigned the highest probability." — `softmax-regression.md`, The Softmax

> "The idea of a softmax dates back to Gibbs (1902), who adapted ideas from physics. Dating even further back, Boltzmann, the father of modern statistical physics, used this trick to model a distribution over energy states in gas molecules." — `softmax-regression.md`

> "By combining softmax and cross-entropy, we can escape the numerical stability issues altogether. … But instead of passing softmax probabilities into our new loss function, we just pass the logits and compute the softmax and its log all at once inside the cross-entropy loss function, which does smart things like the 'LogSumExp trick'." — `softmax-regression-concise.md`, Softmax Revisited

> "Today, MNIST serves as more of a sanity check than as a benchmark. … As a substitute we will focus our discussion in the coming sections on the qualitatively similar, but much smaller Fashion-MNIST dataset which was released in 2017." — `image-classification-dataset.md`

> "Once information from the test set has leaked to the modeler, it can never be a true test set again in the strictest sense. This problem is called *adaptive overfitting*." — `generalization-classification.md`

> "Absent any assumptions on how $p_S$ and $p_T$ relate to each other, learning a robust classifier is impossible." — `environment-and-distribution-shift.md`, Types of Distribution Shift

> "By introducing our model-based decisions to the environment, we might break the model." — `environment-and-distribution-shift.md`, opener (Oxfords/sneakers loan example)

## Contradictions

- **None direct.** All claims align with [[islr-seventh-printing]] (Ch. 4), [[mml-book]] (Ch. 12), [[pml1-murphy]] (Chs. 9–10), and the existing wiki coverage of softmax / cross-entropy / logistic regression / VC dimension.
- **Framing note** (not a contradiction): D2L's distribution-shift section endorses importance-weighted ERM as a principled correction; the Made With ML productionization material ([[AlibiDetect]] / [[EvidentlyAI]] etc.) emphasizes *detection* and *monitoring* more than *correction*. Different pragmatic emphases of the same theory.
- **Modern caveat** D2L itself flags: VC dimension is "powerless (as straightforwardly applied) for explaining why deep neural networks generalize"; deep nets with VC dimension exceeding $n$ generalize anyway. Consistent with the [[DoubleDescent|double-descent]] caveat already in [[GeneralizationGap]].
