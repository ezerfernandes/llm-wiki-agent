# MML Ch 12 — Classification with Support Vector Machines

Deep-dive of Chapter 12 (book pp. 370–394 = PDF pp. 376–400) of [[mml-book|Mathematics for Machine Learning]] (Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong; Cambridge UP 2020, draft rev. 2024-01-15). This is the **fourth and final of the four ML pillars** — *binary classification* — and the book's last chapter. It closes the arc that opened with [[mml-ch09-linear-regression|Ch 9 (regression)]], [[mml-ch10-dimensionality-reduction-pca|Ch 10 (dimensionality reduction)]], and [[mml-ch11-density-estimation-gmm|Ch 11 (density estimation)]]. Of the four worked examples, the SVM is the one chosen to illustrate the **geometric / loss-function-first** design philosophy (in deliberate contrast to the probabilistic-model-first view of Chs 9 and 11), and the one whose optimization problem has **no analytic solution**, forcing a return to the [[mml-ch07-continuous-optimization|Ch 7]] optimization toolbox.

## Summary

The chapter develops binary classification — predictors $f:\mathbb{R}^D\to\{+1,-1\}$ — through the **support vector machine** (SVM), built up in five layers. (1) §12.1 sets up the **[[SeparatingHyperplane|separating hyperplane]]** $\{\mathbf{x}:\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle+b=0\}$ as the decision boundary, with $\mathbf{w}$ the normal vector, classification by the sign of $f(\mathbf{x})=\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle+b$. (2) §12.2 (Primal SVM) introduces the **[[Margin|margin]]**, derives the key identity $r=1/\|\mathbf{w}\|$, and arrives at the **[[HardMarginSVM|hard-margin SVM]]** $\min\frac12\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$ s.t. $y_n(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_n\rangle+b)\ge1$; then relaxes it to the **[[SoftMarginSVM|soft-margin SVM]]** with [[SlackVariable|slack variables]] $\xi_n$ and box parameter $C$; and shows the soft-margin problem is *equivalent* to unconstrained minimization of the **[[HingeLoss|hinge loss]]** $\max\{0,1-y_nf(\mathbf{x}_n)\}$ plus an $\ell_2$ regularizer — the loss-function-first reading. (3) §12.3 (Dual SVM) applies [[LagrangianDuality|Lagrangian duality]] (Ch 7) to obtain the **[[DualSVM|dual program]]**, a [[QuadraticProgramming|convex QP]] in the multipliers $\boldsymbol\alpha$ whose size scales with the number of *examples* $N$ rather than features $D$; the **representer theorem** $\mathbf{w}=\sum_n\alpha_ny_n\mathbf{x}_n$ and **complementary slackness** ([[KKTConditions|KKT]]) expose the **[[SupportVector|support vectors]]** ($\alpha_n>0$). A second geometric reading casts the dual as finding the nearest points of the two class **convex hulls**. (4) §12.4 (Kernels) observes the dual depends on examples *only through inner products*, so replacing $\langle\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j\rangle$ with a **[[KernelFunction|kernel]]** $k(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j)=\langle\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}_i),\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}_j)\rangle_\mathcal{H}$ — the **[[KernelTrick|kernel trick]]** — lifts the linear classifier to a nonlinear one *without ever computing* $\boldsymbol\phi$; kernels must be symmetric positive-semidefinite (every [[GramMatrix|kernel/Gram matrix]] $\mathbf{K}$ is PSD), the polynomial and Gaussian [[RBFKernel|RBF]] kernels being the workhorses. (5) §12.5 (Numerical Solution) expresses the SVM two ways for solvers: the unconstrained hinge-loss problem solved by **subgradient descent / SGD** (the hinge is non-differentiable only at the kink $t=1$), and the constrained primal and dual cast as **standard-form quadratic programs** (Eqs. 12.55–12.57) for off-the-shelf QP solvers (LIBSVM / SVMlight). §12.6 surveys alternatives (perceptron, logistic regression, Fisher discriminant, naive Bayes, random forest), the ERM grounding, and the probabilistic-calibration route (Platt scaling, logistic regression as the calibrated cousin, Gaussian-process classification).

## Key Claims

- **The SVM is the geometric / loss-first counterpart to probabilistic linear regression.** "While in Chapter 9 we considered the machine learning problem in terms of probabilistic models … here we will consider an alternative approach where we reason geometrically about the machine learning task" (§12, p. 371). The maximum-likelihood view *proposes a probabilistic model and derives an optimization problem from it*; the SVM view *starts by designing a function (a loss) to be optimized on training data, following empirical risk minimization* ([[EmpiricalRiskMinimization|ERM]], §8.2). The chapter pairs with Ch 10 (PCA also derived from geometric principles) against the probabilistic Chs 9 & 11. (§12, pp. 370–371)
- **Two reasons the SVM is chosen to teach classification** (§12, p. 371): it gives a *geometric* way to think about supervised learning (relying on Ch 2 inner products and Ch 3 projections), and — unlike Ch 9 — its optimization problem *does not admit an analytic solution*, so it exercises the Ch 7 optimization tools.
- **Binary classification labels are $\{+1,-1\}$, with no intuitive "positivity"** (§12, p. 370). The predictor is $f:\mathbb{R}^D\to\{+1,-1\}$ (Eq. 12.1). The $\pm1$ encoding is mathematically convenient for the SVM (it lets the two correctness conditions collapse into one inequality); probabilistic models prefer $\{0,1\}$.
- **The separating hyperplane is the decision boundary, and $\mathbf{w}$ is its normal vector** (§12.1). $f(\mathbf{x})=\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle+b$ (Eq. 12.2b); the hyperplane is $\{\mathbf{x}:f(\mathbf{x})=0\}$ (Eq. 12.3), an affine subspace of dimension $D-1$ (§2.8). For any two points $\mathbf{x}_a,\mathbf{x}_b$ on it, $f(\mathbf{x}_a)-f(\mathbf{x}_b)=\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_a-\mathbf{x}_b\rangle=0$, so $\mathbf{w}\perp$ the hyperplane (Eqs. 12.4a–b). Classify $\mathbf{x}_\text{test}$ as $+1$ iff $f(\mathbf{x}_\text{test})\ge0$. The two label-correctness conditions $\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_n\rangle+b\ge0$ (when $y_n=+1$, Eq. 12.5) and $<0$ (when $y_n=-1$, Eq. 12.6) combine into the single inequality $y_n(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_n\rangle+b)\ge0$ (Eq. 12.7). (§12.1, pp. 372–373)
- **Linearly separable data admit infinitely many separators; the margin picks a unique one** (§12.2, Fig. 12.3, p. 374). The SVM chooses the separating hyperplane that *maximizes the margin* — the distance to the closest examples. "A classifier with large margin turns out to generalize well (Steinwart and Christmann, 2008)" (§12.2 marginal, p. 374).
- **The margin equals $r=1/\|\mathbf{w}\|$** (§12.2.1, Eq. 12.14, p. 377). Derivation: a point $\mathbf{x}_a$ at distance $r$ projects to $\mathbf{x}_a'$ on the hyperplane via $\mathbf{x}_a=\mathbf{x}_a'+r\frac{\mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}$ (Eq. 12.8, using that $\mathbf{w}$ is the orthogonal direction, §3.8). Substituting into $\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_a'\rangle+b=0$ (Eq. 12.11) and using $\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}\rangle=\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$ (Eq. 3.16) plus the scale fix $\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_a\rangle+b=1$ gives $r=1/\|\mathbf{w}\|$. The distance $r$ "is just a scaling of the vector $\mathbf{w}$" (§12.2.1, p. 375).
- **Margin maximization has two equivalent formulations** (§12.2.1 vs §12.2.2), proven equal in **Theorem 12.1** (§12.2.3). *Formulation 1* (normalize $\|\mathbf{w}\|=1$, maximize $r$): $\max_{\mathbf{w},b,r}r$ s.t. $y_n(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_n\rangle+b)\ge r$, $\|\mathbf{w}\|=1$, $r>0$ (Eq. 12.10/12.20). *Formulation 2* (fix scale so margin $=1$, minimize $\frac12\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$): $\min_{\mathbf{w},b}\frac12\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$ s.t. $y_n(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_n\rangle+b)\ge1$ (Eq. 12.21). The proof reparametrizes $\mathbf{w}'/\|\mathbf{w}'\|$, divides the constraint by $r$, sets $\mathbf{w}''=\mathbf{w}'/(\|\mathbf{w}'\|r)$ so $\|\mathbf{w}''\|=1/r$ (Eq. 12.24), and observes maximizing $1/\|\mathbf{w}''\|^2$ equals minimizing $\frac12\|\mathbf{w}''\|^2$. (§12.2.3, pp. 378–379)
- **Hard-margin SVM** (§12.2, Eqs. 12.18–12.19, p. 377): $\min_{\mathbf{w},b}\frac12\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$ s.t. $y_n(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_n\rangle+b)\ge1$ for all $n$. "Hard" = no margin violations allowed. The $\frac12$ "does not affect the optimal $\mathbf{w},b$ but yields a tidier form when we compute the gradient." It is a **convex quadratic program** (margin note, p. 377) → see [[HardMarginSVM]].
- **Soft-margin SVM** (§12.2.4, Eqs. 12.26a–c, p. 380): for non-separable data, introduce a [[SlackVariable|slack variable]] $\xi_n\ge0$ per example that lets it sit inside the margin or on the wrong side: $\min_{\mathbf{w},b,\boldsymbol\xi}\frac12\|\mathbf{w}\|^2+C\sum_n\xi_n$ s.t. $y_n(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_n\rangle+b)\ge1-\xi_n$, $\xi_n\ge0$. The **regularization parameter** $C>0$ trades off margin size against total slack; *large $C$ = low regularization* (slack weighted heavily, priority to correct classification). Also called the **$C$-SVM**. Note $b$ is *not* regularized (only $\mathbf{w}$), which complicates theory and efficiency (Steinwart & Christmann 2008; Fan et al. 2008). (§12.2.4, p. 380) → see [[SoftMarginSVM]].
- **The hinge loss makes the SVM a loss-function-first method** (§12.2.5, Eq. 12.28, p. 381). With hyperplanes as the hypothesis class (Eq. 12.27), the margin term $\frac12\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$ is the **regularizer** and the loss is the **hinge loss** $\ell(t)=\max\{0,1-t\}$ where $t=yf(\mathbf{x})=y(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle+b)$. It is $0$ when $t\ge1$ (correct and beyond the margin), positive when $0<t<1$ (correct but inside the margin), and grows linearly when $t<0$ (wrong side). The hinge loss is a **convex upper bound on the zero-one loss** $\mathbf{1}(f(\mathbf{x}_n)\ne y_n)$ (Fig. 12.8, p. 382), which is otherwise combinatorial and hard to optimize. The **regularized empirical risk** view (Eq. 12.31, p. 382): $\min_{\mathbf{w},b}\frac12\|\mathbf{w}\|^2+C\sum_n\max\{0,1-y_n(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_n\rangle+b)\}$. This is *equivalent* to the soft-margin SVM (12.26a) because minimizing a hinge over $t$ equals minimizing a slack $\xi$ with constraints $\xi\ge0,\ \xi\ge1-t$ (Eqs. 12.32–12.33). The hard-margin loss is the limit $\ell(t)=0$ if $t\ge1$ else $\infty$ (Eq. 12.30). → see [[HingeLoss]].
- **Margin maximization = $\ell_2$-regularization** (§12.2.5, p. 382). "Recall from Section 12.2.4 that the term $\frac12\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$ arises directly from the margin. In other words, margin maximization can be interpreted as regularization." This is the dual reading of the same object — geometry (large margin) and statistics (small-norm weights / capacity control) coincide.
- **The dual SVM is independent of the number of features** (§12.3, p. 383). The primal SVM's parameters ($\mathbf{w}$) scale with the feature dimension $D$; the dual scales with the number of examples $N$ — useful when $D\gg N$, and "the dual SVM also has the additional advantage that it easily allows kernels to be applied." The subsection "is essentially an application of convex duality, which we discussed in Section 7.2."
- **The Lagrangian, its stationarity conditions, and the representer theorem** (§12.3.1). With multipliers $\alpha_n\ge0$ (for the classification constraint 12.26b) and $\gamma_n\ge0$ (for slack-nonnegativity 12.26c), the [[Lagrangian]] is Eq. 12.34. Setting the partial derivatives to zero gives: $\partial_\mathbf{w}\mathfrak{L}=\mathbf{0}\Rightarrow\mathbf{w}=\sum_n\alpha_ny_n\mathbf{x}_n$ (Eq. 12.38 — a particular instance of the **representer theorem**, Kimeldorf & Wahba 1970: the optimal weight is a linear combination of the training examples, lying in their span, §2.6.1); $\partial_b\mathfrak{L}=\mathbf{0}\Rightarrow\sum_n\alpha_ny_n=0$ (Eq. 12.36); $\partial_{\xi_n}\mathfrak{L}=\mathbf{0}\Rightarrow C-\alpha_n-\gamma_n=0$ (Eq. 12.37), which with $\gamma_n\ge0$ forces $\alpha_n\le C$. (§12.3.1, pp. 383–384)
- **Support vectors are the examples with $\alpha_n>0$; complementary slackness explains the name** (§12.3.1 Remark, p. 384). "The examples $\mathbf{x}_n$, for which the corresponding parameters $\alpha_n=0$, do not contribute to the solution $\mathbf{w}$ at all. The other examples, where $\alpha_n>0$, are called *support vectors* since they 'support' the hyperplane." This is precisely [[KKTConditions|KKT complementary slackness]] at work — only active margin constraints carry weight. → see [[SupportVector]].
- **The dual program** (§12.3.1, Eq. 12.41, p. 385): substituting Eq. 12.38 back into the Lagrangian eliminates $\mathbf{w}$, $b$ (via Eq. 12.36), and the slack terms (via Eq. 12.37), leaving $\min_{\boldsymbol\alpha}\frac12\sum_{i,j}y_iy_j\alpha_i\alpha_j\langle\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j\rangle-\sum_i\alpha_i$ s.t. $\sum_iy_i\alpha_i=0$ and $0\le\alpha_i\le C$. The constraints $0\le\alpha_i\le C$ are **box constraints** (axis-aligned box, efficient for solvers). The objective depends on the data *only through the inner products* $\langle\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j\rangle$ — the hook for kernels. → see [[DualSVM]].
- **Recovering the primal bias $b$** (§12.3.1, Eq. 12.42, p. 385): once $\boldsymbol\alpha$ is found, $\mathbf{w}^*=\sum_n\alpha_ny_n\mathbf{x}_n$ (representer theorem); for a support vector exactly on the margin ($\langle\mathbf{w}^*,\mathbf{x}_n\rangle+b=y_n$), $b^*=y_n-\langle\mathbf{w}^*,\mathbf{x}_n\rangle$. In practice average/median over support vectors. Examples *exactly on the margin* satisfy $0<\alpha_n<C$ (strictly inside the box) — a KKT consequence (margin note, p. 385).
- **Dual = nearest points of the two convex hulls** (§12.3.2, p. 386–388). A third interpretation: build the **[[ConvexHull|convex hull]]** of each class (Eq. 12.43, $\mathrm{conv}(\mathbf{X})=\{\sum_n\alpha_n\mathbf{x}_n:\sum_n\alpha_n=1,\alpha_n\ge0\}$); pick the closest points $\mathbf{c}$ (positive hull) and $\mathbf{d}$ (negative hull); the separating hyperplane bisects $\mathbf{w}:=\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{d}$ (Eq. 12.44). Minimizing $\frac12\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$ (Eq. 12.45) with $\mathbf{c}=\sum_{n:y_n=+1}\alpha_n^+\mathbf{x}_n$, $\mathbf{d}=\sum_{n:y_n=-1}\alpha_n^-\mathbf{x}_n$ recovers the same dual; the hull-coefficient sums $\sum\alpha_n^+=\sum\alpha_n^-=1$ imply $\sum_ny_n\alpha_n=0$ (Eqs. 12.49–12.51). The soft-margin dual corresponds to the **reduced hull** (an upper bound on $\boldsymbol\alpha$ shrinks the hull; Bennett & Bredensteiner 2000a/b).
- **Kernels: lift to nonlinear classifiers without computing $\boldsymbol\phi$** (§12.4, Eq. 12.52, p. 388). The dual contains inner products *only between examples*, never between examples and parameters; so replacing each $\langle\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j\rangle$ by a similarity $k(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j)=\langle\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}_i),\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}_j)\rangle_\mathcal{H}$ — a **kernel**, a function $k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}$ for which a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ and feature map $\boldsymbol\phi$ exist — runs the linear SVM in feature space implicitly. This is the **[[KernelTrick|kernel trick]]** (Schölkopf & Smola 2002): the *choice of classifier* and the *choice of feature representation* become independent. "While the decision boundary is nonlinear, the underlying problem being solved is for a linear separating hyperplane (albeit with a nonlinear kernel)" (Fig. 12.10 caption, p. 389).
- **Kernels must be symmetric positive-semidefinite (Mercer / PSD condition)** (§12.4, Eq. 12.53, p. 389). The matrix $\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}$ with $K_{ij}=k(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j)$ is the **[[GramMatrix|Gram matrix / kernel matrix]]**, and every valid kernel produces a symmetric PSD matrix: $\forall\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^N:\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{K}\mathbf{z}\ge0$ (§3.2.3). There is a unique reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) per kernel (Aronszajn 1950); $\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x})=k(\cdot,\mathbf{x})$ is the **canonical feature map**. → see [[KernelFunction|Kernel]].
- **Common kernels: polynomial, RBF/Gaussian, rational quadratic** (§12.4, p. 389–390). The polynomial kernel is cheap to evaluate even when the explicit monomial expansion is huge ("one multiplication per input dimension"); the **Gaussian RBF kernel** has an *infinite-dimensional* feature space that "cannot be explicitly represented" yet whose similarities are computed in closed form. Kernels need not act on $\mathbb{R}^D$ — $\boldsymbol\phi$ and $k$ can be defined on sets, sequences, strings, graphs, and distributions (Ben-Hur et al. 2008; Gärtner 2008). → see [[RBFKernel]].
- **Three meanings of "kernel"** (§12.4 Remark, p. 390): the RKHS kernel of *this* chapter; the linear-algebra *kernel = null space* (§2.7.3); and the smoothing *kernel* of kernel density estimation (§11.5). Distinct notions sharing a name.
- **Numerical solution: subgradient on the hinge, or QP solvers** (§12.5, pp. 390–392). The hinge-loss objective (12.31) is convex but non-differentiable at the kink $t=1$; the **subgradient** is $g(t)=-1$ ($t<1$), $[-1,0]$ ($t=1$), $0$ ($t>1$) (Eq. 12.54), enabling the Ch 7.1 gradient methods (SGD). Alternatively the primal (Eq. 12.55–12.56) and dual (Eq. 12.57, $\min_{\boldsymbol\alpha}\frac12\boldsymbol\alpha^\top\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{Y}\boldsymbol\alpha-\mathbf{1}^\top\boldsymbol\alpha$) are cast in **standard quadratic-programming form** (§7.3.2) for off-the-shelf convex-QP solvers — primal has $D{+}1{+}N$ variables, dual has $N$. The two flagship implementations are **LIBSVM** (Chang & Lin 2011, open source) and **SVMlight** (Joachims 1999). The equality constraint $\sum y_i\alpha_i=0$ is encoded as two inequalities (Eq. 12.58). "The approach presented here, expressing the SVM problem in standard convex optimization form, is not often used in practice."
- **Further reading: the SVM is one of many classifiers; calibration bridges back to probability** (§12.6, pp. 392–394). Alternatives: perceptron, logistic regression, Fisher discriminant, nearest neighbor, naive Bayes, random forest (Bishop 2006; Murphy 2012). A binary classifier has three possible outputs: the raw real-valued *score* (rankable; classification = thresholding); the squashed $[0,1]$ value (e.g. via a **sigmoid**); and the final $\{+1,-1\}$ decision. The SVM "does not naturally lend itself to a probabilistic interpretation"; converting its score to a calibrated $P(Y=1\mid\mathbf{x})$ needs a separate step (Platt 2000; Zadrozny & Elkan 2001). The maximum-likelihood method with a *well-calibrated* squashing is **logistic regression** (a generalized linear model); the Bayesian version adds a prior (Bayesian logistic regression); latent-function priors yield **Gaussian-process classification** (Rasmussen & Williams 2006). The hinge loss has three equivalent forms (Eqs. 12.28 / 12.29 / 12.33), each convenient for a different purpose (loss-comparison / subgradients / QP).

## Key Quotes

> "The SVM view of machine learning is subtly different from the maximum likelihood view of Chapter 9. The maximum likelihood view proposes a model based on a probabilistic view of the data distribution, from which an optimization problem is derived. In contrast, the SVM view starts by designing a particular function that is to be optimized during training, based on geometric intuitions." — §12, p. 371

The chapter's thesis: two equally valid framings of supervised ML — probabilistic (Chs 9, 11) and geometric / loss-first (Chs 10, 12). The SVM is the canonical loss-first classifier.

> "We have seen something similar already in Chapter 10, where we derived PCA from geometric principles. In the SVM case, we start by designing a loss function that is to be minimized on training data, following the principles of empirical risk minimization (Section 8.2)." — §12, p. 371

Explicitly grounds the SVM in [[EmpiricalRiskMinimization|ERM]] (§8.2) and pairs it with PCA as the two geometric pillars.

> "A classifier with large margin turns out to generalize well (Steinwart and Christmann, 2008)." — §12.2 marginal, p. 374

The generalization motivation for maximizing the margin.

> "Equation (12.18) is known as the hard margin SVM. The reason for the expression 'hard' is because the formulation does not allow for any violations of the margin condition." — §12.2, p. 377

> "The model that allows for some classification errors is called the soft margin SVM. … The key geometric idea is to introduce a slack variable $\xi_n$ corresponding to each example–label pair $(\mathbf{x}_n,y_n)$ that allows a particular example to be within the margin or even on the wrong side of the hyperplane." — §12.2.4, pp. 379–380

> "Here a large value of $C$ implies low regularization, as we give the slack variables larger weight, hence giving higher priority to examples that do not lie on the correct side of the margin." — §12.2.4, p. 380

The $C$ trade-off — and the warning that SVM's $C$ is the *inverse* of the usual numerical-optimization regularization weight.

> "An equivalent way to derive (12.26a) is to use the hinge loss $\ell(t)=\max\{0,1-t\}$ where $t=yf(\mathbf{x})=y(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle+b)$." — §12.2.5, p. 381

> "The first term in (12.31) is called the regularization term or the regularizer …, and the second term is called the loss term or the error term. Recall from Section 12.2.4 that the term $\frac12\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$ arises directly from the margin. In other words, margin maximization can be interpreted as regularization." — §12.2.5, p. 382

The identity that ties geometry (margin) to statistics (regularization).

> "The representer theorem (12.38) also provides an explanation of the name 'support vector machine.' The examples $\mathbf{x}_n$, for which the corresponding parameters $\alpha_n=0$, do not contribute to the solution $\mathbf{w}$ at all. The other examples, where $\alpha_n>0$, are called support vectors since they 'support' the hyperplane." — §12.3.1 Remark, p. 384

The origin of the method's name, via complementary slackness.

> "For a certain class of similarity functions, called kernels, the similarity function implicitly defines a non-linear feature map $\boldsymbol\phi(\cdot)$. Kernels are by definition functions $k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}$ for which there exists a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ and $\boldsymbol\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H}$ a feature map such that $k(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j)=\langle\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}_i),\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}_j)\rangle_\mathcal{H}$." — §12.4, p. 388 (Eq. 12.52)

The kernel definition.

> "The generalization from an inner product to a kernel function (12.52) is known as the kernel trick … as it hides away the explicit non-linear feature map." — §12.4, p. 389

> "Note that we are still solving for hyperplanes, that is, the hypothesis class of functions are still linear. The non-linear surfaces are due to the kernel function." — §12.4, p. 390

The crucial conceptual point: nonlinearity lives entirely in the kernel, not in the classifier.

> "The SVM is a binary classifier that does not naturally lend itself to a probabilistic interpretation. There are several approaches for converting the raw output of the linear function (the score) into a calibrated class probability estimate ($P(Y=1\mid X=\mathbf{x})$) that involve an additional calibration step." — §12.6, pp. 393–394

The flip side of the loss-first design: no built-in probabilities (contrast Chs 9, 11).

## Contradictions / notational quirks

- **No mathematical contradictions with existing wiki content** — Ch 12 *confirms and completes* the SVM/kernel coverage that [[SupportVectorMachine]], [[SeparatingHyperplane]], [[Margin]], and [[KernelTrick]] had already attributed to "[[mml-book]] Ch 12" before this per-chapter ingest. This page canonicalizes those citations to the per-chapter slug `mml-ch12-classification-svm` and adds page-anchored, verbatim detail. All additions are additive.
- **$C$ is an *inverse* regularization weight in SVM convention** (§12.2.4 margin, p. 380): "in many books on numerical optimization, the regularization parameter is multiplied with [the regularizer]. This is in contrast to our formulation … Here a large value of $C$ implies *low* regularization." The hinge-loss reading (Eq. 12.31) puts $C$ on the *loss* term, so $C$ behaves like an *inverse* of the usual $\lambda$ on the regularizer — the same $1/(2C)\leftrightarrow\lambda$ bookkeeping noted on [[SupportVectorMachine]].
- **The bias $b$ is unregularized** (§12.2.4 Remark, p. 380): the soft-margin objective regularizes $\mathbf{w}$ but *not* $b$; "the unregularized term $b$ complicates theoretical analysis and decreases computational efficiency" (Steinwart & Christmann 2008; Fan et al. 2008). Hence the "SVM bias-term conspiracy" footnote (p. 385) on recovering $b^*$.
- **SVM notation $\alpha,\gamma$ vs Ch 7's $\lambda$** (§12.3.1 margin, p. 383): "In Chapter 7 we used $\lambda$ as Lagrange multipliers. In this section, we follow the notation commonly chosen in the SVM literature, and use $\alpha$ and $\gamma$." Same Lagrangian-duality machinery, different symbols.
- **MML's $\|\mathbf{w}\|=1$ scaling differs from the standard SVM presentation** (§12.2.1 Remark, p. 375): "our definition of $\|\mathbf{w}\|=1$ is different from the standard presentation if the SVM was the one provided by Schölkopf and Smola (2002)"; §12.2.3 (Theorem 12.1) proves the equivalence with the more common "margin $=1$" scaling.
- **Three distinct meanings of "kernel"** (§12.4 Remark, p. 390): RKHS kernel (this chapter) vs linear-algebra kernel/null-space (§2.7.3) vs KDE smoothing kernel (§11.5). On the wiki, the bare [[Kernel]] page is the *operating-system* kernel (disambiguated); the ML kernel concept lives on [[KernelFunction]] / [[KernelTrick]] — so this ingest enriches [[KernelFunction]] (not [[Kernel]]) with the MML §12.4 view.
- **The hinge loss has three equivalent forms** (Eqs. 12.28 max-form / 12.29 two-piece / 12.33 slack-with-constraints, noted in §12.6, p. 393) — each convenient for a different task (loss-comparison / subgradient computation / casting as a QP). Not a contradiction, but a notational multiplicity worth recording on [[HingeLoss]].
