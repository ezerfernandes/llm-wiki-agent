## Summary

[[AstonZhang|Zhang]], [[ZacharyLipton|Lipton]], [[MuLi|Li]] & [[AlexanderSmola|Smola]]'s seven-section RNN chapter — D2L's pivot from fixed-length tabular / image inputs to *sequentially structured* data. Walks the full pipeline: sequence math + $k$-step-ahead extrapolation pathology (§sequence) → text preprocessing (load / tokenize / vocabulary / numeric corpus) and the Zipfian distribution of word-frequencies (§text-sequence) → the joint-probability factorization $P(x_1,\ldots,x_T)=\prod_t P(x_t \mid x_{<t})$, [[NGram|$n$-gram]] approximations, [[LaplaceSmoothing|Laplace smoothing]], and [[Perplexity]] as exponentiated average cross-entropy (§language-model) → the [[RNN|RNN]] recurrence $\mathbf{H}_t = \phi(\mathbf{X}_t \mathbf{W}_\textrm{xh} + \mathbf{H}_{t-1} \mathbf{W}_\textrm{hh} + \mathbf{b}_\textrm{h})$ and its identity with a single matmul on the concatenated $[\mathbf{X}_t,\mathbf{H}_{t-1}]$ (§rnn) → a from-scratch character-level RNN language model on H. G. Wells' *The Time Machine* with one-hot inputs, softmax/cross-entropy output, and the per-iteration **gradient-clipping** $\mathbf{g}\leftarrow\min(1,\theta/\|\mathbf{g}\|)\mathbf{g}$ heuristic (§rnn-scratch) → high-level-API parity using framework `nn.RNN` / `SimpleRNN` (§rnn-concise) → and the math of [[Backpropagation|BPTT]] (§bptt), expanding $\partial L/\partial \mathbf{h}_t$ into a sum of $(\mathbf{W}_\textrm{hh}^\top)^{T-i}$ terms whose eigenvalue powers manifest as [[VanishingGradient|vanishing]] / [[ExplodingGradient|exploding]] gradients. The chapter is explicitly a *bridge* — it ends pointing forward to gated variants ([[LSTM]] / [[GRU]]) that mitigate vanishing gradients, and to [[transformer|Transformers]] that have "ceded considerable market share" to.

## Key Claims

- **The chapter is as much about sequence modeling as it is about RNNs.** "The stories of RNNs and of sequence modeling are inextricably linked, and this is as much a chapter about the ABCs of sequence modeling problems as it is a chapter about RNNs." Key insight that paved the way for the sequence-modeling revolution: inputs/targets that cannot easily be fixed-length vectors *can* be represented as varying-length sequences of fixed-length vectors (documents → sequences of words; medical records → encounter/medication sequences; videos → image sequences). RNNs rose to prominence in the 2010s on handwriting recognition (Graves 2008), machine translation (Sutskever, Vinyals, Le 2014), and medical diagnoses (Lipton & Kale 2016).
- **[[AutoregressiveModel|Autoregressive models]] regress a signal on its own past — but the input length varies with $t$.** D2L motivates with the FTSE 100 example: estimating $P(x_t \mid x_{t-1},\ldots,x_1)$. Two recurring strategies: (i) **truncate the history** to a window of length $\tau$ — guarantees fixed-length inputs once $t > \tau$ and matches any standard fixed-vector model (the [[MarkovModel|Markov-condition]] case); (ii) **maintain a latent state** $h_t = g(h_{t-1}, x_{t-1})$ that summarizes the past (the latent-autoregressive case — the conceptual ancestor of the RNN [[HiddenState|hidden state]]).
- **$k$-step-ahead prediction errors compound; multistep extrapolation is fundamentally harder than interpolation.** Plugging earlier predictions back as inputs causes errors to grow $\epsilon_2 \approx \bar\epsilon + c\epsilon_1$. In the synthetic sine demo, 1- and 4-step-ahead predictions track ground truth; 16- and 64-step-ahead predictions decay to a constant. "Always respect the temporal order of the data when training, i.e., never train on future data." Weather-forecast analogy: 24-hour forecasts are accurate, beyond-week forecasts degrade rapidly.
- **Markov models trade history for parameter count.** Whenever a sequence satisfies the [[MarkovCondition|Markov condition]] (the future is conditionally independent of the past given the recent history), the joint reduces to a product $P(x_1)\prod_{t\geq 2} P(x_t\mid x_{t-1},\ldots,x_{t-k})$. With discrete tokens, a "true" Markov model is just a relative-frequency count of $n$-grams; the most-likely sequence is then solvable by dynamic programming. Real text *continues* to gain information with more context, but the gains diminish rapidly — "even today's massive RNN- and Transformer-based language models seldom incorporate more than thousands of words of context."
- **Forward-direction (left-to-right) factorization is preferred for causal reasons.** Right-to-left or random-order factorizations of $P(x_1,\ldots,x_T)$ are equally valid in principle, but left-to-right is preferred because (i) it matches how humans read and predict next-word, (ii) it lets a single LM assign probabilities to arbitrarily-long sequences via incremental multiplication, and (iii) predicting adjacent words is statistically easier than predicting distant ones — and for causally-structured data (where $x_{t+1} = f(x_t) + \epsilon$ but the converse is not true; Hoyer / Janzing / Mooij et al. 2009), the forward direction is the *easier* prediction problem. The book by Peters / Janzing / Schölkopf (2017) treats this in depth.
- **Text-preprocessing pipeline = load → tokenize → vocabulary → numeric corpus.** D2L's `TimeMachine` data class operationalizes the pipeline on H. G. Wells' *The Time Machine* (~30 K words; SHA-1-pinned download). Preprocess via regex `[^A-Za-z]+ → ` + lowercase. **Tokens** are the atomic units; the choice between *character* (~30 unique tokens for English text) and *word* (10s–100s of thousands) tokenization is a design choice that trades vocabulary size against sequence length. **Vocabulary** maps each unique token to a numerical index, drops rare tokens via `min_freq`, reserves an `<unk>` slot for OOV. The chapter then character-tokenizes for the subsequent RNN training because it simplifies vocabulary.
- **Word frequencies follow Zipf's law — a power-law distribution that breaks naive $n$-gram language models.** Empirically on *The Time Machine*: the 10th most-frequent word is less than 1/5 as common as the most popular; log-log frequency-rank is nearly linear: $n_i \propto i^{-\alpha}$. Bigrams and trigrams *also* follow Zipf's law with smaller exponents, and most $n$-grams occur very rarely. This is what makes pure-counting language models (with [[LaplaceSmoothing|Laplace smoothing]]) inadequate at scale and motivates neural language modeling.
- **Language models estimate $P(x_1,\ldots,x_T) = \prod_t P(x_t\mid x_{<t})$ and enable sample generation, likelihood evaluation, and ambiguity resolution.** "To recognize speech" vs "to wreck a nice beach": a competent LM rejects the second. "Dog bites man" is much more frequent than "man bites dog." Generation: simply draw $x_t \sim P(x_t \mid x_{t-1},\ldots,x_1)$ token by token. Scaling note (forward reference to Corpus II): "large language models form the basis of state-of-the-art systems across diverse tasks."
- **[[Perplexity]] = $\exp(\frac{1}{n}\sum_t -\log P(x_t \mid x_{<t}))$ — the geometric mean of inverse next-token probabilities.** Information-theoretic interpretation: cross-entropy averaged over tokens (length-comparable across documents of different sizes). Bounds: perplexity = 1 for a perfect deterministic predictor; +∞ for a model that assigns probability 0 to the true token; $|\mathcal{V}|$ for a uniform predictor (the trivial upper bound any useful model must beat). "For historical reasons, scientists in natural language processing prefer to use a quantity called *perplexity*."
- **Partitioning a corpus into minibatches: random offset $d$ + $m=\lfloor(T-d)/n\rfloor$ length-$n$ subsequences; targets are inputs shifted by one token.** Standard scheme for [[TeacherForcing|teacher-forcing]] training: the model sees $\mathbf{x}_t = [x_t,\ldots,x_{t+n-1}]$ and is asked to predict $\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = [x_{t+1},\ldots,x_{t+n}]$ at *every* time step in parallel. Random $d$ per epoch lets training cover all length-$n$ subsequences over multiple epochs.
- **The RNN recurrence is just an MLP with one extra term.** $\mathbf{H}_t = \phi(\mathbf{X}_t \mathbf{W}_\textrm{xh} + \mathbf{H}_{t-1} \mathbf{W}_\textrm{hh} + \mathbf{b}_\textrm{h})$, output $\mathbf{O}_t = \mathbf{H}_t \mathbf{W}_\textrm{hq} + \mathbf{b}_\textrm{q}$. The added $\mathbf{H}_{t-1}\mathbf{W}_\textrm{hh}$ term makes the hidden state a function of all earlier inputs. Crucially: parameter count is *independent of sequence length* — the same $(\mathbf{W}_\textrm{xh}, \mathbf{W}_\textrm{hh}, \mathbf{b}_\textrm{h}, \mathbf{W}_\textrm{hq}, \mathbf{b}_\textrm{q})$ are applied at every time step (weight-tied across time). D2L proves algebraically and demonstrates numerically that $\mathbf{X}_t\mathbf{W}_\textrm{xh} + \mathbf{H}_{t-1}\mathbf{W}_\textrm{hh}$ equals a single matmul on the concatenated $[\mathbf{X}_t,\mathbf{H}_{t-1}]\cdot[\mathbf{W}_\textrm{xh};\mathbf{W}_\textrm{hh}]$ — the implementation hint behind the [[RecurrentLayer|recurrent layer]] kernels in every framework.
- **Hidden states ≠ hidden layers.** Both are "hidden from view," but a hidden *layer* is a fixed-shape intermediate computation in the input-to-output path of a single forward pass, whereas a hidden *state* is technically an *input* to whatever happens at a given time step — it can only be computed by looking at data at previous time steps. The hidden state is the network's *memory*.
- **Character-level language models tokenize at the character level for vocabulary-size convenience.** D2L's worked example: tokenize "machine" as `['m','a','c','h','i','n','e']`, train an RNN to predict the next character at every time step (input "machin", target "achine"). Bengio, Ducharme, Vincent et al. (2003) is cited as the first proposal to use a neural network for language modeling. Each token is one-hot-encoded; minibatches have shape `(batch_size, num_steps)`; transposed before the RNN loop so the outer axis is time. Training uses softmax + cross-entropy + perplexity tracking. Compared to word-level LMs, character-level LMs gain a tiny vocabulary at the cost of longer sequences and more difficult long-range dependencies.
- **[[BPTT|Backpropagation through time]] unrolls the RNN and applies the chain rule across time steps (Werbos 1990).** The unrolled RNN is a feedforward network with weight tying across time. Gradients with respect to a tied parameter are *summed* over all places it occurs — exactly like the weight-sharing handled in CNNs. For a $T$-step sequence, gradient computation involves $\mathcal{O}(T)$ matrix-products; an input at step 1 passes through ~1000 matrix products for a 1000-token sequence both forward *and* backward.
- **The closed-form BPTT gradient exposes the vanishing/exploding-gradients math.** $\partial L/\partial \mathbf{h}_t = \sum_{i=t}^T (\mathbf{W}_\textrm{hh}^\top)^{T-i}\mathbf{W}_\textrm{qh}^\top \partial L/\partial \mathbf{o}_{T+t-i}$ — a sum of *powers* of $\mathbf{W}_\textrm{hh}^\top$. Eigenvalues $|\lambda|<1$ shrink toward zero (vanishing); $|\lambda|>1$ blow up (exploding). This is the canonical analysis that LSTM / GRU / Transformer architectures were designed to mitigate.
- **Three BPTT truncation strategies: full / regular / randomized.** *Full computation* is computationally infeasible and unstable (butterfly-effect amplification of initial-condition perturbations). *Regular truncation* — terminating the sum at $\partial h_{t-\tau}/\partial w_\textrm{h}$ — is what frameworks actually do (Jaeger 2002, "truncated BPTT"); biases the estimator toward simpler, more stable models, providing a desirable regularization side-effect. *Randomized truncation* (Tallec & Ollivier 2017) introduces a random $\xi_t$ that terminates the chain with probability $1-\pi_t$ — unbiased in expectation but *empirically does no better than regular truncation* (variance hurts more than fidelity helps, and short-range models are what we want anyway).
- **Gradient clipping is the standard exploding-gradient defense.** $\mathbf{g}\leftarrow\min(1,\theta/\|\mathbf{g}\|)\mathbf{g}$ — project the (flattened, all-parameter-concatenated) gradient onto a ball of radius $\theta$. Preserves direction, caps magnitude. "It is a hack. Gradient clipping means that we are not always following the true gradient and it is hard to reason analytically about the possible side effects. However, it is a very useful hack, and is widely adopted in RNN implementations in most deep learning frameworks." D2L trains the character-level RNN with `gradient_clip_val=1`.
- **High-level RNN APIs match from-scratch perplexity at fraction of the runtime.** `nn.RNN` (PyTorch), `tf.keras.layers.SimpleRNN`, MXNet `rnn.RNN` — all expose the same `(input, hidden) → (outputs, new_hidden)` interface. The chapter trains the same character-level *Time Machine* LM at `num_steps=32`, `num_hiddens=32`, `batch_size=1024`, `max_epochs=100`. Flax (JAX) does not provide an RNNCell as of writing; LSTM / GRU are available.

## Key Quotes

> "Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) are deep learning models that capture the dynamics of sequences via *recurrent* connections, which can be thought of as cycles in the network of nodes. ... Recurrent neural networks are *unrolled* across time steps (or sequence steps), with the *same* underlying parameters applied at each step." — §index, the structural definition

> "While the inputs and targets for many fundamental tasks in machine learning cannot easily be represented as fixed-length vectors, they can often nevertheless be represented as varying-length sequences of fixed-length vectors." — §index, the key insight that motivated sequence modeling

> "From the relationship between hidden layer outputs $\mathbf{H}_t$ and $\mathbf{H}_{t-1}$ of adjacent time steps, we know that these variables captured and retained the sequence's historical information up to their current time step, just like the state or memory of the neural network's current time step. Therefore, such a hidden layer output is called a *hidden state*." — §rnn, the memory metaphor

> "Perplexity can be best understood as the reciprocal of the geometric mean of the number of real choices that we have when deciding which token to pick next." — §perplexity, the interpretation

> "To be clear, it is a hack. Gradient clipping means that we are not always following the true gradient and it is hard to reason analytically about the possible side effects. However, it is a very useful hack, and is widely adopted in RNN implementations in most deep learning frameworks." — §rnn-scratch on gradient clipping, the honest assessment

> "High powers of matrices can lead to divergent or vanishing eigenvalues. This manifests itself in the form of exploding or vanishing gradients." — §bptt summary, the underlying cause
