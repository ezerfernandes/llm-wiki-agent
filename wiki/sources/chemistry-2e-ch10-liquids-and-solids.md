## Summary
Chapter 10 of OpenStax *Chemistry 2e* (officially "Intermolecular Forces and States of Matter") explains how the balance between intermolecular forces (IMFs) and kinetic energy determines whether matter is solid, liquid, or gas, and how IMFs control physical properties. It develops the three van der Waals forces (London dispersion, dipole-dipole, hydrogen bonding), the bulk properties of liquids (viscosity, surface tension, capillary action), the energetics and equilibria of phase transitions (including vapor pressure, the Clausius-Clapeyron equation, and heating curves), phase diagrams and critical/supercritical behavior, and the structure of solids (crystalline vs amorphous, the four crystalline-solid types, cubic unit cells, ionic lattices, and X-ray diffraction via Bragg's law).

## Key Claims
- The phase of a substance depends on the relative magnitudes of its intermolecular forces and the kinetic energy of its molecules; IMFs are much weaker than the intramolecular covalent bonds (e.g., ~17 kJ/mol to overcome IMFs in liquid HCl vs ~430 kJ/mol to break the H–Cl bond).
- The three van der Waals forces, weakest to strongest, are London dispersion forces (present in all substances, scale with size/polarizability), dipole-dipole attractions (polar molecules only), and hydrogen bonding (requires F–H, O–H, or N–H; ~5–10% the strength of a covalent bond but stronger than other IMFs).
- Viscosity (resistance to flow) rises with IMF strength and molecular complexity and falls with temperature; surface tension (energy to increase surface area) arises from cohesive forces; capillary rise h = 2T cos θ / (rρg) is driven by adhesion vs cohesion.
- At dynamic equilibrium vaporization and condensation occur at equal rates, giving the temperature-dependent vapor pressure; boiling occurs when vapor pressure equals ambient pressure (normal boiling point at 1 atm); the Clausius-Clapeyron equation relates vapor pressure, temperature, and ΔH_vap.
- Phase-change enthalpies obey ΔH_fus < ΔH_vap and ΔH_sub = ΔH_fus + ΔH_vap; heating curves combine q = mcΔT (within a phase) and q = nΔH (at constant-temperature transitions).
- A phase diagram maps phases in P-T space with a triple point (three phases coexist; water at 0.01 °C, 0.6 kPa) and a critical point (above which no liquid exists; water at 374 °C, ~218 atm); water's solid-liquid boundary has an anomalous negative slope, while CO₂'s triple point lies above 1 atm so solid CO₂ sublimes.
- Crystalline solids fall into four types (ionic, metallic, covalent network, molecular) with characteristic bonding and properties; amorphous solids lack long-range order and soften over a range.
- The three cubic unit cells (simple cubic: 1 atom, CN 6, ~52%; BCC: 2 atoms, CN 8, ~68%; FCC/CCP: 4 atoms, CN 12, 74%) and ionic structures (CsCl, NaCl, zinc blende, fluorite) follow from packing efficiency and cation/anion radius ratios filling tetrahedral, octahedral, or cubic holes.
- X-ray diffraction determines interatomic distances via the Bragg equation nλ = 2d sin θ.

## Key Quotes
> "The phase in which a substance exists depends on the relative extents of its intermolecular forces (IMFs) and the kinetic energies (KE) of its molecules." — chapter framing (10.1)
> "Despite use of the word 'bond,' keep in mind that hydrogen bonds are intermolecular attractive forces, not intramolecular attractive forces (covalent bonds)." — 10.1
> "Hydrogen bonds are much weaker than covalent bonds, only about 5 to 10% as strong, but are generally much stronger than other dipole-dipole attractions and dispersion forces." — 10.1
> "Surface tension is defined as the energy required to increase the surface area of a liquid, or the force required to increase the length of a liquid surface by a given amount." — 10.2
> "The overall attractive interactions between particles are maximized, and the total intermolecular energy is minimized, when the particles pack in the most efficient manner." — 10.6
