# Machine Learning Systems (mlsysbook Vol 1) — Ch 3: ML Workflow

## Summary

Chapter 3 of [[VijayJanapaReddi|Vijay Janapa Reddi]]'s open-access *Machine Learning Systems* ([[mlsysbook-ch01-introduction|Vol 1, "Foundations"]], mlsysbook.ai, 2026) is the **orchestration** chapter: having named the components ([[DAMTaxonomy|Data·Algorithm·Machine]]) and the operating environments ([[DeploymentSpectrum|Cloud/Edge/Mobile/TinyML]]), it asks *how* they connect into a functioning system. Its central distinction is between the **[[MLSystemLifecycle|ML lifecycle]]** (*what gets traversed* — six stages: Problem Definition → Data Collection & Preparation → Model Development & Training → Evaluation & Validation → Deployment & Integration → Monitoring & Maintenance) and the **[[MLWorkflow|ML Workflow]]** (*how the traversal is managed* — the engineering discipline that makes constraints explicit at each stage and traces how they propagate). The chapter opens with a cautionary timeline — a diagnostic model hits 96% accuracy on day 120, then is discarded on day 153 when someone checks that the 4 GB model can't fit the 512 MB target tablet — to argue that **the deployment constraint should have shaped every decision from day one**. ML systems differ from traditional software because *data is the source code*, behavior is probabilistic not deterministic, models degrade when the *world* changes (not just when code changes), and later stages constantly feed back into earlier ones.

Three quantitative tools structure the argument. The **[[ConstraintPropagationPrinciple|constraint propagation principle]]** ($\sim 2^{N_{\text{stage}}-1}$): a constraint discovered at stage $N$ costs roughly $2^{N-1}\times$ more to fix than at stage 1 — a deployment-stage (5) discovery costs 16×, a monitoring-stage (6) discovery 32×. The **[[IterationTax|iteration tax]]**: a lightweight model that iterates hourly (starting at 90% accuracy) beats a large model that iterates weekly (starting at 95%) within a six-month window, because iteration *velocity* compounds — "iteration velocity is a feature." And the **iron law of workflow**, which maps each lifecycle stage onto a term of Ch 1's [[IronLawOfMLSystems|iron law]] ($T = D_{\text{vol}}/\text{BW} + O/(R_{\text{peak}}\cdot\eta_{hw}) + L_{\text{lat}}$): Problem Definition sets target constraints, Data determines $D$ and $D_{\text{vol}}$, Model Development defines $O$, Evaluation verifies $\eta_{hw}$, Deployment minimizes $L_{\text{lat}}$, and Monitoring feeds violations back. A **[[StageInterfaceSpecification|stage interface specification]]** treats each transition as an API contract (inputs, outputs, quality invariants), so violations are caught at the cheapest stage.

The chapter threads a single running case study — **[[DiabeticRetinopathyScreening|diabetic retinopathy (DR) screening]]** for rural clinics (Gulshan et al. 2016; Beede et al. 2020 in Thai clinics) — through all six stages, plus three [[LighthouseModel|Lighthouse-Model]] archetypes ([[ResNet50|ResNet-50]] compute-bound, [[DLRM]] memory-bound, keyword-spotting energy-bound). It closes with three **[[SystemsThinking|systems-thinking]]** patterns (constraint propagation, **[[MultiScaleFeedback|multi-scale feedback]]** spanning minutes→quarters, and **[[EmergentBehavior|emergent complexity]]** with resource trade-offs) and a Fallacies & Pitfalls section. The recurring empirical anchor: **data work consumes 60–80% of ML effort** (CrowdFlower 2016: 60% cleaning + 19% collection = 79%), which is why Part I of the book ends with [[DataEngineering|data engineering]].

## Key Claims

- **Lifecycle ≠ workflow.** The *lifecycle* is the six stages that get traversed; the *workflow* is the engineering discipline of orchestrating them. The lifecycle is *what*, [[MLOps|MLOps]] is *how* (automation/tooling/infrastructure that implements it at scale). The book formally defines the [[MachineLearningLifecycle|ML lifecycle]] as "the iterative engineering process of building, deploying, monitoring, and retraining ML systems, where each stage feeds information back to earlier stages because model performance degrades continuously after deployment."
- **Two parallel pipelines, one system.** A **data pipeline** (collection → ingestion → analysis/curation → labeling → validation → preparation) produces ML-ready datasets; a **model pipeline** (training → evaluation → validation → deployment) produces production systems. Feedback arrows — deployment → collection ("online performance"), plus "data fixes" and "data needs" loops — make it cyclical, not linear.
- **The day-1 deployment-constraint cautionary tale.** A team built a diagnostic model to 95% (day 90) then 96% (day 120) accuracy, handed it to deployment engineers (day 150), who found it needed 4 GB on tablets with 512 MB available (day 152), discarding five months of work (day 153). "The failure was a *workflow* failure." Lab-to-deployment gap is real: a deep-learning DR system with specialist-level accuracy had **21% of images rejected for quality in its first six months** in Thai clinics (Beede et al. 2020).
- **Data work dominates.** CrowdFlower's 2016 survey: 60% of data scientists cited cleaning/organizing data as their biggest time sink, 19% collecting datasets — **79% data-centered** vs. ~16% model-focused (9% mining + 3% training sets + 4% algorithm refinement). The chapter's headline number: **data collection, cleaning, labeling, validation, and preparation often consume 60–80% of ML engineering effort.** "In ML projects, the 'source code' is the data."
- **The constraint propagation principle is exponential.** A constraint discovered at stage $N$ costs $\sim 2^{N-1}\times$ more to fix (Boehm's cost-escalation curve, simplified). Stage 5 (Deployment) = $2^4$ = **16×**; stage 6 (Monitoring) = **32×**. Propagation is bidirectional: a 100 ms latency SLO discovered at deployment constrains model size $O$ (stage 3) → dataset size $D$ (stage 2) → what accuracy is even achievable (stage 1).
- **The iteration tax: velocity compounds.** Over 26 weeks, a large model at 1 experiment/week (start 95%, +0.15 pp/iter) reaches ~98.9%; a small model iterating hourly (start 90%, +0.1 pp/iter, capped at 100 effective experiments) reaches the 99% ceiling. "A system that allows ten experiments/day will almost always eventually outperform a system that allows one experiment/week, even if the latter starts with a better model." This explains why fast-iterating startups out-execute slower large teams.
- **ML breaks OS determinism assumptions.** 50 years of OS kernels optimized for **[[LocalityOfReference|locality of reference]]**; randomly shuffling a multi-terabyte dataset every training epoch is the worst case for VM prefetchers and FS buffers. Cost of violating locality: L1 hit ~1 ns vs. DRAM 50–100 ns (50–100× penalty) vs. NVMe 10–100 µs (10,000–100,000× penalty) — why ML data loaders implement their own prefetching rather than trusting OS page cache.
- **Stage interfaces are API contracts.** Each of the six stages has an Input Contract, Output Contract, and Quality Invariant (e.g., Problem Definition must output "target deployment paradigm is explicit"; Deployment must satisfy "latency and throughput meet paradigm requirements"). Validating outputs at each transition (*auditing stage transitions*) catches violations when correction is cheapest. The worked audit example: a team skips deployment-paradigm selection ("figure it out later") — blocked, because by the propagation principle resolving it later at stage 5 costs $2^4 = 16\times$, ~2–4 iteration cycles (≈8–16 weeks) of rework.
- **Problem Definition is multi-constraint optimization with no software analogue.** The DR system balances five competing constraints: diagnostic accuracy (>90% sensitivity, >80% specificity), computational efficiency (rural-clinic hardware), workflow integration, regulatory compliance (FDA), cost-effectiveness. Constraints stack in **layers**: statistical (what accuracy, which subgroups) on physical (what hardware, latency/memory budgets) on operational (regulatory/organizational/workflow). "A constraint that exists but remains unspecified does not disappear — it surfaces later at exponentially higher cost."
- **Problem definitions evolve.** Unlike stabilized software specs, ML problem definitions are living documents: scaling DR from a handful of clinics to hundreds forced stratified per-group accuracy targets, heterogeneous-hardware tolerance, and fairness reporting. **Demographic error-rate disparities can exceed 40×.**
- **War story — "when the label was the bias."** Amazon's ~12-person Edinburgh recruiting-ML team (2014) trained on a decade of male-dominated hiring data; the model penalized resumes containing "women's" and downgraded all-women's-college graduates. It learned to predict the *historical labeling decision*, not job ability. Disbanded by early 2017; a four-year effort produced no deployable system. Lesson: **problem definition must specify fairness, auditability, and rejection criteria before data collection.**
- **Bandwidth makes infrastructure a data-collection decision.** A rural clinic at 150 patients/day × 10 photos × 5 MB = ~7,500 MB/day; over a 2 Mbps uplink that's ~8+ hours — saturating ~100% of an 8-hour clinic day. Uploading only ~10 KB detection *summaries* per patient cuts bandwidth ~3,000×. Conclusion: **edge deployment (e.g., NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano, 7–15 W) is mandatory, not optional.** Tiered storage (hot NVMe ~$0.10/GB-mo at 500K+ IOPS vs. cold S3 ~$0.023/GB-mo at 100–200 ms) — choosing the wrong tier turns a compute-bound pipeline I/O-bound.
- **Transfer learning collapses data requirements.** Pretraining on [[ImageNet|ImageNet]]'s ~14M images then fine-tuning on ~128,000 retinal images (rather than training from scratch) reaches AUC 0.99, sensitivity 97.5%, specificity 93.4% (Gulshan et al. 2016) — comparable to ophthalmologists. Without it, the DR project would need 10–100× more labeled images. Became widespread ~2013–2014 (Yosinski et al. 2014).
- **Ensembles win competitions but lose deployment.** Bagging/boosting/stacking; competition winners ensemble 10–50 models. The Netflix Prize (2006–2009): BellKor's ensemble cut RMSE 10.06%, won $1M — but **Netflix never deployed it** because serving 800+ models exceeded the business value. "Simpler models plus better data infrastructure delivered more production value."
- **The reproducible system artifact is the real deliverable** — not weights alone, but {model weights, inference code incl. preprocessing, environment specification (Docker/`requirements.txt`/CUDA), configuration}. A 99%-accurate model relying on a library version absent in production "is a broken system." Reproducibility is harder than for code because the "source code" includes data versions, random seeds, hardware, and library versions; nondeterministic floating-point means 97% on one GPU can be 95% on another. Tools: [[MLflow]], [[WeightsAndBiases|Weights & Biases]] for artifact lineage.
- **The MLOps leverage (flywheel) vs. coordination tax.** Manual workflows scale linearly then saturate (every new engineer must sync with every other — combinatorial overhead). A shared platform (feature store, versioned experiment tracker, pipeline scheduler) makes velocity scale *super-linearly* — each engineer inherits the platform rather than negotiating with colleagues. A naive grid search over 5 hyperparameters × 4 values = $4^5$ = **1,024 training runs**; at $100s–$1,000s each, six-figure cycles.
- **Evaluation ≠ validation.** *Evaluation* measures accuracy on held-out i.i.d. test data; *validation* is a multi-dimensional **gate** testing the full deployment constraint surface (latency SLOs, fairness thresholds, cost, robustness under shift). A model at 95% test accuracy can still violate a 100 ms p99 SLO or a fairness constraint. DR AUC 0.99 in the lab dropped to **78% sensitivity** on a five-year-old camera in Chiang Mai. Stratified evaluation, [[ModelCalibration|calibration]] (80% confidence → 80% correctness; Platt/temperature scaling), and the PPV/prevalence trap (95% lab accuracy → 50% PPV in low-prevalence settings) all matter.
- **Validation moves progressively toward production.** *Offline* (held-out, catches algorithmic issues) → *[[ShadowDeployment|shadow mode]]* (predicts but doesn't serve; catches integration issues) → *[[CanaryDeployment|canary]]* (1–5% traffic; catches scaling issues) → *[[ABTesting|A/B test]]* (statistical; catches user-facing issues). Progressive deployment catches **70–80% of production issues before full rollout** — critical because ML failures are statistical (degraded accuracy) not functional (crashes), so they're invisible to traditional health checks.
- **Regulatory validation is a first-class engineering requirement.** FDA regulates AI/ML medical devices under the **Software as a Medical Device (SaMD)** framework (1,000+ authorized, mostly radiology/cardiology); its 2021 AI/ML Action Plan introduced **predetermined change control plans (PCCPs)** for bounded prespecified model modifications. This forces versioned artifacts, reproducible pipelines, and audit trails at every stage. Systems that *fail safely* (refer uncertain cases) earn more clinical trust than those that *fail silently*. Over 80% of healthcare AI projects with strong lab accuracy fail to reach clinical deployment.
- **Edge vs. cloud deployment economics.** For ~500 clinics, cloud inference (per-image GPU + API + network for 5 MB uploads) accrues large annual OpEx with connectivity-outage risk; edge (NVIDIA Jetson) front-loads CapEx but pays back in a few years with electricity-only inference, sub-50 ms latency, and offline operation. "The deployment paradigm selected during Problem Definition determines whether the edge option is even viable."
- **Monitoring: ML degrades silently.** Six months post-launch, a clinic upgrades to sharper cameras (a clinical *improvement*) — yet sensitivity drops 8% because pixel distributions drifted. No code changed. Monitoring tracks a **hierarchy by timescale**: operational metrics (seconds: latency P95 <50 ms, throughput, error rate), proxy metrics (hours, no ground truth needed: confidence distribution, referral rate, image-quality rejection rate), model-performance metrics (weeks, needs ground truth: sensitivity, specificity, subgroup gaps), and drift detection ([[PopulationStabilityIndex|PSI]] >0.2 = significant; [[KolmogorovSmirnovTest|KS test]] $p<0.01$).
- **ML rollback is a mitigation, not a restore.** A rollback restores a model artifact but cannot restore the past data environment — a temporal state mismatch, since the stale model's performance on current live data isn't guaranteed.
- **Three systems-thinking patterns.** (1) **Constraint propagation** (bidirectional cascades; [[MLTechnicalDebt|ML technical debt]] compounds via entanglement, hidden feedback loops, undeclared consumers — ML code is <5% of a production system); a data-quality-specific propagation is a **[[DataCascade|data cascade]]**. (2) **[[MultiScaleFeedback|Multi-scale feedback]]** across ~five orders of magnitude (minute loops catch a misconfigured camera; quarterly loops re-evaluate architecture). (3) **[[EmergentBehavior|Emergent complexity]]**: individual clinics look stable while system-wide analysis detects demographic-subgroup degradation — ML fails through *probabilistic* degradation, not *deterministic* cascades.

## Key Quotes

> "The failure was a *workflow* failure. A deployment constraint that should have shaped every decision from day one was discovered only after the work was done." — the day-153 cautionary timeline; the chapter's organizing motivation

> "In ML projects, the 'source code' is the data, and preparing that source code is a primary engineering activity." — on why data work dominates (60–80% of effort)

> "Iteration velocity is a feature. A system that allows ten experiments/day will almost always eventually outperform a system that allows one experiment/week, even if the latter starts with a better model." — the [[IterationTax|iteration tax]]

> "A constraint that exists but remains unspecified does not disappear — it simply surfaces later at exponentially higher cost." — the [[ConstraintPropagationPrinciple|constraint propagation principle]]

> "The training labels were the bias. ... The model had learned to predict the historical labeling decision, not the underlying ability to do the job." — the Amazon recruiting war story

> "Traditional software maintains static behavior until explicitly updated; ML systems degrade through data drift even when untouched." — the structural difference that makes deployment the *beginning* of the lifecycle

> "We now have the blueprint for the engine, yet an engine without fuel is just a heavy block of metal. The answer lies in data." — closing transition into Ch 4 ([[DataEngineering|Data Engineering]])

## Contradictions

- **No direct factual contradictions** with existing wiki pages. This chapter *operationalizes* the [[MLSystemLifecycle|lifecycle sketch]] from [[mlsysbook-ch01-introduction|Ch 1]] (adding the lifecycle-vs-workflow distinction, stage contracts, the constraint-propagation and iteration-tax math), and uses the [[DeploymentSpectrum|deployment paradigms]] and [[IronLawOfMLSystems|iron law]] from [[mlsysbook-ch02-ml-systems|Ch 2]] as the constraint vocabulary.
- **Consistent with the "more data" caveat across sources.** Like [[mlsysbook-ch02-ml-systems|Ch 2]]'s KWS example (95%→96.5% for 20× data) and [[dmls-ch07-model-deployment|DMLS Ch 7]], this chapter argues *more data is not monotonically beneficial* (500K→1M examples → <1 pp gain at 2× labeling cost); data *quality* beats volume. No conflict — a shared thesis.
- **"Iteration velocity is a feature" complements, not contradicts, DMLS's update-cadence framing.** [[dmls-ch07-model-deployment|DMLS Ch 7]] asks "how often *can* I update?" (Weibo 10-min cycles, AWS every 11.7 s); this chapter generalizes the same insight into the iteration-tax model where cycle *time* dominates outcome regardless of starting accuracy.
- **"Latency" remains multi-scale.** This chapter's latency is end-to-end clinical-workflow request/response (tens of ms; P95 <50 ms), distinct from the foundation-model TTFT/TPOT vocabulary in *AI Engineering* Ch 9. The [[Latency]] page already flags this; no conflict.
