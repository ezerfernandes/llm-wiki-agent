## Summary
Chapter 11 of OpenStax *Chemistry 2e* explains how solutions form, what governs whether they form, and the properties that result. It develops the thermodynamics of the dissolution process (the interplay of solute-solute, solvent-solvent, and solute-solvent intermolecular forces, the stepwise enthalpy-of-solution model, ideal solutions, and entropy as a driving force), distinguishes electrolytes from nonelectrolytes (ionic dissociation vs covalent ionization; strong vs weak), quantifies solubility and its dependence on temperature and (for gases) pressure via Henry's law, derives the four colligative properties (Raoult's-law vapor-pressure lowering, boiling point elevation, freezing point depression, and osmotic pressure) with the van 't Hoff factor for electrolytes, and finally characterizes colloids (the Tyndall effect, dispersed phase/dispersion medium classification, emulsions, gels, and electrostatic stabilization).

## Key Claims
- A solution is a homogeneous mixture; the solvent is the majority component and solutes are dispersed on a molecular scale, each surrounded by solvent species, and do not settle out.
- Solution formation is spontaneous when favored by a decrease in internal energy (exothermic) and/or an increase in matter dispersal (entropy); matter dispersal always increases, so entropy can drive even endothermic dissolution (e.g., NH₄NO₃ cold packs).
- An ideal solution forms with no energy change when solute-solvent forces equal the forces in the separated components (e.g., He+Ar gases; methanol+ethanol); the overall enthalpy of solution is the net of three steps — separating solute (endo), expanding solvent (endo), and solvation (exo).
- Whether an ionic compound dissolves depends on the balance of interionic electrostatic forces (solute-solute) against ion-water solvation forces: solvation ≫ electrostatic → exothermic, highly soluble (NaOH); electrostatic ≫ solvation → endothermic, poorly soluble (CaCO₃).
- Electrolytes yield mobile ions and conduct electricity; ionic compounds dissolve by physical **dissociation** and are strong electrolytes (even sparingly soluble ones), while covalent electrolytes like HCl produce ions by chemical **ionization** with water (H₂O + HCl → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻). Strong = ~100% ionized; weak = partial.
- Solubility is the equilibrium maximum concentration; solutions are unsaturated, saturated, or (metastably) supersaturated. Gas solubility falls with rising temperature and rises with partial pressure per Henry's law, Cg = k·Pg.
- "Like dissolves like": miscibility of liquids and the dissolving of solids follow from matching polarities and the relative strengths of solute-solute, solvent-solvent, and solute-solvent attractions; most solid solubilities rise with temperature (with exceptions like Ce₂(SO₄)₃).
- Colligative properties depend only on the number of dissolved particles (not their identity): Raoult's law (P_A = X_A·P°_A) gives vapor-pressure lowering; ΔT_b = K_b·m; ΔT_f = K_f·m; osmotic pressure Π = M·R·T. For electrolytes each is multiplied by the van 't Hoff factor i.
- The van 't Hoff factor i = (mol particles)/(mol formula units) is ideally the number of ions per formula unit, but measured values are lower (NaCl i ≈ 1.9 vs 2) because of ion pairing / residual interionic attraction (Debye-Hückel); i approaches the ideal value in dilute solutions.
- Colloids are intermediate between solutions and suspensions: particles too large to be molecular but too small to settle; they scatter light (Tyndall effect) and are classified by dispersed phase + dispersion medium (sol, aerosol, emulsion, gel, foam). Emulsions need an emulsifying agent; colloidal particles are often like-charged, which keeps them dispersed and allows removal by electrostatic precipitation.

## Key Quotes
> "Solutions are crucial to the processes that sustain life and to many other processes involving chemical reactions." — chapter introduction
> "When the strengths of the intermolecular forces of attraction between solute and solvent species in a solution are no different than those present in the separated components, the solution is formed with no accompanying energy change." — 11.1 (ideal solutions)
> "The electrostatic attraction between an ion and a molecule with a dipole is called an ion-dipole attraction." — 11.2
> "The quantity of an ideal gas that dissolves in a definite volume of liquid is directly proportional to the pressure of the gas." — 11.3 (Henry's law)
> "Colligative properties include vapor pressure lowering, boiling point elevation, freezing point depression, and osmotic pressure." — 11.4
> "A class of mixtures called colloids exhibit properties intermediate between those of suspensions and solutions." — 11.5
