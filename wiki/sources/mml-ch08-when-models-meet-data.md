## Summary

Chapter 8 (book pp. 251–288) of [[mml-book|Mathematics for Machine Learning]] **opens Part II — Central Machine Learning Problems** and is the conceptual bridge from the six mathematical foundations of Part I to the four ML pillars ([[LinearRegression|regression]] Ch 9, [[PrincipalComponentAnalysis|dimensionality reduction]] Ch 10, [[GaussianMixtureModel|density estimation]] Ch 11, [[SupportVectorMachine|classification]] Ch 12). It expands the three components of any ML system introduced in [[mml-ch01-introduction-and-motivation|Ch 1]] — **data, models, learning** — and answers the question "what does it mean to *learn*?" three different ways. §8.1 fixes the vocabulary: **data are vectors** $\mathbf{x}_n\in\mathbb{R}^D$ (the [[DesignMatrix|example matrix]] $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$), a **model** is either a **[[Predictor|predictor]] function** $f:\mathbb{R}^D\to\mathbb{R}$ (§8.1.2) or a **[[ProbabilisticModel|probability distribution]]** (§8.1.3), and **learning is finding parameters** in three distinct algorithmic phases — *prediction/inference*, *training/parameter-estimation*, and *hyperparameter-tuning/model-selection* (§8.1.4). The body then presents three "flavors" of learning. §8.2 **[[EmpiricalRiskMinimization|empirical risk minimization]]** (the non-probabilistic route, via four design choices: [[HypothesisClass|hypothesis class]], [[LossFunction|loss function]], [[Regularization|regularization]], search procedure) — defining [[EmpiricalRisk|empirical risk]] $\mathbf{R}_{\text{emp}}$ vs [[ExpectedRisk|expected/true risk]] $\mathbf{R}_{\text{true}}$, [[Overfitting|overfitting]], and [[CrossValidation|cross-validation]] as a generalization-error estimator. §8.3 **[[MaximumLikelihoodEstimation|parameter estimation]]** — [[MaximumLikelihoodEstimation|MLE]] (minimize the [[NegativeLogLikelihood|negative log-likelihood]]), [[MAPEstimation|MAP]] (add a log-prior, the probabilistic analog of regularization), and [[ModelFitting|model fitting]] (overfitting / underfitting / fitting-well). §8.4 **[[BayesianInference|probabilistic modeling and inference]]** — the joint $p(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\theta)$ as the central object, full Bayesian inference (integrate over $\boldsymbol\theta$ rather than picking a point estimate), the [[MarginalLikelihood|marginal likelihood/evidence]], and [[LatentVariable|latent-variable models]]. §8.5 **[[DirectedGraphicalModel|directed graphical models]]** (Bayesian networks) — graph↔factorization, [[PlateNotation|plate notation]], and [[DSeparation|d-separation]] for reading [[ConditionalIndependence|conditional independence]] off the graph. §8.6 **[[ModelSelection|model selection]]** — [[NestedCrossValidation|nested cross-validation]], [[BayesianModelSelection|Bayesian model selection]], the [[BayesFactor|Bayes factor]], [[OccamsRazor|Occam's razor]] (automatic in the marginal likelihood), and [[AkaikeInformationCriterion|AIC]]/[[BayesianInformationCriterion|BIC]]. Philosophically, the chapter frames the whole learning-and-model-selection procedure as **[[Abduction|abduction]]** — inference to the best explanation, neither pure induction nor deduction (§8.2 marginal, p. 258). A recurring structural claim ties the three flavors together: **the prior is to MAP/Bayes what regularization is to ERM, and the likelihood is to MLE what the loss function is to ERM** (§8.3 intro, p. 265).

## Key Claims

### 8.1 Data, Models, and Learning (pp. 251–258)

- **There are three major components of any ML system — data, models, and learning — and the guiding principle is that good models perform well on *unseen* data** (§8.1, p. 251). "The main question of machine learning is 'What do we mean by good models?'" The word *model* "has many subtleties" and is revisited repeatedly; "good" is defined operationally via a performance metric (accuracy, distance from ground truth) on data not yet seen.
- **Data are assumed tabular, tidy, and numerical — each example is a $D$-dimensional vector $\mathbf{x}_n$** (§8.1.1, pp. 252–253). Each row is an *instance / example / data point*, each column a *feature / attribute / covariate*. The book defers the hard problem of *feature engineering* (genomic sequences, text, webpage content, social graphs — "data science") to domain experts and assumes data already converted to a numerical representation. Even tabular data requires choices: a categorical *gender* column becomes $\{0,1\}$ or $\{-1,+1\}$; a London postcode is decoded to (latitude, longitude); columns should be **shifted and scaled to empirical mean 0 and empirical variance 1** absent other information (Tables 8.1 → 8.2 worked example). The **Name** column is dropped — uninformative for the task and useful to anonymize. Citations: tidy data (Wickham 2014; Codd 1990); data science (Stray 2016; Adhikari & DeNero 2018).
- **Notation: $N$ examples indexed $n=1,\dots,N$; $D$ features indexed $d=1,\dots,D$; supervised pairs $(\mathbf{x}_n,y_n)$ with [[Label|label]] $y_n$; the example matrix $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$** (§8.1.1, p. 253). The label $y_n$ has many names — *target, response variable, annotation*. The running example: predict annual salary ($y$) from age ($x$) — a supervised problem (Fig. 8.1, the $x=60$ prediction marked by a dashed red line not in the training data). The database row-orientation is convenient here, but Ch 10 (PCA) prefers examples as *column* vectors.
- **Representing data as vectors lets us reuse all of Part I** (§8.1.1, pp. 253–254). Linear algebra (Ch 2) manipulates the vectors; a geometry/inner product (Ch 3) lets us *compare* two examples ("examples with similar features should have similar labels" — Chs 9, 12); optimization (Ch 7) solves the resulting learning problem. Better representations are found two ways: **lower-dimensional approximations** (PCA, Ch 10, tied to eigen/SVD of Ch 4) or **nonlinear higher-dimensional combinations** via an explicit **[[FeatureMap|feature map]]** $\phi(\cdot)$ that becomes a **kernel** in §12.4. Deep learning (Goodfellow et al. 2016) learns features from the data itself; not covered here, but §5.6 backprop is the relevant math.
- **A model is one of two things: a predictor *function* or a *probabilistic model*** (§8.1.2–8.1.3, pp. 255–256). **(1) Predictor as a function** (§8.1.2): a [[Predictor|predictor]] maps an input example to an output; for a real-valued scalar output $f:\mathbb{R}^D\to\mathbb{R}$ (Eq. 8.1). The book restricts to **linear functions** $f(\mathbf{x})=\boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf{x}+\theta_0$ (Eq. 8.2) to avoid functional analysis — "linear functions strike a good balance between the generality of the problems that can be solved and the amount of background mathematics that is needed," and Chs 2–3 suffice to state them precisely. **(2) Predictor as a probabilistic model** (§8.1.3): data are *noisy observations of some true underlying effect*; we want predictors that express **uncertainty** (Fig. 8.3 draws a Gaussian predictive distribution at $x=60$). Restricting to **finite-dimensional-parameter** distributions (no stochastic processes / random measures) lets us treat probabilistic models as **multivariate probability distributions** — already a rich class.
- **Learning is finding parameters, in three conceptually distinct algorithmic phases** (§8.1.4, pp. 257–258): **(1) prediction or inference**, **(2) training or parameter estimation**, **(3) hyperparameter tuning or model selection**. *Prediction* applies a trained predictor with fixed parameters/model-choice to new data; when the model is probabilistic the prediction phase is called **inference**. *Training* adjusts the model on training data and has two strategies — finding the best predictor by a quality measure (a **point estimate**) or **[[BayesianInference|Bayesian inference]]**; point estimates work for both model types, Bayesian inference requires a probabilistic model. *Most training methods are hill-climbing on an objective* (e.g. maximizing a likelihood) using gradients (Ch 5) and numerical optimization (Ch 7).
- **The three meanings of "learning" map onto three principles — ERM (§8.2), maximum likelihood (§8.3), probabilistic modeling (§8.4)** (§8.1.4, p. 257). For the non-probabilistic model we follow **empirical risk minimization** (directly an optimization problem for parameters); for the statistical model we use **maximum likelihood**; we can additionally model parameter uncertainty with a full probabilistic model.
- **Naming is *not* agreed upon — "inference" is overloaded** (§8.1.4, Remark, p. 257): "the word 'inference' is sometimes also used to mean parameter estimation of a probabilistic model, and less often may be also used to mean prediction for non-probabilistic models." A genuine notational hazard flagged by the authors themselves.
- **Generalizing well requires balancing fit against simplicity — achieved by regularization (§8.2.3) or a prior (§8.3.2) — and the philosophy is [[Abduction|abduction]], not induction or deduction** (§8.1.4, p. 258, margin). "We will need to balance between fitting well on training data and finding 'simple' explanations of the phenomenon." Per the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (Douven 2017), **abduction = inference to the best explanation**. Margin gag: *"A good movie title is 'AI abduction.'"*
- **Hyperparameters are high-level structural choices (e.g., number of mixture components, distribution class); choosing them is [[ModelSelection|model selection]]** (§8.1.4, p. 258). For non-probabilistic models, model selection is often done by **[[NestedCrossValidation|nested cross-validation]]** (§8.6.1). **Remark (parameters vs hyperparameters, p. 258):** "The distinction between parameters and hyperparameters is somewhat arbitrary, and is mostly driven by the distinction between what can be numerically optimized versus what needs to use search techniques. Another way to consider the distinction is to consider parameters as the explicit parameters of a probabilistic model, and to consider hyperparameters (higher-level parameters) as parameters that control the distribution of these explicit parameters." See [[Hyperparameter]].

### 8.2 Empirical Risk Minimization (pp. 258–265)

- **ERM asks "what is learning?" without building a probabilistic model, via four design choices** (§8.2, p. 258). Originally popularized by the [[SupportVectorMachine|support vector machine]] proposal (Ch 12), but its principles are general. The four subsections answer four questions: **§8.2.1** what is the set of functions we allow the predictor to take? (**[[HypothesisClass|hypothesis class]]**); **§8.2.2** how do we measure how well the predictor performs on the training data? (**[[LossFunction|loss function]]**); **§8.2.3** how do we construct predictors from only training data that perform well on unseen test data? (**[[Regularization|regularization]]**); **§8.2.4** what is the procedure for searching over the space of models? (**[[CrossValidation|cross-validation]]**).
- **8.2.1 Hypothesis class:** given $N$ examples $\mathbf{x}_n\in\mathbb{R}^D$ and scalar labels $y_n\in\mathbb{R}$, estimate a parametrized predictor $f(\cdot,\boldsymbol\theta):\mathbb{R}^D\to\mathbb{R}$ and find $\boldsymbol\theta^*$ s.t. $f(\mathbf{x}_n,\boldsymbol\theta^*)\approx y_n$ for all $n$ (Eq. 8.3); notation $\hat{y}_n=f(\mathbf{x}_n,\boldsymbol\theta^*)$ (§8.2.1, p. 259). A *parametrized class of functions* (e.g. **affine functions**) is the **hypothesis class**. **Example 8.1 (OLS regression):** with the **bias-augmentation trick** — prepend a unit feature $x^{(0)}=1$ so $\mathbf{x}_n=[1,x_n^{(1)},\dots,x_n^{(D)}]^\top$ and $\boldsymbol\theta=[\theta_0,\dots,\theta_D]^\top$ — the affine model $f(\mathbf{x}_n,\boldsymbol\theta)=\theta_0+\sum_{d=1}^D\theta_d x_n^{(d)}$ (Eq. 8.5) becomes the **linear** predictor $f(\mathbf{x}_n,\boldsymbol\theta)=\boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf{x}_n$ (Eq. 8.4), $f:\mathbb{R}^{D+1}\to\mathbb{R}$. Neural networks (Goodfellow et al. 2016) supply richer nonlinear classes. **Remark:** ERM is described in the supervised (labeled) setting for ease; it is common to also choose a parametrized class such as affine functions.
- **8.2.2 Loss function and empirical risk:** a [[LossFunction|loss function]] $\ell(y_n,\hat{y}_n)\ge 0$ takes the ground-truth label and the prediction and returns a non-negative "error/loss"; the goal is to minimize the **average** loss over the $N$ training examples (§8.2.2, pp. 259–260). The set of examples is assumed **[[IID|independent and identically distributed]]** (i.i.d., §6.4.5) — *independent* ⇒ the empirical mean is a good estimate of the population mean (§6.4.1). With the example matrix $\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_N]^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$ and label vector $\mathbf{y}=[y_1,\dots,y_N]^\top\in\mathbb{R}^N$, the **[[EmpiricalRisk|empirical risk]]** is $\mathbf{R}_{\text{emp}}(f,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N\ell(y_n,\hat{y}_n)$ (Eq. 8.6), with $\hat{y}_n=f(\mathbf{x}_n,\boldsymbol\theta)$. Minimizing it is **empirical risk minimization**. **Example 8.2 (least-squares loss):** squared loss $\ell(y_n,\hat{y}_n)=(y_n-\hat{y}_n)^2$ gives $\min_{\boldsymbol\theta}\frac{1}{N}\sum_n(y_n-f(\mathbf{x}_n,\boldsymbol\theta))^2$ (Eq. 8.7); substituting the linear predictor (Eq. 8.8) and going to matrix form yields $\min_{\boldsymbol\theta}\frac{1}{N}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta\|^2$ (Eq. 8.9) — the **least-squares problem**, with a closed-form solution via the normal equations (§9.2).
- **The real target is the [[ExpectedRisk|expected (true) risk]] — the loss over the infinite population, not the finite sample** (§8.2.2, pp. 260–261). For a predictor $f$ with parameters fixed, the expected risk is $\mathbf{R}_{\text{true}}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},y}[\ell(y,f(\mathbf{x}))]$ (Eq. 8.10) — "the true risk if we had access to an infinite amount of data," the expectation over the (infinite) set of all possible data and labels. Margin: also called the **population risk**. Two practical questions follow: how to change training to **generalize** well, and how to **estimate** expected risk from finite data. **Remark:** in principle the loss should match the task's performance measure (accuracy, RMSE, cost-sensitive); in practice there is often a mismatch for ease of implementation / optimization efficiency.
- **8.2.3 Regularization reduces overfitting by penalizing complexity** (§8.2.3, pp. 262–263). The held-out **test set** simulates unseen data; we split into a training set ($_{\text{train}}$) used to fit and a test set ($_{\text{test}}$) used to evaluate generalization — and must **not** cycle back to training after observing the test set ("Even knowing only the performance of the predictor on the test set leaks information" — Blum & Hardt 2015). A sufficiently rich class can **memorize** the training data to zero empirical risk yet fail on new data — **[[Overfitting|overfitting]]** (Mitchell 1997): "the predictor fits too closely to the training data and does not generalize well." Diagnostic: overfitting occurs when the train-risk $\mathbf{R}_{\text{emp}}(f,\mathbf{X}_{\text{train}},\mathbf{y}_{\text{train}})$ **underestimates** the expected risk $\mathbf{R}_{\text{true}}(f)$; since we estimate $\mathbf{R}_{\text{true}}$ by the test risk $\mathbf{R}_{\text{emp}}(f,\mathbf{X}_{\text{test}},\mathbf{y}_{\text{test}})$, a test risk *much larger* than the train risk indicates overfitting. The fix: **[[Regularization|regularization]]** — a **penalty term** that biases the search away from overly flexible predictors, "a way to compromise between accurate solution of empirical risk minimization and the size or complexity of the solution." **Example 8.3 (regularized least squares):** add $+\lambda\|\boldsymbol\theta\|^2$ to the least-squares objective: $\min_{\boldsymbol\theta}\frac{1}{N}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta\|^2+\lambda\|\boldsymbol\theta\|^2$ (Eq. 8.12). The term $\|\boldsymbol\theta\|^2$ is the **regularizer**, $\lambda$ the **regularization parameter** (trades off training loss vs parameter magnitude — magnitudes get large under overfitting, Bishop 2006). **The regularizer biases $\boldsymbol\theta$ toward the origin and reappears in probabilistic models as the prior probability of the parameters (§8.3.2); for the posterior to share the prior's form, prior and likelihood must be conjugate (§6.6). In Ch 12 the regularizer is equivalent to a large margin.**
- **8.2.4 Cross-validation estimates generalization without wasting data** (§8.2.4, pp. 263–264). The held-out set is also called the **validation set**; small $\mathcal{V}$ ⇒ noisy (high-variance) performance estimate, but we want as much data as possible for training — a contradiction resolved by **[[CrossValidation|cross-validation]]**. **$K$-fold CV** partitions the data $\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{V}$ ($\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{V}=\emptyset$) into $K$ chunks, $K-1$ forming the training set $\mathcal{R}$ and one the validation set $\mathcal{V}$, iterating over all $K$ assignments (Fig. 8.4, $K=5$) and averaging the $K$ runs. CV approximates the expected generalization error $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{V}}[R(f,\mathcal{V})]\approx\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K R(f^{(k)},\mathcal{V}^{(k)})$ (Eq. 8.13), where $f^{(k)}$ is the predictor trained on $\mathcal{R}^{(k)}$ and $R$ is e.g. RMSE on $\mathcal{V}^{(k)}$. Two approximation sources: the finite training set (suboptimal $f^{(k)}$) and the finite validation set (inaccurate risk estimate). Cost: training $K$ times can be expensive, especially when multiple complexity/regularization hyperparameters require an *exponential* number of runs (→ nested CV, §8.6.1). But CV is an **embarrassingly parallel** problem — with enough compute it costs no more than a single assessment.
- **8.2.5 Further reading:** ERM's original development was heavily theoretical (Vapnik 1998) → **statistical learning theory** (Vapnik 1999; Evgeniou et al. 2000; Hastie et al. 2001; von Luxburg & Schölkopf 2011; Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David 2014). Regularization has roots in ill-posed inverse problems (Neumaier 1998); the form shown is **Tikhonov regularization**, related to the constrained Ivanov regularization, the bias–variance trade-off, and feature selection (Bühlmann & Van De Geer 2011). CV alternatives: bootstrap, jackknife (Efron & Tibshirani 1993; Hall 1992). **Crucially: ERM is *not* "probability free."** "There is an underlying unknown probability distribution $p(\mathbf{x},y)$ that governs the data generation. However, the approach of empirical risk minimization is agnostic to that choice" — unlike standard statistics, which requires $p(\mathbf{x},y)$ explicitly; and since $p$ is a *joint* over $\mathbf{x}$ and $y$, labels can be non-deterministic, and we need not specify the noise distribution for $y$ (§8.2.5, p. 265).

### 8.3 Parameter Estimation (pp. 265–272)

- **The probabilistic route mirrors ERM: the likelihood is analogous to the loss (§8.2.2), the prior to the regularizer (§8.2.3)** (§8.3 intro, p. 265). This is the chapter's load-bearing analogy.
- **8.3.1 Maximum likelihood estimation = minimize the negative log-likelihood** (§8.3.1, pp. 265–267). MLE defines a function of the parameters that finds a well-fitting model; the estimation problem is focused on the likelihood, "more precisely its negative logarithm." For data $\mathbf{x}$ and a family of densities $p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta)$ parametrized by $\boldsymbol\theta$, the **[[NegativeLogLikelihood|negative log-likelihood]]** is $\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol\theta)=-\log p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta)$ (Eq. 8.14) — the subscript $\mathbf{x}$ emphasizes that **the data are fixed and $\boldsymbol\theta$ varies** (usually dropped, written $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol\theta)$). Two complementary readings: with $\boldsymbol\theta$ fixed, $p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta)$ models data uncertainty; with the data fixed (observed) and $\boldsymbol\theta$ varying, $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol\theta)$ tells us *"how likely a particular setting of $\boldsymbol\theta$ is for the observations."* For supervised learning we specify the **conditional** $p(y_n\,|\,\mathbf{x}_n,\boldsymbol\theta)$. **Example 8.4 (Gaussian likelihood):** assume i.i.d. Gaussian observation noise $\varepsilon_n\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$ with linear model $\mathbf{x}_n^\top\boldsymbol\theta$, so $p(y_n\,|\,\mathbf{x}_n,\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathcal{N}(y_n\,|\,\mathbf{x}_n^\top\boldsymbol\theta,\sigma^2)$ (Eq. 8.15).
- **i.i.d. ⇒ the data likelihood factorizes ⇒ the NLL is a sum** (§8.3.1, pp. 266–267). Independence ⇒ $p(\mathcal{Y}\,|\,\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol\theta)=\prod_{n=1}^N p(y_n\,|\,\mathbf{x}_n,\boldsymbol\theta)$ (Eq. 8.16); *identically distributed* ⇒ each factor is the same distribution with shared parameters. Taking $-\log$ (and $\log(ab)=\log a+\log b$) gives the NLL $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol\theta)=-\log p(\mathcal{Y}\,|\,\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol\theta)=-\sum_{n=1}^N\log p(y_n\,|\,\mathbf{x}_n,\boldsymbol\theta)$ (Eq. 8.17) — a sum of simpler functions, easier to optimize. **Caveat:** although $\boldsymbol\theta$ sits to the right of the conditioning bar, **it should not be interpreted as observed/fixed** — $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol\theta)$ is a function of $\boldsymbol\theta$, minimized w.r.t. $\boldsymbol\theta$. **Remark:** the negative sign is "a historical artifact" — we want to *maximize* likelihood but the optimization literature *minimizes*. **Example 8.5 (Gaussian NLL = least squares):** expanding the Gaussian (Eqs. 8.18a–d) gives $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol\theta)=\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_n(y_n-\mathbf{x}_n^\top\boldsymbol\theta)^2-\sum_n\log\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}$; with $\sigma$ given the second term is constant, so **minimizing $\mathcal{L}$ is exactly the least-squares problem** (compare Eq. 8.8). The closed-form ML solution (Ch 9); for non-Gaussian noise there may be no closed form → numerical optimization (Ch 7).
- **8.3.2 MAP estimation adds a prior — the probabilistic form of regularization** (§8.3.2, pp. 268–269). With prior knowledge about $\boldsymbol\theta$, multiply an extra **prior** $p(\boldsymbol\theta)$ onto the likelihood. By Bayes' theorem (§6.3), the **posterior** is $p(\boldsymbol\theta\,|\,\mathbf{x})=\frac{p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta)p(\boldsymbol\theta)}{p(\mathbf{x})}$ (Eq. 8.19), where $p(\boldsymbol\theta)$ is the **prior**, $p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta)$ the **likelihood**, $p(\boldsymbol\theta\,|\,\mathbf{x})$ the **posterior**. To maximize the posterior, the denominator $p(\mathbf{x})$ (independent of $\boldsymbol\theta$) is ignored: $p(\boldsymbol\theta\,|\,\mathbf{x})\propto p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta)p(\boldsymbol\theta)$ (Eq. 8.20). We then minimize the **negative log-posterior** → **[[MAPEstimation|maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation]]**. **Example 8.6:** a zero-mean Gaussian parameter prior $p(\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol\Sigma)$ is conjugate to the Gaussian likelihood (§6.6.1), so the posterior is again Gaussian (Fig. 8.6: the prior **biases the slope flatter and the intercept toward zero**). **MAP bridges the non-probabilistic and probabilistic worlds** — it explicitly acknowledges a prior, yet still produces only a *point estimate* of $\boldsymbol\theta$.
- **MLE properties and its small-data failure mode** (§8.3.2, Remark, pp. 269–270, citing Lehmann & Casella 1998; Efron & Hastie 2016): **asymptotic consistency** (MLE → true value as $N\to\infty$, plus an approximately normal error); the required sample sizes can be large; the **error variance decays as $1/N$**; and **in the "small" data regime, MLE can overfit**. The principle of MLE (and MAP) uses probabilistic modeling but **has not yet gone fully Bayesian** — training still returns a *single* parameter set (a point estimate). §8.4 takes the full step: treat parameters as random variables and use the *full* parameter distribution when predicting.
- **8.3.3 Model fitting = optimizing parameters to minimize a loss (e.g. NLL); three outcomes — overfitting, underfitting, fitting-well** (§8.3.3, pp. 270–272). A parametrization defines a **model class** $M_{\boldsymbol\theta}$; we optimize $\boldsymbol\theta$ so $M_{\boldsymbol\theta}$ is as close as possible to the (unknown) true data-generating model $M^*$ (Fig. 8.7; "closeness" = the chosen objective, e.g. squared loss). **Overfitting:** the model class is *too rich* (e.g. 7th-order polynomials for linear data) — overfit models "use all [their] modeling power to reduce the training error" and, on noisy data, "find some useful signal in the noise itself," causing enormous problems away from training data (Fig. 8.8a; overfit models typically have *many* parameters). **Underfitting:** the class is *not rich enough* (straight lines for sinusoidal data) — optimization can't get close to $M^*$ (Fig. 8.8b; underfit models typically have *few* parameters). **Fitting well:** the class is *about right* — good generalization (Fig. 8.8c). Practically we often use very rich classes (deep nets) and mitigate overfitting with **regularization (§8.2.3) or priors (§8.3.2)**; choosing the class itself is **model selection (§8.6)**. Margin: detect overfitting by *low training risk but high test risk in cross-validation* (§8.2.4).
- **8.3.4 Further reading:** for probabilistic models, MLE generalizes least-squares regression (Ch 9); restricting the predictor to $p(y_n\,|\,\mathbf{x}_n,\boldsymbol\theta)=\varphi(\boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf{x}_n)$ (Eq. 8.21) with a nonlinear **link function** $\varphi$ gives **generalized linear models** (Agresti 2002; McCullagh & Nelder 1989) — covering binary classification and count data; the exponential-family view is §6.6. **MLE is frequentist** (originally Fisher, 1930s); the Bayesian–frequentist debate reduces to the definition of probability (§6.1.1; Cheeseman 1985; Jaynes 2003; balanced view: Efron & Hastie 2016). For models where MLE is infeasible, see method-of-moments / $M$-estimation / estimating equations (Casella & Berger 2002).

### 8.4 Probabilistic Modeling and Inference (pp. 272–278)

- **A probabilistic model is fully specified by the joint distribution of all its random variables** (§8.4.1, p. 273). Probabilistic models represent uncertain aspects as probability distributions and offer "a unified and consistent set of tools from probability theory (Chapter 6) for modeling, inference, prediction, and model selection." The **joint** $p(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\theta)$ (observed variables $\mathbf{x}$, hidden parameters $\boldsymbol\theta$) is central — it encapsulates: the **prior and likelihood** (product rule, §6.3); the **marginal likelihood** $p(\mathbf{x})$ (sum rule — integrate out the parameters — key to model selection §8.6); the **posterior** (joint ÷ marginal likelihood). "Only the joint distribution has this property. Therefore, a probabilistic model is specified by the joint distribution of all its random variables." Worked motivation: a coin flip in two steps — a Bernoulli parameter $\mu$ then a sample $x\sim\text{Ber}(\mu)$; $\mu$ is unknown and unobservable, so we must *learn* it from outcomes.
- **8.4.2 Bayesian inference = compute the full posterior, then propagate parameter uncertainty into predictions** (§8.4.2, pp. 273–274). MLE/MAP yield a single best $\boldsymbol\theta^*$ (the key problem is **optimization**), and predictions use $p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta^*)$; but "focusing solely on some statistic of the posterior … leads to loss of information," which can be critical for decision-making systems whose objectives differ from the likelihood. **[[BayesianInference|Bayesian inference]]** (Gelman et al. 2004) instead finds the whole posterior $p(\boldsymbol\theta\,|\,\mathcal{X})=\frac{p(\mathcal{X}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta)p(\boldsymbol\theta)}{p(\mathcal{X})}$ with $p(\mathcal{X})=\int p(\mathcal{X}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta)p(\boldsymbol\theta)\,d\boldsymbol\theta$ (Eq. 8.22) — *"the key idea is to exploit Bayes' theorem to invert the relationship between the parameters $\boldsymbol\theta$ and the data."* Predictions then **average over all plausible parameters**: $p(\mathbf{x})=\int p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta)p(\boldsymbol\theta)\,d\boldsymbol\theta=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol\theta}[p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta)]$ (Eq. 8.23) — the prediction no longer depends on $\boldsymbol\theta$ (marginalized/integrated out), weighted by the posterior's plausibility.
- **Point estimation vs Bayesian inference: optimization vs integration** (§8.4.2, p. 274). Parameter estimation (MLE/MAP) → a *consistent point estimate*; the key computational problem is **optimization**; predictions are straightforward. Bayesian inference → a *posterior distribution*; the key computational problem is **integration**; predictions require *another* integration (Eq. 8.23). Bayesian inference is a principled way to incorporate prior knowledge, side information, and structural knowledge, and to propagate parameter uncertainty into predictions (valuable for risk assessment / exploration in data-efficient learning — Deisenroth et al. 2015; Kamthe & Deisenroth 2018). **Practical challenge:** without a **conjugate prior** (§6.6.1), the integrals in Eqs. 8.22–8.23 are analytically intractable; resort to **stochastic approximations (MCMC** — Gilks et al. 1996) or **deterministic approximations** — Laplace (Bishop 2006; MacKay 2003), **variational inference** (Jordan et al. 1999; Blei et al. 2017), **expectation propagation** (Minka 2001a). Successful applications: large-scale topic modeling, CTR prediction, data-efficient RL, online ranking, recommenders, Bayesian optimization. **Remark (p. 275):** the "variables" vs "parameters" separation is somewhat arbitrary — placing a prior on a parameter and integrating it out turns it into a random variable.
- **8.4.3 Latent-variable models add unobserved variables $\mathbf{z}$ distinct from parameters $\boldsymbol\theta$** (§8.4.3, pp. 275–277). **[[LatentVariable|Latent variables]]** $\mathbf{z}$ (besides $\boldsymbol\theta$) do *not* parametrize the model explicitly; they may describe the data-generating process (interpretability), and they "often simplify the structure of the model and allow us to define simpler and richer model structures" — usually with *fewer* parameters (Paquet 2008; Murphy 2012). Learning via MLE is principled via the **[[EMAlgorithm|expectation maximization (EM)]] algorithm** (Dempster et al. 1977; Bishop 2006). Examples: **PCA** (Ch 10), **Gaussian mixture models** (Ch 11), **hidden Markov models** / dynamical systems for time series, meta-learning. But learning in latent-variable models is *generally hard* (Ch 11). The generative process gives the conditional $p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathbf{z},\boldsymbol\theta)$ (Eq. 8.24) and a prior $p(\mathbf{z})$ on the latents. The **likelihood marginalizes out the latents**: $p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta)=\int p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathbf{z},\boldsymbol\theta)p(\mathbf{z})\,d\mathbf{z}$ (Eq. 8.25) — "the likelihood must not depend on the latent variables $\mathbf{z}$; it is only a function of the data $\mathbf{x}$ and the model parameters $\boldsymbol\theta$." This likelihood feeds MLE, MAP, or Bayesian inference exactly as before, yielding a parameter posterior $p(\boldsymbol\theta\,|\,\mathcal{X})=\frac{p(\mathcal{X}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta)p(\boldsymbol\theta)}{p(\mathcal{X})}$ (Eq. 8.26). One can also compute a **latent-variable posterior** $p(\mathbf{z}\,|\,\mathcal{X})=\frac{p(\mathcal{X}\,|\,\mathbf{z})p(\mathbf{z})}{p(\mathcal{X})}$ (Eq. 8.27) — though marginalizing out *both* $\mathbf{z}$ and $\boldsymbol\theta$ at once is generally impossible; easier is the latent posterior *conditioned* on parameters $p(\mathbf{z}\,|\,\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol\theta)=\frac{p(\mathcal{X}\,|\,\mathbf{z},\boldsymbol\theta)p(\mathbf{z})}{p(\mathcal{X}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta)}$ (Eq. 8.28), computed for PCA and GMMs in Chs 10–11. **Remark:** in later chapters the $\mathbf{z}$ vs $\boldsymbol\theta$ distinction blurs — uncertain parameters are also "latent/hidden" because unobserved.
- **8.4.4 Further reading:** probabilistic models capture uncertainty in a principled way (Bishop 2006; Barber 2012; Murphy 2012; Ghahramani 2015); analytic solutions are rare, so sampling (MCMC) and variational inference dominate. **Probabilistic programming** treats program variables as random variables and lets the compiler handle Bayesian inference under the hood (§8.4.4, p. 278) — a rapidly changing field.

### 8.5 Directed Graphical Models (pp. 278–283)

- **A [[DirectedGraphicalModel|directed graphical model]] (= [[BayesianNetwork|Bayesian network]]) visually captures how a joint distribution factorizes** (§8.5, p. 278). It is "a compact and succinct way to specify probabilistic models" letting the reader "visually parse dependencies between random variables." The joint by itself (e.g. $p(a,b,c)$) tells us nothing about independence relations; the graph does. **Properties:** simple visualization; can *design/motivate* new statistical models; inspection of the graph alone reveals properties like conditional independence; complex inference/learning computations become graphical manipulations. **Semantics:** **nodes are random variables; directed edges (arrows) indicate conditional probabilities** — an arrow $a\to b$ means $p(b\,|\,a)$ (§8.5.1, p. 279). Margin: with extra assumptions the arrows can be read as *causal* (Pearl 2009). **Remark:** not every distribution can be represented by a given graphical model (Bishop 2006).
- **Graph ↔ factorization is a two-way street** (§8.5.1, pp. 279–280). **Joint → graph** (Example 8.7): $p(a,b,c)=p(c\,|\,a,b)p(b\,|\,a)p(a)$ (Eq. 8.29) ⇒ "$c$ depends on $a,b$; $b$ depends on $a$; $a$ depends on neither" ⇒ the DAG of Fig. 8.9(a). General recipe: (1) create a node for every random variable; (2) for each conditional, add arrows from the conditioning nodes. **Graph → joint** (Example 8.8, Fig. 8.9b): the joint is the product of one conditional per node, each conditioned only on its **parents**: $p(x_1,\dots,x_5)=p(x_1)p(x_5)p(x_2\,|\,x_5)p(x_3\,|\,x_1,x_2)p(x_4\,|\,x_2)$ (Eq. 8.30). **In general $p(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{k=1}^K p(x_k\,|\,\mathrm{Pa}_k)$** (Eq. 8.31), where $\mathrm{Pa}_k$ = parents of $x_k$ (nodes with arrows pointing *into* $x_k$).
- **[[PlateNotation|Plate notation]] compactly represents repeated / i.i.d. structure; shading marks observed variables; hyperpriors add a layer** (§8.5.1, pp. 280–281). A repeated Bernoulli experiment $p(x_1,\dots,x_N\,|\,\mu)=\prod_{n=1}^N p(x_n\,|\,\mu)$ (Eqs. 8.32–8.33) factorizes because the trials are independent. Graphically: **observed variables are shaded nodes**; the single parameter $\mu$ is shared across all $x_n$. Fig. 8.10(a) draws $x_1,\dots,x_N$ explicitly; Fig. 8.10(b) wraps them in a **plate** (a box repeating its contents $N$ times) — equivalent but more compact. Fig. 8.10(c) places a **hyperprior** $\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)$ on $\mu$ — "a second layer of prior distributions on the parameters of the first layer of priors"; if $\alpha,\beta$ are deterministic (not random) their circle is omitted.
- **8.5.2 d-separation reads conditional independence directly off the graph** (§8.5.2, p. 281, "d-separation" Pearl 1988). For disjoint node-sets $\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}$, to test whether $\mathcal{A}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathcal{B}\,|\,\mathcal{C}$ (Eq. 8.34) is implied by the DAG, consider **all trails** (paths ignoring arrow direction) from any node in $\mathcal{A}$ to any node in $\mathcal{B}$. A trail is **blocked** if it contains any node such that **either**: (i) the arrows meet **head-to-tail or tail-to-tail** at the node *and* the node is in $\mathcal{C}$; **or** (ii) the arrows meet **head-to-head** at the node *and* **neither the node nor any of its descendants** is in $\mathcal{C}$. If *all* trails are blocked, $\mathcal{A}$ is **d-separated** from $\mathcal{B}$ by $\mathcal{C}$, and the joint satisfies $\mathcal{A}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathcal{B}\,|\,\mathcal{C}$. **Example 8.9** (Fig. 8.11, chain $a\to b\to c$ with both $\to d\to e$): $b\perp\!\!\!\perp d\,|\,a,c$ (8.35); $a\perp\!\!\!\perp c\,|\,b$ (8.36); but $b\not\perp\!\!\!\perp d\,|\,c$ (8.37) and $a\not\perp\!\!\!\perp c\,|\,b,e$ (8.38) — conditioning on the descendant $e$ of a head-to-head node *un*blocks a path ("explaining away").
- **8.5.3 Three graphical-model families** (§8.5.3, p. 283, Fig. 8.12): **directed graphical models / Bayesian networks** (this section); **undirected graphical models / Markov random fields**; **factor graphs**. Introductions: Bishop (2006, ch. 8), Koller & Friedman (2009). Graph algorithms enable inference/learning by **local message passing** (ranking, computer vision, coding theory, signal processing). A topic *not* covered: **structured prediction** (Bakir et al. 2007; Nowozin et al. 2014). Renewed interest in graphical models for **causal inference** (Pearl 2009; Imbens & Rubin 2015; Peters et al. 2017).

### 8.6 Model Selection (pp. 283–288)

- **[[ModelSelection|Model selection]] chooses high-level structural decisions that control model flexibility/expressivity** (§8.6, pp. 283–284). The choices (e.g. functional form of the likelihood) determine the number and type of free parameters and hence flexibility — a degree-1 polynomial $y=a_0+a_1x$ only models linear relations, degree-2 adds quadratics. **One might think flexible models are always preferable — but at training time we can only use the training set, whose performance is not what we care about; MLE can overfit on small data; ideally the model works on the (unavailable-at-train-time) test set.** Model selection is concerned with assessing how a model **generalizes**.
- **8.6.1 Nested cross-validation: two nested levels of $K$-fold CV** (§8.6.1, p. 284, Fig. 8.13). Apply CV one more time: for each outer split, run another round of CV. The **inner level** estimates the performance of a particular model/hyperparameter choice on an internal **validation set**; the **outer level** estimates generalization for the *best* choice selected by the inner loop, on the held-out **test set**. The inner loop estimates the expected validation error $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{V}}[\mathbf{R}(\mathcal{V}\,|\,M)]\approx\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K\mathbf{R}(\mathcal{V}^{(k)}\,|\,M)$ (Eq. 8.39); pick the best-performing $M$, then evaluate it on the test set. CV also yields higher-order statistics — e.g. the **standard error** $\sigma/\sqrt{K}$ ($K$ experiments, $\sigma$ = std-dev of per-experiment risk; margin), a measure of how uncertain the mean estimate is. See [[NestedCrossValidation]].
- **8.6.2 Bayesian model selection embodies Occam's razor *automatically*** (§8.6.2, pp. 285–287). Generally, model-selection methods trade off complexity and data fit; assuming simpler models are less prone to overfitting, "the objective of model selection is to find the simplest model that explains the data reasonably well" — **[[OccamsRazor|Occam's razor]]**. **Remark:** "If we treat model selection as a hypothesis testing problem, we are looking for the simplest hypothesis that is consistent with the data" (Murphy 2012). One *could* place a prior favoring simpler models, but it is unnecessary: **an "automatic Occam's razor" is quantitatively embodied in Bayesian probability** (Smith & Spiegelhalter 1980; Jefferys & Berger 1992; MacKay 1992). Intuition (Fig. 8.14, adapted from MacKay 2003): predictions $p(\mathcal{D}\,|\,M_i)$ are normalized over the dataset space $\mathcal{D}$, so they integrate to 1; a simple model $M_1$ predicts a *small* range of datasets sharply; a complex model $M_2$ spreads probability over *more* datasets and so assigns *less* to any one — if the observed data fall in the region $C$ both can explain, **the simpler model $M_1$ is the more probable** (under equal priors). **Bayesian model selection as hierarchical inference** (Fig. 8.15): for a finite model set $M=\{M_1,\dots,M_K\}$, place a prior $p(M)$ over models; the generative process is $M_k\sim p(M)$, $\boldsymbol\theta_k\sim p(\boldsymbol\theta\,|\,M_k)$, $\mathcal{D}\sim p(\mathcal{D}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta_k)$ (Eqs. 8.40–8.42). The **model posterior** $p(M_k\,|\,\mathcal{D})\propto p(M_k)p(\mathcal{D}\,|\,M_k)$ (Eq. 8.43) **no longer depends on the parameters** $\boldsymbol\theta_k$ because they have been integrated out: $p(\mathcal{D}\,|\,M_k)=\int p(\mathcal{D}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta_k)p(\boldsymbol\theta_k\,|\,M_k)\,d\boldsymbol\theta_k$ (Eq. 8.44) — the **model evidence** or **[[MarginalLikelihood|marginal likelihood]]**. The MAP model is $M^*=\arg\max_{M_k}p(M_k\,|\,\mathcal{D})$ (Eq. 8.45); under a uniform prior $p(M_k)=\frac{1}{K}$ this reduces to picking the model with the **maximum evidence**. **Remark (likelihood vs marginal likelihood):** the likelihood is prone to overfitting, but the marginal likelihood is *not* (the parameters are integrated out, so we no longer fit them), and it "automatically embodies a trade-off between model complexity and data fit (Occam's razor)."
- **8.6.3 Bayes factors compare two models via the ratio of marginal likelihoods** (§8.6.3, p. 287). For models $M_1,M_2$ and data $\mathcal{D}$, the **posterior odds** $\frac{p(M_1\,|\,\mathcal{D})}{p(M_2\,|\,\mathcal{D})}=\underbrace{\frac{p(M_1)}{p(M_2)}}_{\text{prior odds}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{p(\mathcal{D}\,|\,M_1)}{p(\mathcal{D}\,|\,M_2)}}_{\text{Bayes factor}}$ (Eq. 8.46). The **prior odds** measures how much the prior favors $M_1$; the **[[BayesFactor|Bayes factor]]** is the ratio of marginal likelihoods, measuring how well $\mathcal{D}$ is predicted by $M_1$ vs $M_2$. Under a uniform model prior the prior odds is 1, so the posterior odds is the Bayes factor itself $\frac{p(\mathcal{D}\,|\,M_1)}{p(\mathcal{D}\,|\,M_2)}$ (Eq. 8.47); Bayes factor $>1$ ⇒ choose $M_1$ (with significance guidelines, Jeffreys 1961). **Remark (Jeffreys–Lindley paradox):** "the Bayes factor always favors the simpler model since the probability of the data under a complex model with a diffuse prior will be very small" (Murphy 2012) — a *diffuse* prior makes many models a priori plausible. **Remark (computing the marginal likelihood):** generally requires solving the intractable integral (Eq. 8.44) → numerical integration (Stoer & Bulirsch 2002), Monte Carlo, or Bayesian Monte Carlo (O'Hagan 1991; Rasmussen & Ghahramani 2003); special case: a **conjugate prior** $p(\boldsymbol\theta)$ gives a closed-form marginal likelihood (done for linear regression, Ch 9).
- **8.6.4 Further reading — high-level modeling choices and information criteria** (§8.6.4, p. 288). Model-selection-worthy choices: polynomial degree (regression), number of mixture components, neural-net architecture, SVM kernel type, PCA latent dimensionality, optimizer learning-rate schedule. The automatic Occam's razor penalizes **function complexity, not literally parameter count** (Rasmussen & Ghahramani 2001) — it even works for Bayesian nonparametrics with infinitely many parameters (Gaussian processes). For MLE-focused selection there are **information criteria** (largest value wins): **[[AkaikeInformationCriterion|Akaike information criterion (AIC)]]** $\log p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta)-M$ (Eq. 8.48, Akaike 1974) corrects the MLE bias with a penalty $M$ = number of parameters; **[[BayesianInformationCriterion|Bayesian information criterion (BIC)]]** $\log p(\mathbf{x})=\log\int p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta)p(\boldsymbol\theta)\,d\boldsymbol\theta\approx\log p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta)-\frac{1}{2}M\log N$ (Eq. 8.49, Schwarz 1978), for exponential-family distributions, **penalizes complexity more heavily than AIC** ($N$ = data points).

> "One of the guiding principles of machine learning is that good models should perform well on unseen data." — §8.1, p. 251
>
> The operational definition of "good" — generalization, not training fit — that motivates the entire chapter (ERM's expected risk, regularization, cross-validation, model selection).

> "There are conceptually three distinct algorithmic phases when discussing machine learning algorithms: 1. Prediction or inference 2. Training or parameter estimation 3. Hyperparameter tuning or model selection." — §8.1.4, p. 257
>
> The chapter's organizing trichotomy of *learning* — and the source of the "inference"-overloading hazard noted in the same subsection.

> "In philosophy, this is considered to be neither induction nor deduction, but is called abduction. According to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, abduction is the process of inference to the best explanation." — §8.1.4, p. 258 (margin), citing Douven 2017
>
> The philosophical framing of the whole learning-plus-model-selection procedure — the canonical anchor for [[Abduction]] in this wiki.

> "Equation (8.6) is called the empirical risk and depends on three arguments, the predictor $f$ and the data $\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}$. This general strategy for learning is called empirical risk minimization." — §8.2.2, p. 260
>
> The definition of [[EmpiricalRisk|empirical risk]] $\mathbf{R}_{\text{emp}}(f,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})=\frac1N\sum_n\ell(y_n,\hat y_n)$ and of [[EmpiricalRiskMinimization|ERM]].

> "$\mathbf{R}_{\text{true}}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},y}[\ell(y,f(\mathbf{x}))]$ … this is the true risk if we had access to an infinite amount of data. The expectation is over the (infinite) set of all possible data and labels." — §8.2.2, Eq. 8.10, pp. 260–261
>
> The [[ExpectedRisk|expected/true risk]] — the population quantity ERM's empirical risk is a finite-sample proxy for; the gap between them is overfitting.

> "Thinking about empirical risk minimization (Section 8.2) as 'probability free' is incorrect. There is an underlying unknown probability distribution $p(\mathbf{x},y)$ that governs the data generation. However, the approach of empirical risk minimization is agnostic to that choice." — §8.2.5, p. 265
>
> The subtle point distinguishing ERM from standard statistics: ERM never *specifies* $p(\mathbf{x},y)$, and since it is a joint, labels can be non-deterministic.

> "The negative sign in (8.17) is a historical artifact that is due to the convention that we want to maximize likelihood, but numerical optimization literature tends to study minimization of functions." — §8.3.1, Remark, p. 267
>
> Why we minimize the **negative** log-likelihood — the bridge that makes [[MaximumLikelihoodEstimation|MLE]] an [[EmpiricalRiskMinimization|ERM]] instance.

> "Maximum a posteriori estimation can be considered to bridge the non-probabilistic and probabilistic worlds as it explicitly acknowledges the need for a prior distribution but it still only produces a point estimate of the parameters." — §8.3.2, p. 269
>
> The exact role of [[MAPEstimation|MAP]]: halfway between [[MaximumLikelihoodEstimation|MLE]]/ERM and full [[BayesianInference|Bayesian inference]].

> "Bayesian inference is about finding this posterior distribution … The key idea is to exploit Bayes' theorem to invert the relationship between the parameters $\boldsymbol\theta$ and the data $\mathcal{X}$ (given by the likelihood) to obtain the posterior distribution $p(\boldsymbol\theta\,|\,\mathcal{X})$." — §8.4.2, p. 274
>
> The defining statement of [[BayesianInference|Bayesian inference]] as inversion — and the integration problem (Eqs. 8.22–8.23) that distinguishes it from point estimation.

> "If all paths are blocked, then $\mathcal{A}$ is said to be d-separated from $\mathcal{B}$ by $\mathcal{C}$, and the joint distribution over all of the variables in the graph will satisfy $\mathcal{A}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathcal{B}\,|\,\mathcal{C}$." — §8.5.2, p. 281
>
> The payoff of [[DSeparation|d-separation]]: read [[ConditionalIndependence|conditional independence]] off the graph topology alone.

> "The objective of model selection is to find the simplest model that explains the data reasonably well. This concept is also known as Occam's razor." — §8.6.2, p. 285
>
> The principle behind [[ModelSelection|model selection]] / [[OccamsRazor|Occam's razor]].

> "There are some important differences between a likelihood and a marginal likelihood (evidence): While the likelihood is prone to overfitting, the marginal likelihood is typically not as the model parameters have been marginalized out … Furthermore, the marginal likelihood automatically embodies a trade-off between model complexity and data fit (Occam's razor)." — §8.6.2, Remark, p. 286
>
> Why the [[MarginalLikelihood|marginal likelihood/evidence]] is the principled model-selection criterion — and the automatic [[OccamsRazor|Occam's razor]].

> "The Jeffreys-Lindley paradox states that the 'Bayes factor always favors the simpler model since the probability of the data under a complex model with a diffuse prior will be very small.'" — §8.6.3, Remark, p. 287, quoting Murphy 2012
>
> The cautionary note on [[BayesFactor|Bayes factors]] — a diffuse prior over a complex model dilutes its evidence.

## Contradictions / notational quirks

- **"Inference" is overloaded — flagged by the authors themselves** (§8.1.4 Remark, p. 257): it can mean (canonically) *prediction with a probabilistic model*, but "sometimes" *parameter estimation of a probabilistic model*, and "less often" *prediction for non-probabilistic models*. The wiki uses [[BayesianInference]] for the §8.4 posterior-computation sense and [[Prediction]] for the test-time sense; both meanings should be read in context.
- **Empirical risk uses a bold-$\mathbf{R}$ / sans-serif convention** $\mathbf{R}_{\text{emp}}$, $\mathbf{R}_{\text{true}}$ (Eqs. 8.6, 8.10) and switches to a plain $R(f^{(k)},\mathcal{V}^{(k)})$ for the per-fold CV risk (Eq. 8.13) and $\mathbf{R}(\mathcal{V}\,|\,M)$ for the nested-CV validation risk (Eq. 8.39). The wiki's [[Generalization]] page (from [[d2l-linear-regression|D2L]]) writes the same quantities as $R_{\text{emp}}$ (training error) and $R$ (generalization error). Same content, different typography.
- **"Expected risk" vs "population risk" vs "generalization error/risk"** — §8.2.2 (margin, p. 261) notes *expected risk* is "also" called **population risk**; the [[Generalization]] page calls it **generalization error** $R=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P}[\ell]$. All three name $\mathbf{R}_{\text{true}}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},y}[\ell(y,f(\mathbf{x}))]$ (Eq. 8.10).
- **Validation set vs test set naming drifts within the chapter.** §8.2.3 introduces a *test set* for held-out evaluation; §8.2.4 then says the held-out set "is also sometimes referred to as the *validation set*" and uses $\mathcal{V}$; §8.6.1 finally fixes the *nested-CV* convention — the **inner-loop** set is the **validation set** (model choice), the **outer-loop** set is the **test set** (final generalization estimate). The single-loop §8.2 usage conflates the two; the nested §8.6.1 usage separates them. No mathematical contradiction, but the term "test set" shifts meaning between §8.2.3 and §8.6.1.
- **MAP is presented as minimizing the *negative log-posterior*** (§8.3.2, p. 269) but the [[MAPEstimation]] page (and most texts) write it as $\arg\max_{\boldsymbol\theta}[\log p(\mathcal{D}\,|\,\boldsymbol\theta)+\log p(\boldsymbol\theta)]$ — the same optimum up to the sign convention (minimize NLL+NL-prior ⇔ maximize LL+L-prior), consistent with the §8.3.1 "historical artifact" remark.
- **Parameters vs hyperparameters is "somewhat arbitrary"** (§8.1.4 Remark, p. 258) — the book is explicit that the line is pragmatic (numerically-optimizable vs search-required; or explicit-parameters vs parameters-of-the-parameter-distribution), not fundamental. The [[Hyperparameter]] page's "chosen by the programmer, not learned" framing (from [[mechanics-of-ml]]) is a special case of the search-required side.
- **$\mathbf{z}$ (latent) vs $\boldsymbol\theta$ (parameter) distinction "blurs" in later chapters** (§8.4.3 Remark, p. 277): uncertain parameters are also "latent/hidden" because unobserved, and a prior-then-integrate move turns any parameter into a random variable (§8.4.2 Remark, p. 275). Read [[LatentVariable]] $\mathbf{z}$ and parameter $\boldsymbol\theta$ as a soft, modeling-convenience distinction, not a hard one.
