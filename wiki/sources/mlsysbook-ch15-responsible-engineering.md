# Machine Learning Systems (mlsysbook Vol 1) — Ch 15: Responsible Engineering

## Summary

Chapter 15 of Vijay Janapa Reddi's *Machine Learning Systems* (Harvard, [[mlsysbook-ch01-introduction|Vol 1]], 2026), the third chapter of the **Deploy** part (Ch 13–16), reframes responsibility as **ML systems engineering done completely** rather than a separate ethics discipline. Its governing question — *"reliable for whom?"* — is contrasted with the reliability focus of the preceding [[mlsysbook-ch14-ml-operations|MLOps chapter]]: if MLOps is the control loop for *reliability*, responsible engineering is the control loop for *safety*. The central thesis is that a system can pass **verification** (meets stated requirements) while failing **validation** (does not meet users' true needs), so that a model "optimizing flawlessly for its stated objective" can cause systematic harm — the failure is in the *specification*, not the code. The chapter operationalizes this through the [[DAMTaxonomy|D·A·M taxonomy]] (Data · Algorithm · Machine), locating each responsibility failure on an axis: biased **Data** (the Fuel), a misaligned **Algorithm** objective (the Blueprint), or inadequate **Machine** infrastructure / energy cost (the Engine).

The body builds an engineering toolkit in five movements. First, the **responsibility gap**: concrete failures (Amazon's scrapped 2014 recruiting tool, [[COMPAS]] recidivism scoring, the Optum healthcare cost-proxy algorithm, [[GenderShades]] facial recognition) show optimization succeeding while systems fail, via [[ProxyVariable|proxy variables]], [[FeedbackLoop|feedback loops]] ([[GoodhartsLaw|Goodhart's Law]]), and [[DistributionShift|distribution shift]] — all *silent* failure modes invisible to aggregate monitoring. Second, a **responsible engineering checklist**: predeployment assessment, [[ModelCard|model cards]] / [[Datasheets|datasheets]], [[DisaggregatedEvaluation|disaggregated/intersectional testing]] across populations, [[Fairness|fairness metrics]] ([[DemographicParity|demographic parity]], [[EqualOpportunity|equal opportunity]], [[EqualizedOdds|equalized odds]]) computed from confusion matrices, the fairness-accuracy [[ParetoFrontier|Pareto frontier]], [[Explainability|explainability]] requirements ([[SHAP]]/[[LIME]] vs. [[Interpretability|interpretable models]]), the regulatory landscape ([[EuropeanAIAct|EU AI Act]], [[GDPR]] Articles 15/22, US sectoral law), and [[IncidentResponse|monitoring & incident response]]. Third, **environmental & cost awareness**: [[GreenAI|Green AI]] vs. Red AI, efficiency-as-responsibility ([[Quantization|quantization]] 2–4×, [[Pruning|pruning]] 50–90%, [[knowledgedistillation|distillation]] 5–20×), and a worked [[TotalCostOfOwnership|TCO]] / [[CarbonFootprint|carbon]] model showing **inference dominates training by ~40–47:1** over a system's life. Fourth, **data governance & compliance**: security/RBAC/encryption, [[DifferentialPrivacy|differential privacy]] + [[FederatedLearning|federated learning]] + [[MembershipInferenceAttack|membership-inference]] testing, GDPR/HIPAA architecture constraints, [[DataLineage|data lineage]] (Apache Atlas, DataHub) and [[AuditTrail|audit infrastructure]]. Fifth, **fallacies and pitfalls** (responsibility-can-wait, aggregate-metrics-suffice, attribute-removal-eliminates-bias, documentation-equals-accountability, responsibility-is-legal, training-not-inference carbon). Throughout, the [[LighthouseModel|Lighthouse KWS]] running example (a DS-CNN voice assistant) grounds the governance discussion.

The unifying insight: **translating responsibility concerns into measurable properties makes them tractable** — *"fairness gap <5% across groups" is actionable; "be fair" is not*. The same constrained-optimization machinery and physical invariants ([[IronLawOfMLSystems|iron law]], energy-movement, Pareto frontier) the book teaches for latency/throughput extend to fairness, carbon, and accountability as additional objectives.

## Key Claims

- **Verification ≠ validation; the bug is in the spec, not the code.** A loan model approving 95% of qualified majority applicants while rejecting 40% of equally qualified minority applicants "meets its loss function perfectly." Responsibility is a **system property, not a model property**; it is an *architectural* concern because in ML, data flows through shared representations, so a biased dataset "does not produce a localized bug; it corrupts every prediction." Maps to the [[DAMTaxonomy|D·A·M taxonomy]]: Data / Algorithm / Machine.
- **Amazon recruiting tool (Data-axis failure).** Built starting 2014 by Amazon's Edinburgh team to rate applicants 1–5, trained on ~10 years of overwhelmingly male resumes. By 2015 it penalized resumes containing "women's" and downgraded all-women's-college graduates; scrapped by 2017 after two years of failed remediation. Removing explicit gender failed because the model reconstructed it from **proxy variables** (college names, activity descriptions, career-gap patterns) — "fairness laundering": discriminating while appearing compliant.
- **COMPAS (Algorithm-axis failure).** ProPublica (Angwin et al. 2016): the recidivism tool was *calibrated* (a score of 7 = same re-offend probability across groups) but had skewed errors — Black defendants who did **not** re-offend were flagged high-risk at nearly 2× the White rate (44.9% vs. 23.5%); White re-offenders mislabeled low-risk far more often (47.7% vs. 28%). It satisfied **calibration** but violated **[[EqualizedOdds|equalized odds]]** — the **Impossibility Theorem of Fairness**: when base rates differ between groups, calibration and equalized odds cannot both hold (Chouldechova 2017; Kleinberg et al. 2016).
- **Optum healthcare proxy trap (Obermeyer et al. 2019, ~50K patients).** The algorithm predicted *future cost* as a proxy for *future need*; because the US system spends less on Black patients at equal illness, Black patients got lower risk scores at equal disease burden. Reformulating to predict illness markers directly raised the share of Black patients flagged for high-risk care management from **17.7% → 46.5%**. "Optimizing for a proxy inherits the biases of the system that generated the proxy."
- **Silent failures.** Traditional software fails *loudly* (null-pointer, timeout); ML fails *silently* because degraded predictions look normal — a sepsis model can recommend aggressive treatment for low-risk patients after an EHR change with all health checks green. A model's accuracy on a minority subgroup can drop **>30 percentage points** while aggregate metrics barely move. Failure-mode taxonomy by detection time: Crash (immediate) → Performance degradation (minutes) → Data quality (hours–days) → **Distribution shift** (days–weeks) → **Fairness violation** (weeks–months, requires redesign).
- **[[DistributionShift|Distribution shift]] is the umbrella; data drift and concept drift are its subtypes.** Defined as violation of the stationarity assumption $P_0 \neq P_t$: **data drift** = $p(x)$ shifts while $p(y\mid x)$ stable; **concept drift** = $p(y\mid x)$ itself shifts. Measured against divergence statistics (Jensen-Shannon $\mathcal{D}_{JS}$) but thresholds must be empirically calibrated per task. It is an *environmental* failure, distinct from training-time model error.
- **[[GoodhartsLaw|Goodhart's Law]] is the canonical algorithm-axis silent failure.** Clicks-vs-satisfaction correlation can decay from 0.8 → 0.2 once clicks become the optimization target ("clickbait"); YouTube radicalization pathways (Ribeiro et al. 2020) and Facebook's 2018 News Feed pivot toward "meaningful interactions" (accepting reduced reach/watch-time) both show engagement proxies are incomplete. Counterfactual evaluation (random holdouts) is needed to re-calibrate the proxy.
- **[[GenderShades]] / disaggregated evaluation.** Buolamwini & Gebru (2018, MIT Media Lab) audited Microsoft/IBM/Face++ facial recognition on the Fitzpatrick skin scale: error rates ranged from **0.8% (light-skinned males) to 34.7% (dark-skinned females)** — a **>43× disparity** invisible in aggregate accuracy. The measurement methodology *was* the intervention; Microsoft later cut worst-case error **~20×** (below 2% for all groups, Raji et al. 2019). Statistics of representation: validating a 1%-of-population subgroup to ~1pp margin at 95% confidence needs ~100× more data than the majority under random sampling — fairness requires **intentional data engineering**, not just more data.
- **Fairness has multiple conflicting mathematical definitions.** Worked loan example (Group A 10K applicants, Group B 2K): **demographic parity** disparity, equal **TPR** 90% vs 60% (a **30 percentage-point** gap → 4× higher false-negative rate for the minority group), equal **FPR** (so equalized odds violated despite matched FPR). Industry starting thresholds: error-rate ratio <1.25× for high-stakes, FPR differences <5pp for screening, selection-rate ratio ≥0.8 (the **four-fifths rule**, 1978 Uniform Guidelines / EEOC; rooted in *Griggs v. Duke Power* 1971 disparate-impact doctrine).
- **The fairness-accuracy Pareto frontier is exponential, not linear.** Perfect fairness (zero disparity) often costs significant accuracy, but a "sweet spot" (Point B: ~91% accuracy, ~3.6× fairer) usually achieves large fairness gains with modest accuracy loss. Mitigations: **threshold adjustment** (postprocessing, per-group, no retraining — Hardt et al. 2016), **reweighting** (preprocessing/importance sampling, Kamiran & Calders 2011, ~1–3% majority-accuracy cost), **adversarial debiasing** (in-processing, +20–50% training time, 1–3% accuracy cost). The "price of fairness" in the hiring scenario is a ~1.x% aggregate utility tax — *a system constraint, not a bug*.
- **Explainability vs. interpretability.** *Interpretability* is intrinsic (linear regression yes, 100-layer net no); *explainability* is post-hoc ([[LIME]] local surrogate; [[SHAP]] = Shapley values from cooperative game theory, exact computation expensive). Requirements vary by domain: credit (individual explanation, adverse-action notices), medical (clinical support), content moderation (appeal-supporting), recommendation (optional), fraud (internal-only, to prevent gaming). The "hospital shortcut" war story (Zech et al. 2018, Mount Sinai) — pneumonia models learned hospital-specific artifacts, not disease; external validation and saliency maps are QA gates.
- **Regulation turns responsibility into architecture constraints.** **EU AI Act** (Regulation 2024/1689, in force Aug 1 2024, phased): four risk tiers; Annex III enumerates 8 high-risk domains (biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, essential services incl. credit/insurance, law enforcement, migration, justice) — regulated by *what decision is made*, not model architecture. Fines: up to **EUR 35M or 7%** of global turnover (prohibited practices), **EUR 15M or 3%** (many other obligations). **[[GDPR]] Article 22** restricts solely-automated decisions; Article 15(1)(h) grants access to "meaningful information about the logic"; human review must be substantive, not "rubber-stamping." US sectoral: ECOA / Fair Housing Act (adverse-action notices), HIPAA (PHI de-identification, 6-year audit retention, up to $50K/violation, $1.5M/yr cap), FDA, employment law.
- **The automation paradox (Uber ATG 2018, NTSB).** A self-driving test car detected the pedestrian but kept re-classifying her path; automatic emergency braking was disabled, relying on a distracted safety driver. "Adding a human backup to an unreliable system does not make it reliable; it creates a new system with complex failure modes" — if the AI is 99% reliable, the human trusts it 100% and the backup is useless precisely when needed. HITL must specify review scope, confidence thresholds, reviewer training, and reviewer-performance monitoring.
- **Inference dominates total cost of ownership.** Worked recommendation-system [[TotalCostOfOwnership|TCO]]: $3,200/training cycle, ~$3,200×12 over 3 years, vs. **~$500K/yr inference** ($1.5M+ over 3 years) — a **~47:1 inference:training ratio**; breakdown ≈ training 2%, inference 73%, operations 25%. Inference costs typically exceed training by **10–1,000×** depending on traffic. A **20% inference-latency reduction via quantization saves ~$300K and a few tonnes of CO₂** — easily justifying the optimization. Carbon factor used: 400 W A100 × ~0.4 kg/kWh grid intensity = **0.16 kg CO₂e/GPU-hour**.
- **[[GreenAI|Green AI]] / carbon as a first-class metric.** Strubell et al. 2019: training one large NLP model emitted as much carbon as ~5 cars over their lifetimes, sparking Green AI. Schwartz et al. 2020: state-of-the-art gains 2012–2018 needed a **300,000×** compute increase ("Red AI"); they proposed reporting FLOPs alongside accuracy. GPT-3-scale training (~1,287 MWh × US grid ~0.4 kg/kWh) ≈ **>500 tonnes CO₂e ≈ 100+ cars/year**; each **1% pipeline-efficiency gain ≈ 1 car-year** removed. Carbon = Energy (kWh) × Carbon Intensity (kg/kWh); cloud-region selection and carbon-aware scheduling can matter more than algorithm choice.
- **Data governance has four interlocking domains.** **Security** (RBAC mapping org policy to DB permissions, S3 bucket policies, column-level security, separate feature-store read/write paths; encryption at rest + TLS 1.3 in transit; AWS/GCP KMS; code-signing for edge model updates). **Privacy** ([[DifferentialPrivacy|differential privacy]] ε-budget tracking validated via [[MembershipInferenceAttack|membership inference]]; [[FederatedLearning|federated learning]] / FedAvg = "data minimization by architecture," combined with DP because gradients leak via reconstruction attacks; k-anonymity/l-diversity/t-closeness fail against ML attacks). **Compliance** (GDPR right-to-erasure within 30 days across all derived artifacts; data minimization; cross-border localization; data cards). **Audit** (immutable append-only stores — Apache Iceberg, Delta Lake, hash chains; HIPAA 6-yr retention; a large platform may log >50B events/day). **[[DataLineage|Lineage]]** (Apache Atlas, DataHub + Airflow/Kubeflow) traces any feature back to source audio and identifies every artifact to delete on an erasure request (GDPR Article 30).
- **Meta governance fine (Jan 2023).** Irish DPC fined Meta Ireland EUR 210M (Facebook) + EUR 180M (Instagram) = **EUR 390M** for relying on contractual necessity as the legal basis for personalized ads — penalties from *insufficient governance infrastructure to demonstrate lawful processing*, not a breach. Zillow's **$304M** Q3 2021 iBuying write-down is read as a D·A·M failure (data: pandemic price volatility; algorithm: pricing uncertainty; machine: missing capacity limits / circuit breakers).
- **Fallacies & pitfalls.** Retrofitting fairness costs *quarters* vs. *weeks* at inception (6–12 months rework, or cancellation); aggregate metrics hide >40× disparities; removing sensitive attributes fails because models recover them at **70–90% accuracy** from ZIP/purchase/browsing proxies; documentation ≠ enforcement (**40–60% of models operate outside documented scope within 18 months** — scope creep); responsibility-as-legal-only ignores that engineering decisions made months earlier set the solution space; measuring only training carbon ignores the 40:1-dominant inference term.

## Key Quotes

> "Operations ensures the system runs reliably: low latency, high availability, accurate predictions. Responsible engineering asks a harder question: *reliable for whom?*" — opening framing

> "When we confuse mathematical optimization with value alignment, we build systems that are *technically robust* but *socially fragile*." — the responsibility gap

> "If MLOps is the control loop for *reliability*, then Responsible Engineering is the control loop for *safety*." — situating Ch 15 against [[mlsysbook-ch14-ml-operations|Ch 14]]

> "Eliminating explicit protected attributes without eliminating their proxies produces a model that discriminates while appearing compliant—a failure mode called 'fairness laundering.'" — on [[ProxyVariable|proxy variables]]

> "Optimizing for a proxy inherits the biases of the system that generated the proxy. The proxy-target relationship must be audited across every demographic subgroup the system serves." — Optum/Obermeyer war story

> "Adding a human backup to an unreliable system does not make it reliable; it creates a new system with complex failure modes." — the automation paradox (Uber ATG fatality)

> "A bridge that is 'safe on average' but collapses under a heavy truck is a failure. Similarly, an ML system that is 'accurate on average' but fails for a specific ethnic or gender group is an engineering failure." — the Flaw of Averages

> "The iron law governs *how fast* our systems run; responsible engineering governs *how well* they serve." — extending the [[IronLawOfMLSystems|iron law]]

> "'Fairness gap <5 percent across groups' is actionable; 'be fair' is not." — the chapter's unifying thesis on measurable properties

> "Responsible engineering is the same constrained optimization problem this book has been teaching, evaluated over a wider set of objectives that include societal impact alongside throughput and latency."

> "Responsible engineering is ML systems engineering done completely, not a separate discipline." — Summary

## Contradictions

- **No direct contradiction with the predecessor [[dmls-ch11-human-side|DMLS Ch 11]]** — it is a *systems-engineering re-framing*, not a revision. DMLS gives a 6-step process and case studies (Ofqual, Strava); mlsysbook Ch 15 reorganizes the same concerns (proxy bias, slice evaluation, model cards, fairness-accuracy trade-off) around the D·A·M taxonomy, adds quantitative TCO/carbon accounting, the EU AI Act / GDPR Article 22 regulatory layer, and a governance-as-enforcement model. The wiki's [[ResponsibleAI]] page should treat both as complementary lenses on the same discipline.
- **Inference-vs-training carbon framing reinforces [[dmls-ch07-model-deployment|DMLS Ch 7]] / Ch 13.** This chapter's "measure inference, not just training" pitfall sharpens the deployment chapters' cost-dominance message — consistent, no conflict.
- **Tension on whether responsibility belongs to engineering vs. ethics/legal boards.** Ch 15 argues *ownership must sit within engineering* (only engineers can translate fairness goals into testable properties and the architecture forecloses fixes early), explicitly pushing back on the "responsible AI = legal compliance" fallacy. This is a stronger engineering-ownership claim than DMLS Ch 11's more process/governance-distributed framing; not a contradiction, but a difference of emphasis.
- **"Removing sensitive attributes ensures fairness" is named a fallacy** — directly contradicts a common practitioner intuition (and naive readings of anti-discrimination compliance); reconciled by the proxy-variable mechanism (70–90% reconstruction accuracy).
