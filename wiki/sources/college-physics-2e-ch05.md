## Summary
Chapter 5 of OpenStax College Physics 2e applies Newton's laws to three real-world resistive and deformation phenomena: friction between surfaces, drag forces in fluids, and the elastic response of materials under stress. It moves from idealized frictionless mechanics to quantitative treatments of static and kinetic friction, velocity-dependent drag and terminal velocity, and Hooke's-law deformation governed by Young's, shear, and bulk moduli. Examples span skydiving, suspension cables, bones, and deep-ocean compression.

## Key Claims
- Friction always acts parallel to contact surfaces and opposes relative motion; its magnitude is set by the [[NormalForce]] and a coefficient, not by contact area.
- Static friction is variable up to a maximum (`f_s ≤ μ_s N`); kinetic friction is roughly constant (`f_k = μ_k N`) and usually smaller than maximum static friction.
- On an incline, sliding at constant velocity gives `μ_k = tan θ`, a practical way to measure the [[CoefficientOfFriction]].
- For large objects at moderate-to-high speed, drag scales with the square of velocity: `F_D = (1/2) C ρ A v²`.
- [[TerminalVelocity]] occurs when drag balances weight, giving `v_t = sqrt(2mg/(ρ C A))`; cross-sectional area strongly affects it, as the headfirst vs. spread-eagle skydiver examples show.
- For small particles at low speed, drag follows Stokes' law: `F_s = 6 π r η v`.
- [[HookesLaw]] states force is proportional to deformation for small displacements: `F = k ΔL`.
- Three deformation modes — longitudinal (tension/compression via Young's modulus), shear (shear modulus), and volumetric (bulk modulus) — each relate [[Stress]] to [[Strain]] linearly in the elastic region.
- Stress is force per unit area (N/m²); strain is the dimensionless fractional deformation.
- Biological tissues (tendons, bones, arteries) and engineered materials (reinforced concrete) illustrate elastic and shear behavior; bones resist shear especially well.

## Key Quotes
> Friction is the resistive force you feel parallel to two touching surfaces, and it pushes back against motion or any tendency to move. — paraphrased, intro to friction characteristics
> A falling body stops speeding up once drag grows to match gravity; that steady speed is its terminal velocity. — paraphrased, terminal-velocity definition
> Stretch or squeeze something gently and the deformation grows in direct proportion to the force applied. — paraphrased, statement of Hooke's law
