# ParProcBook Ch7: Message Passing Systems

Chapter 7 (book pp. 181–186, PDF pp. 201–206) of *Programming on Parallel Machines: GPU, Multicore, Clusters and More* by [[NormMatloff]] of [[UCDavis]]. A short six-page transition chapter that backfills the **hardware substrate** of the [[MessagePassingArchitecture|message-passing paradigm]] [[parproc-ch01-intro-parallel-processing|Ch1]] surveyed: a historical detour through 1980s–90s **[[Hypercube|hypercubes]]** (Intel and nCube), the modern [[NetworkOfWorkstations|Network of Workstations (NOW)]] / [[Beowulf]] / [[Cluster]] continuum, the *"network is literally the weakest link"* motto with [[Infiniband]] as the practical fast-interconnect, and a half-page reframing of [[ScatterGather|scatter/gather]] as the higher-level paradigm bridging [[MPI]] / [[MapReduce]] / R's [[Snow]].

## Summary

Ch7 opens with a status report on the message-passing paradigm: *"Message passing systems are probably the most common platforms for parallel processing today."* Even after 2005's *"shared-memory hardware for the masses"* arrived via dual-core commodity PCs (and the *"chips of higher core multiplicity are commercially available, with a decline of price being inevitable"* prediction), *"the message-passing paradigm continues to thrive. Many people believe it is more amenable to writing really fast code, and the advent of **cloud computing** has given message-passing a big boost. In addition, many of the world's very fastest systems (see www.top500.org for the latest list) are in fact of the message-passing type."* This single paragraph reframes Ch1's three-paradigms-of-equal-stature posture into a more pragmatic *"message-passing-still-dominates"* claim grounded in (a) cloud as a new economic substrate and (b) Top500 as empirical evidence.

**§7.2 A Historical Example: Hypercubes.** *"A popular class of parallel machines in the 1980s and early 90s was that of **hypercubes**. Intel sold them, for example, as did a subsidiary of Oracle, nCube. A hypercube would consist of some number of ordinary Intel processors, with each processor having some memory and serial I/O hardware for connection to its 'neighbor' processors."* Hypercubes are presented as commercially extinct but conceptually important: *"Hypercubes proved to be too expensive for the type of performance they could achieve, and the market was small anyway. Thus they are not common today, but they are still important, both for historical reasons (in the computer field, old techniques are often recycled decades later), and because the algorithms developed for them have become quite popular for use on general machines."*

**§7.2.1 Definitions.** *"A **hypercube** of dimension d consists of D = 2^d **processing elements** (PEs), i.e. processor-memory pairs, with fast serial I/O connections between neighboring PEs. We refer to such a cube as a **d-cube**."* PE numbering is the architecturally load-bearing detail: *"The PEs in a d-cube will have numbers 0 through D-1. Let (c_{d-1},...,c_0) be the base-2 representation of a PE's number. The PE has fast point-to-point links to d other PEs, which we will call its **neighbors**. Its ith neighbor has number (c_{d-1},...,1−c_{i-1},...,c_0)"* — i.e. *flip bit i*. Worked example: in a 4-cube (D=16), PE **1011** has four neighbors **0011, 1111, 1001, 1010** (flip each bit position once). Footnote: *"Note that we number the digits from right to left, with the rightmost digit being digit 0."*

The chapter then walks the **inductive construction** of higher-d cubes: *"To build a (d+1)-dimensional cube from two d-dimensional cubes, just follow this recipe: (a) Take a d-dimensional cube and duplicate it. Call these two cubes subcube 0 and subcube 1. (b) For each pair of same-numbered PEs in the two subcubes, add a binary digit 0 to the front of the number for the PE in subcube 0, and add a 1 in the case of subcube 1. Add a link between them."* A figure shows two 3-cubes joined into a 4-cube, with all 4-bit PE labels (0000…1111) drawn out.

**Sub-cubes, roots, partners.** *"Given a PE of number (c_{d-1},...,c_0) in a d-cube, we will discuss the i-cube to which this PE belongs, meaning all PEs whose first d-i digits match this PE's. Of all these PEs, the one whose last i digits are all 0s is called the **root** of this i-cube."* Footnote: *"Note that this is indeed an i-dimensional cube, because the last i digits are free to vary."* Worked: in the 4-cube, the 2-cube containing PE 1011 consists of {1000, 1001, 1010, 1011} (all PEs whose first two digits are 10), with root **1000**. The **partner** relation pairs up PEs across a sub-cube split: *"Given a PE, we can split the i-cube to which it belongs into two (i-1)-subcubes, one consisting of those PEs whose digit i-1 is 0 (to be called subcube 0), and the other consisting of those PEs whose digit i-1 is 1 (to be called subcube 1). Each given PE in subcube 0 has as its **partner** the PE in subcube 1 whose digits match those of the given PE, except for digit i-1."* In the 4-cube example, splitting the 3-cube {1000…1111} on bit 2: PE 1000 partners 1100, PE 1001 partners 1101, etc.

**The hypercube-vs-NOW communication asymmetry.** *"Each link between two PEs is a dedicated connection, much preferable to the shared link we have when we run, say, MPI, on a collection of workstations on an Ethernet. On the other hand, if one PE needs to communicate with a non-neighbor PE, multiple links (as many as d of them) will need to be traversed. Thus the nature of the communications costs here is much different than for a network of workstations, and this must be borne in mind when developing programs."* This is the chapter's central architectural observation: **hypercube communication cost is logarithmic in P** (max path length = d = log₂ P, hop-counted) but each link is dedicated; **[[NetworkOfWorkstations|NOW]] communication cost is whatever-the-Ethernet-tells-you** (potentially every pair-talk contends for the same shared medium), but the *topology is flat*. Algorithm designers who internalized the hypercube model can use the bit-flip-neighbor structure to engineer log-depth communication patterns; on a NOW, the topology assumptions break.

**§7.3 Networks of Workstations (NOWs).** *"The idea here is simple: Take a bunch of commodity PCs and network them for use as parallel processing systems. They are of course individual machines, capable of the usual uniprocessor, nonparallel applications, but by networking them together and using message-passing software environments such as MPI, we can form very powerful parallel systems. The networking does result in a significant loss of performance, but the price/performance ratio in NOW can be much superior in many applications to that of shared-memory or hypercube hardware of comparable number of CPUs."* The NOW is positioned as the *economic* answer to both the hypercube market collapse and the persistent expense of dedicated shared-memory hardware.

**§7.3.1 The Network Is Literally the Weakest Link.** *"Still, one factor which can be key to the success of a NOW is to use a fast network, both in terms of hardware and network protocol. Ordinary Ethernet and TCP/IP are fine for the applications envisioned by the original designers of the Internet, e.g. e-mail and file transfer, but they are slow in the NOW context."* The chapter's prescribed answer is **[[Infiniband]] (IB)**: *"A popular network for a NOW today is Infiniband (IB) (www.infinibandta.org). It features low latency, about 1.0-3.0 microseconds, high bandwidth, about 1.0-2.0 gigaBytes per second, and uses a low amount of the CPU's cycles, around 5-10%."* Three structural properties of IB are highlighted:

1. *"The basic building block of IB is a switch, with many inputs and outputs, similar in concept to Ω-net. You can build arbitrarily large and complex topologies from these switches."* (Cross-reference to [[OmegaNetwork]] from Ch3.)
2. *"A central point is that IB, as with other high-performance networks designed for NOWs, uses **RDMA** (Remote Direct Memory Access) read/write, which eliminates the extra copying of data between the application program's address space to that of the operating system."* RDMA is the modern equivalent of the hypercube's *dedicated link* in one important sense — both bypass intermediary copies — but achieved via software/firmware on commodity NICs rather than custom interconnect hardware.
3. *"IB has high performance and scalable[³] implementations of distributed locks, semaphores, collective communication operations. An atomic operation takes about 3-5 microseconds."* The footnote defines scalable: *"The term scalable arises frequently in conversations on parallel processing. It means that this particular method of dealing with some aspect of parallel processing continues to work well as the system size increases. We say that the method **scales**."* IB also supports *"true **multicast**, i.e. the simultaneous sending of messages to many nodes. Note carefully that even though MPI has its **MPI_Bcast()** function, it will send things out one at a time unless your network hardware is capable of multicast, and the MPI implementation you use is configured specifically for that hardware."* This is a load-bearing fine print: **MPI's collective operations are not magically efficient** — without hardware multicast support and a matching MPI build, `MPI_Bcast` is implemented as N point-to-point sends.

External pointers: RDMA Consortium at www.rdmaconsortium.org, a tuned-MPI-on-IB paper at nowlab.cse.ohio-state.edu/publications/journal-papers/2004/liuj-ijpp04.pdf.

**§7.3.2 Other Issues.** *"Increasingly today, the workstations themselves are multiprocessor machines, so a NOW really is a hybrid arrangement. They can be programmed either purely in a message-passing manner — e.g. running eight MPI processes on four dual-core machines — or in a mixed way, with a shared-memory approach being used within a workstation but message-passing used between them."* The MPI-everywhere style is the simpler model (every process talks via `MPI_Send`/`MPI_Recv` regardless of physical co-location); the **hybrid MPI+OpenMP** model exploits intra-node shared memory but pays for the complexity of two programming models in one codebase. The chapter does not develop the hybrid story further here.

**Terminology cleanup.** *"NOWs have become so popular that there are now 'recipes' on how to build them for the specific purpose of parallel processing. The term **Beowulf** come to mean a NOW, usually with a fast network connecting them, used for parallel processing. The term NOW itself is no longer in use, replaced by **cluster**. Software packages such as ROCKS (http://www.rocksclusters.org/wordpress/) have been developed to make it easy to set up and administer such systems."* So the modern term hierarchy is: **NOW** (older, the academic phrase) → **Beowulf** (a NOW with a fast network + a recipe) → **[[Cluster]]** (the contemporary umbrella). All three name the same machine today; **Cluster** wins in practice.

**§7.4 Scatter/Gather Operations.** *"Writing message-passing code is a lot of work, as the programmer must explicitly arrange for transfer of data. Contrast that, for instance, to shared-memory machines, in which cache coherency transactions will cause data transfers, but which are not arranged by the programmer and not even seen by him/her. In order to make coding on message-passing machines easier, higher-level systems have been devised. These basically operate in the **scatter/gather** paradigm, in which a 'manager' node sends out chunks of work to the other nodes, serving as 'workers,' and then collects and assembles the results sent back the workers. **MPI** includes scatter/gather operations in its wide offering of functions, and they are used in many MPI applications. R's **snow** package, which will be discussed in Section ??, is based entirely on scatter/gather, as is **MapReduce**, to be discussed below."* The section is a one-paragraph re-framing of the same [[ScatterGather]] pattern Ch1 introduced — but now positioned as the *generic answer to message-passing's manual-data-transfer burden*. Note the *Section ??* — an unresolved forward-reference in the source PDF, suggesting either a build-time numbering glitch or a deferred snow chapter that wasn't yet integrated when the PDF was rendered.

## Key Claims

- **Message passing is the most common platform for parallel processing today, despite the 2005 multicore-PC inflection.** *"Message passing systems are probably the most common platforms for parallel processing today."* (Ch7 opening, p. 181). *"Yet the message-passing paradigm continues to thrive. Many people believe it is more amenable to writing really fast code, and the advent of cloud computing has given message-passing a big boost. In addition, many of the world's very fastest systems (see www.top500.org for the latest list) are in fact of the message-passing type."* (§7.1, p. 181). This is a meaningful update vs Ch1's three-paradigms-of-equal-stature framing — Ch7 explicitly argues that **cloud + Top500** keep message-passing dominant.
- **Hypercubes are commercially extinct but algorithmically important.** *"Hypercubes proved to be too expensive for the type of performance they could achieve, and the market was small anyway. Thus they are not common today, but they are still important, both for historical reasons (in the computer field, old techniques are often recycled decades later), and because the algorithms developed for them have become quite popular for use on general machines."* (§7.2, p. 182). Intel and nCube (an Oracle subsidiary) were the named vendors.
- **A d-cube has 2^d PEs and each PE has d neighbors, one per bit flip.** *"A hypercube of dimension d consists of D = 2^d processing elements (PEs), i.e. processor-memory pairs, with fast serial I/O connections between neighboring PEs."* *"Let (c_{d-1},...,c_0) be the base-2 representation of a PE's number. The PE has fast point-to-point links to d other PEs, which we will call its neighbors. Its ith neighbor has number (c_{d-1},...,1−c_{i-1},...,c_0)."* (§7.2.1, p. 182). The bit-flip-neighbor structure is what makes hypercubes algorithmically distinctive — broadcast / reduce / scan algorithms can be written as **dimension-by-dimension** sweeps in d phases.
- **Higher-d cubes are inductively built by duplicate-and-prefix.** *"To build a (d+1)-dimensional cube from two d-dimensional cubes, just follow this recipe: (a) Take a d-dimensional cube and duplicate it. Call these two cubes subcube 0 and subcube 1. (b) For each pair of same-numbered PEs in the two subcubes, add a binary digit 0 to the front of the number for the PE in subcube 0, and add a 1 in the case of subcube 1. Add a link between them."* (§7.2.1, p. 183).
- **Sub-cubes, roots, and partners formalize the bit-projection structure.** An *i-cube containing PE P* = all PEs whose first d−i digits match P's; its **root** is the PE in that set whose last i digits are all 0. The **partner** of a PE in subcube 0 (across an (i−1)-split on bit i−1) is the PE in subcube 1 with all other digits matching. These structures are the data-types many hypercube algorithms recurse over. (§7.2.1, pp. 183–184).
- **Hypercube communication is structured (d hops max, dedicated links) but non-neighbor talk traverses multiple links; NOW communication is shared-medium.** *"Each link between two PEs is a dedicated connection, much preferable to the shared link we have when we run, say, MPI, on a collection of workstations on an Ethernet. On the other hand, if one PE needs to communicate with a non-neighbor PE, multiple links (as many as d of them) will need to be traversed. Thus the nature of the communications costs here is much different than for a network of workstations, and this must be borne in mind when developing programs."* (§7.2.1, p. 184).
- **A [[NetworkOfWorkstations|NOW]] is just commodity PCs networked together for parallel processing.** *"Take a bunch of commodity PCs and network them for use as parallel processing systems. They are of course individual machines, capable of the usual uniprocessor, nonparallel applications, but by networking them together and using message-passing software environments such as MPI, we can form very powerful parallel systems."* (§7.3, p. 184). The defining sentence is essentially identical to Ch1's introduction of [[Cluster]]; Ch7 confirms NOW/Beowulf/cluster are co-extensive.
- **The network is literally the weakest link.** *"Ordinary Ethernet and TCP/IP are fine for the applications envisioned by the original designers of the Internet, e.g. e-mail and file transfer, but they are slow in the NOW context."* (§7.3.1, p. 184). The chapter's named answer is **[[Infiniband]]**.
- **[[Infiniband]] specs (per Ch7): latency 1.0–3.0 μs, bandwidth 1.0–2.0 GB/s, CPU usage 5–10%.** *"It features low latency, about 1.0-3.0 microseconds, high bandwidth, about 1.0-2.0 gigaBytes per second, and uses a low amount of the CPU's cycles, around 5-10%."* (§7.3.1, p. 184). The basic building block is a switch *"similar in concept to Ω-net"* ([[OmegaNetwork]]); arbitrarily large topologies compose from switches.
- **RDMA eliminates the application/OS-space copy.** *"IB, as with other high-performance networks designed for NOWs, uses RDMA (Remote Direct Memory Access) read/write, which eliminates the extra copying of data between the application program's address space to that of the operating system."* (§7.3.1, p. 184). This is the modern-NOW analog of the hypercube's dedicated point-to-point link — both bypass copy overhead.
- **An IB atomic operation takes 3–5 μs.** *"IB has high performance and scalable implementations of distributed locks, semaphores, collective communication operations. An atomic operation takes about 3-5 microseconds."* (§7.3.1, p. 185).
- **MPI_Bcast is not multicast unless your hardware + MPI build support it.** *"IB implements true multicast, i.e. the simultaneous sending of messages to many nodes. Note carefully that even though MPI has its MPI_Bcast() function, it will send things out one at a time unless your network hardware is capable of multicast, and the MPI implementation you use is configured specifically for that hardware."* (§7.3.1, p. 185). A practical performance gotcha: **collective operations have abstraction-level semantics but implementation-level cost** — without hardware multicast, `MPI_Bcast` is O(P) sequential sends.
- **Modern NOWs are hybrid (multi-core nodes); they can be programmed pure-MPI or MPI+OpenMP.** *"Increasingly today, the workstations themselves are multiprocessor machines, so a NOW really is a hybrid arrangement. They can be programmed either purely in a message-passing manner — e.g. running eight MPI processes on four dual-core machines — or in a mixed way, with a shared-memory approach being used within a workstation but message-passing used between them."* (§7.3.2, p. 185). The pure-MPI path is simpler; the hybrid path can be faster but pays a complexity tax.
- **Terminology: NOW → Beowulf → Cluster (current).** *"The term NOW itself is no longer in use, replaced by **cluster**."* (§7.3.2, p. 185). The three names refer to the same hardware today; **cluster** is the contemporary term, **Beowulf** the recipe name, **NOW** the older academic term.
- **Scatter/gather is the higher-level paradigm that bridges MPI / snow / MapReduce.** *"Writing message-passing code is a lot of work, as the programmer must explicitly arrange for transfer of data. [...] In order to make coding on message-passing machines easier, higher-level systems have been devised. These basically operate in the scatter/gather paradigm, in which a 'manager' node sends out chunks of work to the other nodes, serving as 'workers,' and then collects and assembles the results sent back the workers."* (§7.4, p. 185). The chapter explicitly names MPI's `MPI_Scatter`/`MPI_Gather`, R's [[Snow]] package, and [[MapReduce]] as all three being scatter/gather systems.

## Contradictions

- **Ch1's three-paradigms-of-equal-stature framing vs Ch7's "message-passing dominates" claim.** Ch1 surveyed [[SharedMemoryArchitecture]] / [[MessagePassingArchitecture]] / [[SIMD]] without ranking them. Ch7 explicitly says *"message passing systems are probably the most common platforms for parallel processing today"* and backs it with cloud + Top500. This is not a contradiction so much as a **chapter-specific empirical update** — Ch1 introduces the taxonomy, Ch7 reports the contemporary market share.
- **Ch7's NOW/Beowulf/Cluster collapse vs Ch1's separable presentations.** Ch1 introduced [[Cluster]] and [[Beowulf]] as distinct ([[Beowulf]] = a cluster recipe) without explicitly mentioning NOW. Ch7 §7.3.2 retires NOW as a term in favor of cluster and explicitly identifies all three as referring to the same hardware. The earlier pages are not wrong but are made cleaner by this consolidation; updating [[Cluster]] / [[Beowulf]] with the NOW-was-the-earlier-name clarification is the natural cross-edit.
- **`MPI_Bcast` is not always a hardware multicast.** Ch1 introduced `MPI_Bcast` (implicitly, via the scatter/gather discussion) without comment on its implementation. Ch7 §7.3.1 warns that without hardware multicast and a matching MPI build, `MPI_Bcast` *"will send things out one at a time"* — i.e. O(P) sequential. This is an implementation-detail clarification that doesn't contradict Ch1 but adds an asterisk to its rosy framing.
- **No contradictions with prior Ch1–Ch6 architectural claims.** Ch7 is a backfill / clarification chapter; nothing in §7.1–§7.4 overturns earlier material. The reframing is *"message-passing is currently dominant — here's the hardware substrate it actually runs on"* rather than *"Ch1 was wrong about X."*
