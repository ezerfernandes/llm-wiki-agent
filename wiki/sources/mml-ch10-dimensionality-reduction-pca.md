## Summary

Chapter 10 (book pp. 317–347) of [[mml-book|Mathematics for Machine Learning]] is the **second of the four ML pillars** — *dimensionality reduction* — and the worked application of the linear-algebra (Ch 2), analytic-geometry (Ch 3), matrix-decomposition (Ch 4), Gaussian (Ch 6) and constrained-optimization (Ch 7) foundations. It develops **[[PrincipalComponentAnalysis|principal component analysis]] (PCA)** — proposed by Pearson (1901) and Hotelling (1933), also called the **[[KarhunenLoeveTransform|Karhunen–Loève transform]]** in signal processing — from first principles. PCA seeks a linear *coder/decoder*: an encoder $\mathbf z_n=\mathbf B^\top\mathbf x_n\in\mathbb R^M$ compresses a centered data point $\mathbf x_n\in\mathbb R^D$ to a low-dimensional code, and a decoder $\tilde{\mathbf x}_n=\mathbf B\mathbf z_n$ reconstructs it, where $\mathbf B\in\mathbb R^{D\times M}$ has orthonormal columns spanning the **[[PrincipalSubspace|principal subspace]]**. The chapter's central result is reached by **three equivalent derivations** — maximum projected variance (§10.2), minimum [[ReconstructionError|reconstruction error]] (§10.3), and a latent-variable / **[[ProbabilisticPCA|probabilistic PCA]]** generative model (§10.7) — all of which select the columns of $\mathbf B$ as the eigenvectors of the [[DataCovarianceMatrix|data covariance matrix]] $\mathbf S=\frac1N\sum_n\mathbf x_n\mathbf x_n^\top$ associated with the $M$ largest eigenvalues. It closes with eigenvector computation via [[SingularValueDecomposition|SVD]] / [[Eigendecomposition|eigendecomposition]] / [[PowerIteration|power iteration]] (§10.4), the $N\!\ll\!D$ high-dimension trick (§10.5), the practical pipeline of centering / [[DataStandardization|standardization]] / eigendecomposition / projection (§10.6), and PPCA's likelihood / MLE / relationship to [[FactorAnalysis|factor analysis]] and [[IndependentComponentAnalysis|ICA]] (§10.7–10.8). PCA is framed throughout as **lossy compression "similar to jpeg or mp3"** and as an optimal **linear [[Autoencoder|auto-encoder]]**.

## Key Claims

### Problem setting & the coder/decoder (§10.1, pp. 317–319)
- **Dimensionality reduction exploits low-dimensional structure.** High-dimensional data (e.g. a $640\times480$ color image is a point in a million-dimensional space) is "often overcomplete," with redundant, correlated dimensions, so it "possesses an intrinsic lower-dimensional structure" (§10, p. 317). Reduction is "a compression technique, similar to jpeg or mp3" (p. 317).
- **PCA is linear dimensionality reduction** proposed by Pearson (1901) and Hotelling (1933), "around for more than 100 years," used for compression, visualization, identifying patterns / latent factors (§10, p. 317). In signal processing it is the **[[KarhunenLoeveTransform|Karhunen–Loève transform]]** (p. 318).
- **The coding/reconstruction setup** (§10.1, Eqs. 10.1–10.3): for i.i.d. data $\mathcal X=\{\mathbf x_1,\dots,\mathbf x_N\}$, $\mathbf x_n\in\mathbb R^D$, with mean $\mathbf 0$ and data covariance $\mathbf S=\frac1N\sum_n\mathbf x_n\mathbf x_n^\top$ (Eq. 10.1), the **code** is $\mathbf z_n=\mathbf B^\top\mathbf x_n\in\mathbb R^M$ (Eq. 10.2) with **projection matrix** $\mathbf B=[\mathbf b_1,\dots,\mathbf b_M]\in\mathbb R^{D\times M}$ (Eq. 10.3) whose columns are **orthonormal** ($\mathbf b_i^\top\mathbf b_j=\delta_{ij}$). The aim is projections $\tilde{\mathbf x}_n=\mathbf B\mathbf B^\top\mathbf x_n\in\mathbb R^D$ that resemble the $\mathbf x_n$ while minimizing compression loss.
- **PCA is a linear [[Autoencoder|auto-encoder]] / encoder–decoder bottleneck** (Fig. 10.2, p. 319): $\mathbf z=\mathbf B^\top\mathbf x$ is the **encoder**, $\tilde{\mathbf x}=\mathbf B\mathbf z$ the **decoder**; $\mathbf z\in\mathbb R^M$ is the information bottleneck.
- **MNIST is the running example**: $60{,}000$ handwritten digits, each a $28\times28$ grayscale image $\Rightarrow\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^{784}$ (p. 320, Fig. 10.3).

### Maximum-variance perspective (§10.2, pp. 320–325)
- **Information ≈ variance ("space filling")**: PCA maximizes the variance retained in the low-dimensional code to retain maximal information (§10.2, p. 320), per Hotelling (1933).
- **Centering is WLOG** (Remark, p. 320, Eq. 10.6): $\mathbb V_z[\mathbf z]=\mathbb V_x[\mathbf B^\top(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)]=\mathbb V_x[\mathbf B^\top\mathbf x]$ — the code's variance is mean-invariant — and $\mathbb E_z[\mathbf z]=\mathbf B^\top\mathbb E_x[\mathbf x]=\mathbf 0$, so the data may be assumed centered without loss of generality.
- **First principal component = top eigenvector of $\mathbf S$** (§10.2.1): maximizing $V_1=\frac1N\sum_n z_{1n}^2=\mathbf b_1^\top\mathbf S\mathbf b_1$ (Eq. 10.9b) subject to $\|\mathbf b_1\|^2=1$ (constraint needed because scaling $\mathbf b_1$ inflates $V_1$ quadratically) gives the constrained problem (10.10). The [[LagrangeMultipliers|Lagrangian]] $\mathfrak L(\mathbf b_1,\lambda)=\mathbf b_1^\top\mathbf S\mathbf b_1+\lambda_1(1-\mathbf b_1^\top\mathbf b_1)$ (Eq. 10.11) yields $\mathbf S\mathbf b_1=\lambda_1\mathbf b_1$ (Eq. 10.13) with $\mathbf b_1^\top\mathbf b_1=1$ — i.e. $\mathbf b_1$ is the **eigenvector of $\mathbf S$ with the largest eigenvalue**, the **first principal component**; $\sqrt{\lambda_1}$ is its *loading* (standard deviation accounted for).
- **Variance along $\mathbf b_1$ equals its eigenvalue**: $V_1=\mathbf b_1^\top\mathbf S\mathbf b_1=\lambda_1\mathbf b_1^\top\mathbf b_1=\lambda_1$ (Eq. 10.15). Reconstruction of the 1-D component: $\tilde{\mathbf x}_n=\mathbf b_1 z_{1n}=\mathbf b_1\mathbf b_1^\top\mathbf x_n$ (Eq. 10.16).
- **The $m$-th PC by deflation** (§10.2.2): subtracting the effect of the first $m-1$ components, $\hat{\mathbf X}=\mathbf X-\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\mathbf b_i\mathbf b_i^\top\mathbf X=\mathbf X-\mathbf B_{m-1}\mathbf X$ (Eq. 10.17), and maximizing the variance $V_m=\mathbf b_m^\top\hat{\mathbf S}\mathbf b_m$ of the transformed data gives $\hat{\mathbf S}\mathbf b_m=\lambda_m\mathbf b_m$; crucially $\hat{\mathbf S}\mathbf b_m=\mathbf S\mathbf b_m=\lambda_m\mathbf b_m$ (Eq. 10.21), so $\mathbf b_m$ is *also* the $m$-th largest eigenvector of $\mathbf S$. The already-found PCs become eigenvectors of $\hat{\mathbf S}$ with eigenvalue $0$ (span its null space).
- **Variance retained $=\sum$ of top eigenvalues**: $V_M=\sum_{m=1}^M\lambda_m$ (Eq. 10.24); variance *lost* by compression is $J_M=\sum_{j=M+1}^D\lambda_j=V_D-V_M$ (Eq. 10.25); relative variance captured is $V_M/V_D$. The **spectral theorem** (Thm 4.15) guarantees a real orthonormal eigenbasis because $\mathbf S$ is symmetric. (Example 10.2: MNIST "8" — only a few of the 200 largest eigenvalues differ significantly from 0.)
- **Notation quirk** (Remark, p. 323): the chapter writes the data matrix $\mathbf X=[\mathbf x_1,\dots,\mathbf x_N]\in\mathbb R^{D\times N}$ with data points as **columns** — the transpose of the conventional $N\times D$ "statistics" data matrix — so the algebra works without transposing.

### Projection perspective (§10.3, pp. 325–332)
- **Same answer via minimum reconstruction error** (Pearson 1901): defining the average squared [[ReconstructionError|reconstruction error]] $J_M=\frac1N\sum_n\|\mathbf x_n-\tilde{\mathbf x}_n\|^2$ (Eq. 10.29) with $\tilde{\mathbf x}_n=\sum_{m=1}^M z_{mn}\mathbf b_m=\mathbf B\mathbf z_n$ (Eq. 10.28), and minimizing, recovers the *identical* eigenvector solution.
- **Optimal coordinates = orthogonal-projection coordinates** (§10.3.2): $z_{in}=\mathbf x_n^\top\mathbf b_i=\mathbf b_i^\top\mathbf x_n$ (Eq. 10.32). Hence (i) the optimal linear projection $\tilde{\mathbf x}_n$ is the [[OrthogonalProjection|orthogonal projection]] of $\mathbf x_n$ onto the principal subspace, $\tilde{\mathbf x}=\mathbf B(\mathbf B^\top\mathbf B)^{-1}\mathbf B^\top\mathbf x=\mathbf B\mathbf B^\top\mathbf x$ for an ONB (Eq. 10.34); and (ii) an orthogonal projection is the *best* linear map given the objective (10.29).
- **The displacement vector lives in the orthogonal complement** (§10.3.3): $\mathbf x_n-\tilde{\mathbf x}_n=\bigl(\sum_{j=M+1}^D\mathbf b_j\mathbf b_j^\top\bigr)\mathbf x_n$ (Eq. 10.38a) — the projection of $\mathbf x_n$ onto $U^\perp=\mathrm{span}[\mathbf b_{M+1},\dots,\mathbf b_D]$ (Fig. 10.9).
- **PCA = best rank-$M$ approximation of the identity** (Remark, Low-Rank Approximation, Eqs. 10.39–10.40): $\sum_{m=1}^M\mathbf b_m\mathbf b_m^\top=\mathbf B\mathbf B^\top$ is symmetric of rank $M$, and $J_M=\frac1N\sum_n\|(\mathbf I-\mathbf B\mathbf B^\top)\mathbf x_n\|^2$ — finding the basis amounts to finding the best rank-$M$ approximation $\mathbf B\mathbf B^\top$ of $\mathbf I$ ([[LowRankApproximation|low-rank approximation]], §4.6).
- **Loss = trace of projected covariance** (§10.3.3, Eqs. 10.41–10.44): $J_M=\sum_{j=M+1}^D\mathbf b_j^\top\mathbf S\mathbf b_j=\operatorname{tr}\!\bigl((\sum_{j=M+1}^D\mathbf b_j\mathbf b_j^\top)\mathbf S\bigr)$, and minimizing reduces to $J_M=\sum_{j=M+1}^D\lambda_j$ (Eq. 10.44). Minimizing the reconstruction error ⟺ minimizing the variance projected onto the orthogonal complement ⟺ **maximizing the variance in the principal subspace** — so the projection perspective yields *exactly* the same solution as the maximum-variance perspective (p. 332). Choose the eigenvectors of the $M$ *largest* eigenvalues (equivalently discard the $D-M$ smallest).

### Eigenvector computation & low-rank approximations (§10.4, pp. 333–335)
- **Two routes to the eigenvectors of $\mathbf S=\frac1N\mathbf X\mathbf X^\top$** (Eqs. 10.45–10.46): (1) **eigendecomposition** of $\mathbf S$ directly; (2) **SVD** of $\mathbf X=\mathbf U\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf V^\top$ (Eq. 10.47) — since $\mathbf S=\frac1N\mathbf U\boldsymbol\Sigma\boldsymbol\Sigma^\top\mathbf U^\top$ (Eq. 10.48), the **columns of $\mathbf U$ are the eigenvectors of $\mathbf S$** and the eigenvalues relate to the singular values via $\lambda_d=\sigma_d^2/N$ (Eq. 10.49). This links the maximum-variance view to the SVD.
- **[[EckartYoung|Eckart–Young theorem]]** (Thm 4.25, §10.4.1, Eqs. 10.50–10.51): the best rank-$M$ approximation $\tilde{\mathbf X}_M=\operatorname{argmin}_{\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf A)\le M}\|\mathbf X-\mathbf A\|_2$ is the SVD truncated at the top-$M$ singular value, $\tilde{\mathbf X}_M=\mathbf U_M\boldsymbol\Sigma_M\mathbf V_M^\top$. PCA chooses $\mathbf B$ to be the top-$M$ columns of $\mathbf U$.
- **Iterative methods beat full decomposition for a few PCs** (§10.4.2): the **Abel–Ruffini theorem** (Ruffini 1799; Abel 1826) means no algebraic solution exists for the characteristic-polynomial roots of matrices $>4\times4$, so eigenvalues are found *iteratively* (`np.linalg.eigh` / `np.linalg.svd`). When only the leading eigenvectors are needed, **[[PowerIteration|power iteration]]** is more efficient: $\mathbf x_{k+1}=\dfrac{\mathbf S\mathbf x_k}{\|\mathbf S\mathbf x_k\|}$ (Eq. 10.52) converges to the eigenvector of the largest eigenvalue (the basis of Google's **PageRank**, Page et al. 1999).

### PCA in high dimensions — the $N\ll D$ trick (§10.5, pp. 335–336)
- **Eigendecomposition of $\mathbf S\in\mathbb R^{D\times D}$ scales cubically in $D$** and is infeasible for, e.g., $10{,}000\times10{,}000$ covariance matrices (100×100-pixel images). When **$N\ll D$**, $\mathbf S=\frac1N\mathbf X\mathbf X^\top$ has rank $N$ (so $D-N+1$ zero eigenvalues).
- **The trick** (Eqs. 10.53–10.57): left-multiply the eigen-equation $\frac1N\mathbf X\mathbf X^\top\mathbf b_m=\lambda_m\mathbf b_m$ by $\mathbf X^\top$ to get $\frac1N\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\,\mathbf c_m=\lambda_m\mathbf c_m$ with $\mathbf c_m:=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf b_m$ (Eq. 10.56). The much smaller $N\times N$ matrix $\frac1N\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X$ shares the **same nonzero eigenvalues** as $\mathbf S$. Recover the original eigenvectors by left-multiplying by $\mathbf X$: $\mathbf S(\mathbf X\mathbf c_m)=\lambda_m(\mathbf X\mathbf c_m)$ (Eq. 10.57), so $\mathbf X\mathbf c_m$ is an eigenvector of $\mathbf S$ (normalize to unit norm before use).

### Key steps of PCA in practice (§10.6, pp. 336–338)
1. **Mean subtraction** — center the data by subtracting $\boldsymbol\mu$ from every point (mean $\mathbf 0$). "Not strictly necessary but reduces the risk of numerical problems" (Fig. 10.11b).
2. **[[DataStandardization|Standardization]]** — divide each dimension $d$ by its standard deviation $\sigma_d$, making the data unit-free with variance $1$ along each axis (Fig. 10.11c). This is the [[Whitening|whitening]]-style normalization step.
3. **Eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix** — compute eigenvalues / eigenvectors of $\mathbf S$ (symmetric ⇒ spectral theorem ⇒ ONB of eigenvectors); eigenvectors scaled by eigenvalue magnitude (the longer one spans the principal subspace; the covariance is the ellipse), Fig. 10.11d.
4. **Projection** — for a new point $\mathbf x_*$, standardize using the *training* mean/std $x_*^{(d)}\leftarrow(x_*^{(d)}-\mu_d)/\sigma_d$ (Eq. 10.58), then project $\tilde{\mathbf x}_*=\mathbf B\mathbf B^\top\mathbf x_*$ (Eq. 10.59) with coordinates $\mathbf z_*=\mathbf B^\top\mathbf x_*$ (Eq. 10.60). **PCA returns the coordinates (10.60), not the projections.** To map back to original data space, *undo* the standardization: $\tilde x_*^{(d)}\leftarrow\tilde x_*^{(d)}\sigma_d+\mu_d$ (Eq. 10.61, Fig. 10.11f).
- **Reconstruction quality grows with $M$** (Example 10.4, Fig. 10.12): MNIST "8" reconstructions sharpen from blurry/generic (1 PC) to near-perfect (500 PCs); 784 PCs reconstruct exactly. Average squared reconstruction error $\frac1N\sum_n\|\mathbf x_n-\tilde{\mathbf x}_n\|^2=\sum_{i=M+1}^D\lambda_i$ (Eq. 10.62) drops off rapidly (Fig. 10.13).

### Latent-variable perspective — Probabilistic PCA (§10.7, pp. 339–343)
- **PPCA** (Tipping & Bishop 1999) phrases PCA as a continuous-latent-variable generative model with a likelihood, enabling: noisy-observation handling, Bayesian model comparison via the marginal likelihood, data simulation, missing-data via Bayes, novelty detection, principled extension (mixtures of PCA), and full Bayesian treatment. Classical PCA is the **noise-free MLE special case**.
- **Generative model** (§10.7.1, Eqs. 10.63–10.67): a latent $\mathbf z\in\mathbb R^M$ with standard-normal prior $p(\mathbf z)=\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$ and $\mathbf x=\mathbf B\mathbf z+\boldsymbol\mu+\boldsymbol\epsilon$ (Eq. 10.63) with Gaussian observation noise $\boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2\mathbf I)$, giving $p(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z,\mathbf B,\boldsymbol\mu,\sigma^2)=\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf B\mathbf z+\boldsymbol\mu,\sigma^2\mathbf I)$ (Eq. 10.64). **Ancestral sampling**: sample $\mathbf z_n\sim p(\mathbf z)$, then $\mathbf x_n\sim p(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z_n,\dots)$. The directed [[DirectedGraphicalModel|graphical model]] (Fig. 10.14) has the arrow $\mathbf z\to\mathbf x$ — a low-dim latent *cause* of high-dim observations; inference "inverts" this arrow.
- **Likelihood (latents integrated out)** (§10.7.2, Eqs. 10.68–10.70): $p(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf B,\boldsymbol\mu,\sigma^2)=\int\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf B\mathbf z+\boldsymbol\mu,\sigma^2\mathbf I)\mathcal N(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)\,d\mathbf z=\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu,\mathbf B\mathbf B^\top+\sigma^2\mathbf I)$ — mean $\boldsymbol\mu$ (Eq. 10.69), covariance $\mathbf B\mathbf B^\top+\sigma^2\mathbf I$ (Eq. 10.70b). This marginal (not the conditional 10.64, which still depends on $\mathbf z$) is what MLE/MAP optimizes.
- **Joint & posterior** (§10.7.2–10.7.3): cross-covariance $\mathrm{Cov}[\mathbf x,\mathbf z]=\mathbf B$ (Eq. 10.71); the joint $p(\mathbf x,\mathbf z)$ is Gaussian (Eq. 10.72). Gaussian conditioning (§6.5.1) gives the **posterior over latents** $p(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)=\mathcal N(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf m,\mathbf C)$ with $\mathbf m=\mathbf B^\top(\mathbf B\mathbf B^\top+\sigma^2\mathbf I)^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)$ (Eq. 10.74) and $\mathbf C=\mathbf I-\mathbf B^\top(\mathbf B\mathbf B^\top+\sigma^2\mathbf I)^{-1}\mathbf B$ (Eq. 10.75). The posterior covariance $\mathbf C$ is **data-independent** and measures embedding confidence (small $\det\mathbf C$ ⇒ confident embedding).
- **MLE parameters** (§10.8, Eqs. 10.77–10.79, Tipping & Bishop 1999): $\boldsymbol\mu_{\text{ML}}=\frac1N\sum_n\mathbf x_n$ (sample mean); $\mathbf B_{\text{ML}}=\mathbf T(\boldsymbol\Lambda-\sigma^2\mathbf I)^{1/2}\mathbf R$ where $\mathbf T$ holds $M$ eigenvectors of $\mathbf S$, $\boldsymbol\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_M)$, and $\mathbf R$ is an arbitrary orthogonal matrix (so $\mathbf B_{\text{ML}}$ is unique only up to rotation — essentially an SVD); $\sigma^2_{\text{ML}}=\frac1{D-M}\sum_{j=M+1}^D\lambda_j$ (the average leftover variance in the orthogonal complement is treated as noise).
- **Noise-free limit $\sigma\to0$ recovers classical PCA**: then $\mathbf B\mathbf B^\top=\mathbf T\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf T^{-1}=\mathrm{Cov}[\mathcal X]$ (Eqs. 10.80–10.81), so (P)PCA is a **decomposition of the data covariance matrix**. EM (Roweis 1998) is recommended for streaming/MLE; Gavish & Donoho (2014) give a singular-value-threshold heuristic ($\tfrac{4\sigma\sqrt D}{\sqrt 3}$) and Minka (2001b) a Bayesian model-selection route for choosing $M$.

### Further reading & relatives (§10.8, pp. 343–347)
- **Linear auto-encoder** (Fig. 10.16, Eq. 10.76): with $\mathbf z_n=\mathbf B^\top\mathbf x_n$ the squared auto-encoding loss $\frac1N\sum_n\|\mathbf x_n-\mathbf B\mathbf B^\top\mathbf x_n\|^2$ is *identical* to (10.29) — PCA is the optimal linear auto-encoder. A nonlinear mapping gives a **nonlinear auto-encoder**; replacing both maps with deep nets gives the **deep auto-encoder** (encoder = *recognition / inference network*, decoder = *generator*). Setting deep-AE activations to identity recovers PCA.
- **Information-theoretic view**: the code is a lossy compressed version of the data; maximizing the mutual information between data and code yields the same PCA solution (MacKay 2003).
- **Bayesian PCA** (Bishop 1999) places a prior $p(\boldsymbol\mu,\mathbf B,\sigma^2)$ and integrates parameters out — automatic selection of latent dimensionality $M$ and overfitting control via MCMC / variational inference.
- **[[FactorAnalysis|Factor analysis]]** (Spearman 1904; Bartholomew et al. 2011): allows a *per-dimension* noise variance $\sigma_d^2$ instead of isotropic $\sigma^2$ — more flexible likelihood, **no closed-form MLE** (needs EM), not all stationary points are global optima; FA is invariant to scaling but *not* to rotation (PPCA is invariant to rotation but not scaling).
- **[[IndependentComponentAnalysis|ICA]]** (Hyvärinen et al. 2001): same linear-Gaussian-likelihood model but a **non-Gaussian prior** $p(\mathbf z)$ — solves blind-source separation (the "cocktail party" problem). PCA finds the subspace but, being rotation-invariant, *cannot identify the sources themselves*; ICA's non-Gaussian prior breaks the rotation symmetry.
- **Nonlinear extensions**: **kernel PCA** (Schölkopf et al. 1998) via the kernel trick (infinite-dimensional features, building on the §10.5 inner-product trick); the **Gaussian process latent-variable model (GP-LVM)** (Lawrence 2005) replacing the linear $\mathbf z\to\mathbf x$ map with a GP; **Bayesian GP-LVM** (Titsias & Lawrence 2010). Cunningham & Ghahramani (2015) survey linear dimensionality reduction; PCA, FA, ICA are its three linear examples.

## Key Quotes

> "Working directly with high-dimensional data, such as images, comes with some difficulties: It is hard to analyze, interpretation is difficult, visualization is nearly impossible, and (from a practical point of view) storage of the data vectors can be expensive. … We can think of dimensionality reduction as a compression technique, similar to jpeg or mp3, which are compression algorithms for images and music." — §10, p. 317

> "PCA, proposed by Pearson (1901) and Hotelling (1933), has been around for more than 100 years and is still one of the most commonly used techniques for data compression and data visualization." — §10, p. 317

> "In the signal processing community, PCA is also known as the Karhunen-Loève transform." — §10, p. 318

> "Retaining most information after data compression is equivalent to capturing the largest amount of variance in the low-dimensional code (Hotelling, 1933)." — §10.2, p. 320

> "to maximize the variance of the low-dimensional code, we choose the basis vector associated with the largest eigenvalue of the data covariance matrix. This eigenvector is called the first principal component." — §10.2.1, p. 322

> "the variance of the data, when projected onto an $M$-dimensional subspace, equals the sum of the eigenvalues that are associated with the corresponding eigenvectors of the data covariance matrix." — §10.2.2, p. 324

> "Minimizing the average squared reconstruction error is equivalent to maximizing the variance of the projected data." — §10.3.3, margin note, p. 332

> "the eigenvalues $\lambda_d$ of $S$ are related to the singular values of $X$ via $\lambda_d = \sigma_d^2/N$." — §10.4, Eq. 10.49, p. 333

> "Power iteration chooses a random vector $x_0$ that is not in the null space of $S$ and follows the iteration $x_{k+1} = Sx_k/\lVert Sx_k\rVert$. … This sequence of vectors converges to the eigenvector associated with the largest eigenvalue of $S$. The original Google PageRank algorithm (Page et al., 1999) uses such an algorithm." — §10.4.2, pp. 334–335

> "the PCA solution that we obtained by maximizing the variance in the projected space or by minimizing the reconstruction error is obtained as the special case of maximum likelihood estimation in a noise-free setting." — §10.7, p. 340

> "the arrow points from $z$ to $x$, which means that the PPCA model assumes a lower-dimensional latent cause $z$ for high-dimensional observations $x$. In the end, we are obviously interested in finding something out about $z$ given some observations." — §10.7.1, Remark, p. 341

> "This means we end up with the same objective function as in (10.29) … so that we obtain the PCA solution when we minimize the squared auto-encoding loss. If we replace the linear mapping of PCA with a nonlinear mapping, we get a nonlinear auto-encoder." — §10.8, p. 344

## Contradictions / notational quirks

- **Data matrix orientation** (Remark, p. 323): MML uses $\mathbf X=[\mathbf x_1,\dots,\mathbf x_N]\in\mathbb R^{D\times N}$ with samples as **columns** — the transpose of the conventional $N\times D$ statistics layout (and of Bishop 2006 / Murphy 2012, which the chapter explicitly notes). When cross-referencing Murphy Ch 20 / ISLR / scikit-learn, transpose accordingly. Hence $\mathbf S=\frac1N\mathbf X\mathbf X^\top$ here, vs $\frac1N\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X$ in row-major texts.
- **Standardization is "not strictly necessary"** (§10.6) but recommended for numerical stability — consistent with general PCA practice; note that standardizing changes which directions dominate (correlation-PCA vs covariance-PCA), a subtlety the chapter does not belabor.
- **$\mathbf B_{\text{ML}}$ is unique only up to an orthogonal rotation $\mathbf R$** (Eq. 10.78) — PPCA (and PCA) recover the *subspace*, not a canonical basis within it; this rotation invariance is exactly why PCA cannot do blind-source separation (only ICA can). Not a contradiction, but a frequently-missed identifiability caveat.
- **No contradictions with existing wiki content.** The four-derivations framing on [[PrincipalComponentAnalysis]] counts the KLT (§10.1) as a fourth "characterization"; this chapter (per the book's own §10.8 recap) presents **three derivations** (max-variance, min-reconstruction-error, latent-variable) with the KLT being a *name* for the same transform rather than an independent derivation. Both framings are internally consistent; this page follows the book's "three derivations" count and flags the KLT as an alias.
