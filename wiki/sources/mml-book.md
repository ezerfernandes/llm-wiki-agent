## Summary

[[MarcDeisenroth]], [[AAldoFaisal]] & [[ChengSoonOng]]'s 417-page [[CambridgeUniversityPress]] textbook (2020 first ed., draft revision 2024-01-15; freely available at <https://mml-book.com>) packages the **mathematical pre-requisites needed to read a modern machine-learning textbook** into a single self-contained volume. The book is split into two parts: **Part I — Mathematical Foundations** ([[LinearAlgebra]], [[AnalyticGeometry]], [[MatrixDecomposition]], [[VectorCalculus]], [[ProbabilityAndDistributions]], [[ContinuousOptimization]]) — and **Part II — Central Machine Learning Problems** ([[LinearRegression]], [[PrincipalComponentAnalysis|PCA]], [[GaussianMixtureModel|Gaussian Mixture Models]], [[SupportVectorMachine|SVM]]). The four pillars (regression, dimensionality reduction, density estimation, classification) are deliberately framed as *applications* of the six mathematical foundations, with the explicit goal of closing the "high-school-math → ML-textbook" gap. It positions itself between [[pml1-murphy|Murphy's *Probabilistic ML*]] (broader ML methods, assumes the math) and Bishop / MacKay (covers some background but only a chapter or two).

## Per-Chapter Deep Dives

- [[mml-ch01-introduction-and-motivation|Ch 1 — Introduction and Motivation]] — data/model/learning trichotomy, predictor-vs-training disambiguation, the four-pillars × six-foundations metaphor (Fig. 1.1), and bottom-up vs. top-down reading strategies.
- [[mml-ch02-linear-algebra|Ch 2 — Linear Algebra]] — systems of linear equations, matrix algebra & inverse, Gaussian elimination / row-echelon / pivots / particular+homogeneous solution, groups & vector-space axioms, subspaces, linear independence, basis / dimension / rank, linear mappings (transformation matrices, basis change, image/kernel, rank–nullity), and affine spaces.
- [[mml-ch03-analytic-geometry|Ch 3 — Analytic Geometry]] — inner products (bilinear forms, the dot product, SPD matrices via Thm 3.5) inducing norms inducing metrics; Cauchy–Schwarz → angles & orthogonality; orthogonal matrices, orthonormal bases (Gram–Schmidt), orthogonal complements & normal vectors; the inner product of functions (Fourier); orthogonal projection (onto lines, subspaces, affine spaces — the central ML operation behind regression/PCA/SVM); and rotations.
- [[mml-ch04-matrix-decompositions|Ch 4 — Matrix Decompositions]] — summarizing matrices (determinant via Laplace expansion + signed volume; trace + cyclic invariance; characteristic polynomial; eigenvalues/eigenvectors/eigenspaces; algebraic vs geometric multiplicity; defective matrices; the spectral theorem), factorizing them (Cholesky $\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^\top$; eigendecomposition/diagonalization $\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}^{-1}$; SVD $\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^\top$ — the "fundamental theorem of linear algebra"), and approximating them (low-rank/truncated-SVD + Eckart–Young optimality in the spectral norm); closes with the matrix phylogeny taxonomy.
- [[mml-ch05-vector-calculus|Ch 5 — Vector Calculus]] — the gradient-machinery chapter: difference quotient → derivative; Taylor series / polynomial (univariate + multivariate outer-product-tensor form); product/quotient/sum/chain differentiation rules; partial derivatives collected into the **gradient as a row vector** (Eq. 5.40, the deliberate row-vector convention) and the multivariate chain rule; the $m\times n$ **Jacobian** (numerator layout) with its determinant as a volume-scaling factor for change-of-variables; gradients of matrices (higher-order tensors + the flatten shortcut); the useful-gradient-identities table; **backpropagation = reverse-mode automatic differentiation** over a computation graph (forward vs reverse mode); the symmetric **Hessian** and higher-order derivatives; linearization and multivariate Taylor; Taylor-linearization for the extended Kalman filter / Laplace approximation.
- [[mml-ch06-probability-and-distributions|Ch 6 — Probability and Distributions]] — the uncertainty foundation: probability as a generalization of Boolean logic ([[CoxJaynesTheorem|Cox–Jaynes]]) with Bayesian/frequentist agnosticism; the [[ProbabilitySpace]] $(\Omega,\mathcal{A},P)$ and the [[RandomVariable]] *as a function*; [[ProbabilityMassFunction|pmf]] / [[ProbabilityDensityFunction|pdf]] / [[CumulativeDistributionFunction|cdf]]; the [[SumRule]] + [[ProductRule]] + [[BayesTheorem]] (prior·likelihood/evidence); summary statistics ([[ExpectedValue|expectation]], [[Mean]]/[[Median]]/[[Mode]], [[Variance]] with its three formulas, [[Covariance]]/[[CovarianceMatrix]]/[[Correlation]], [[EmpiricalMean|empirical statistics]]), [[StatisticalIndependence]]/[[ConditionalIndependence]], and random variables as an [[InnerProduct|inner-product space]] (correlation = cos θ); the [[GaussianDistribution|multivariate Gaussian]] (marginals/conditionals/products/linear-transforms/sampling); [[ConjugatePrior|conjugacy]] + [[SufficientStatistics]] + [[ExponentialFamily]] with [[NaturalParameters]]; and [[ChangeOfVariables|change of variables / inverse transform]] via the Jacobian determinant.
- [[mml-ch07-continuous-optimization|Ch 7 — Continuous Optimization]] — the optimization engine closing Part I: the unconstrained / constrained / [[ConvexOptimization|convex]] split (mind map Fig. 7.1); [[GradientDescent|gradient descent]] (steepest descent ⊥ contours; [[LearningRate|step-size]]/learning-rate choice + adaptive heuristics; the [[ConditionNumber]] $\kappa=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$ convergence story + [[Preconditioner|preconditioning]]) plus [[Momentum|momentum]] (heavy-ball / noise-averaging) and [[StochasticGradientDescent|SGD]] (the *unbiased-gradient-estimate* license for mini-batching); [[LagrangeMultipliers|constrained optimization via Lagrange multipliers]] — the [[Lagrangian]], primal/dual, the minimax inequality + [[Duality|weak/strong duality]], [[KKTConditions|KKT + complementary slackness]]; and [[ConvexOptimization|convex optimization]] — [[ConvexSet|convex sets]], [[ConvexFunction|convex functions]] + [[JensensInequality|Jensen's inequality]], *every local minimum is global*, [[LinearProgramming|linear]] + [[QuadraticProgramming|quadratic]] programming (with their duals), and the [[ConvexConjugate|Legendre–Fenchel transform / convex conjugate]].
- [[mml-ch08-when-models-meet-data|Ch 8 — When Models Meet Data]] — **opens Part II**, bridging the six foundations to the four pillars: data as vectors + the two model views ([[Predictor|predictor-as-function]] vs [[ProbabilisticModel|probabilistic model]]) + the three meanings of "learning" (prediction/inference, training, model selection); [[EmpiricalRiskMinimization|ERM]]'s four design choices ([[HypothesisClass|hypothesis class]], [[LossFunction|loss]], [[Regularization|regularization]], search) with [[EmpiricalRisk|empirical]] vs [[ExpectedRisk|expected risk]], [[Overfitting|overfitting]], [[CrossValidation|cross-validation]]; [[MaximumLikelihoodEstimation|MLE]] = minimize the [[NegativeLogLikelihood|NLL]], [[MAPEstimation|MAP]] = add a [[Prior|prior]] (the probabilistic regularizer); full [[BayesianInference|Bayesian inference]] (integrate over $\boldsymbol\theta$), the [[MarginalLikelihood|marginal likelihood/evidence]], [[LatentVariable|latent variables]]; [[DirectedGraphicalModel|directed graphical models]] / [[BayesianNetwork|Bayesian networks]] + [[PlateNotation|plate notation]] + [[DSeparation|d-separation]]; and [[ModelSelection|model selection]] — [[NestedCrossValidation|nested CV]], Bayesian model selection, the [[BayesFactor|Bayes factor]], [[OccamsRazor|Occam's razor]], AIC/BIC — framed as [[Abduction|abduction]]. The recurring analogy: likelihood ↔ loss, prior ↔ regularizer.
- [[mml-ch09-linear-regression|Ch 9 — Linear Regression]] — the **first of the four pillars** (regression): the Gaussian [[NoiseModel|noise model]] / likelihood (§9.1); the defining "linear in the parameters, not the inputs" claim + [[FeatureMap|feature maps]] / [[PolynomialRegression|polynomial regression]]; [[MaximumLikelihoodEstimation|MLE]] = [[LeastSquares|least squares]] via the [[NormalEquations|normal equations]] $\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{ML}}=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$ (§9.2.1); [[Overfitting|overfitting]] vs polynomial degree ([[RMSE]], best $M=4$, §9.2.2); [[MAPEstimation|MAP]] = [[RidgeRegression|ridge / regularized least squares]] with the [[Regularization|regularizer]] revealed as a negative-log Gaussian prior (§9.2.3–9.2.4); [[BayesianLinearRegression|Bayesian linear regression]] — closed-form Gaussian parameter posterior (Thm 9.1, completing the squares), [[PosteriorPredictiveDistribution|posterior predictive]] (aleatoric + epistemic variance), [[MarginalLikelihood|marginal likelihood]] (§9.3); MLE as [[OrthogonalProjection|orthogonal projection]] onto $\text{col}(\boldsymbol\Phi)$ (§9.4); [[GeneralizedLinearModels|GLMs]] / [[LogisticRegression|logistic regression]] / [[GaussianProcess|Gaussian processes]] / [[Lasso|LASSO]] (§9.5).
- [[mml-ch10-dimensionality-reduction-pca|Ch 10 — Dimensionality Reduction with PCA]] — the **second of the four pillars** (dimensionality reduction): [[PrincipalComponentAnalysis|PCA]] = the [[KarhunenLoeveTransform|Karhunen–Loève transform]], set up as a linear coder/decoder ([[Autoencoder|auto-encoder]]) $\mathbf z=\mathbf B^\top\mathbf x$, $\tilde{\mathbf x}=\mathbf B\mathbf B^\top\mathbf x$; **three equivalent derivations** — maximum projected variance (§10.2, [[LagrangeMultipliers|Lagrange]] → $\mathbf S\mathbf b=\lambda\mathbf b$), minimum [[ReconstructionError|reconstruction error]] (§10.3, [[OrthogonalProjection|orthogonal projection]] onto the [[PrincipalSubspace|principal subspace]]), and latent-variable / [[ProbabilisticPCA|probabilistic PCA]] (§10.7) — all projecting onto the top-$M$ eigenvectors of the [[DataCovarianceMatrix|data covariance]] $\mathbf S=\frac1N\sum_n\mathbf x_n\mathbf x_n^\top$, retaining variance $\sum_{m=1}^M\lambda_m$ (Eq. 10.24); eigenvector computation via [[SingularValueDecomposition|SVD]] ($\lambda_d=\sigma_d^2/N$) / [[Eigendecomposition|eigendecomposition]] / [[PowerIteration|power iteration]] + [[EckartYoung|Eckart–Young]] (§10.4); the $N\ll D$ high-dimension trick (§10.5); the practical centering / [[DataStandardization|standardization]] / [[Whitening|whitening]] / eigendecomposition / projection pipeline (§10.6); PPCA generative model + Gaussian marginal likelihood $\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu,\mathbf B\mathbf B^\top+\sigma^2\mathbf I)$ + MLE (§10.7–10.8); PCA as the optimal linear auto-encoder and its relatives [[FactorAnalysis|factor analysis]] / [[IndependentComponentAnalysis|ICA]] / kernel PCA / GP-LVM (§10.8). "Compression like jpeg or mp3."
- [[mml-ch11-density-estimation-gmm|Ch 11 — Density Estimation with GMM]] — the **third of the four pillars** (density estimation): the [[GaussianMixtureModel|Gaussian mixture model]] $p(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)$ as a convex combination of $K$ Gaussian [[MixtureComponent|components]] with [[MixtureWeight|mixture weights]] $\sum_k\pi_k=1$ (§11.1); why the i.i.d. log-likelihood $\sum_n\log\sum_k\pi_k\mathcal N(\mathbf x_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)$ has **no closed-form MLE** (the $\log$ can't enter the sum over $k$, p. 351); [[Responsibility|responsibilities]] $r_{nk}=\pi_k\mathcal N(\mathbf x_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)/\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal N(\mathbf x_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j,\boldsymbol\Sigma_j)$ as soft cluster assignments (§11.2.1); the three responsibility-weighted updates — mean $\boldsymbol\mu_k^{\text{new}}=\frac{\sum_n r_{nk}\mathbf x_n}{\sum_n r_{nk}}$ (Thm 11.1, an importance-weighted estimate), covariance (Thm 11.2), weight $\pi_k=N_k/N$ (Thm 11.3, via a [[LagrangeMultipliers|Lagrange multiplier]]); the [[EMAlgorithm|EM algorithm]] alternating E-step (recompute $r_{nk}$) and M-step (recompute parameters) with monotone log-likelihood increase to a local optimum (§11.3); the discrete [[LatentVariable|latent-variable]] view — one-hot indicator $\mathbf z$, $\pi_k=p(z_k=1)$, ancestral sampling, the marginalized likelihood, and $r_{nk}$ as the Bayes posterior $p(z_k=1\mid\mathbf x)$ (§11.4); $K$-means as the hard-assignment special case, plus [[Histogram|histograms]] and [[KernelDensityEstimation|kernel density estimation]] (§11.5). The discrete-latent peer of Ch 10's continuous-latent PPCA.
- [[mml-ch12-classification-svm|Ch 12 — Classification with SVM]] — the **fourth and last of the four pillars** (binary classification) and the book's closing chapter: the [[SupportVectorMachine|support vector machine]] built as the **geometric / loss-function-first counterpart to probabilistic regression** — a [[SeparatingHyperplane|separating hyperplane]] $\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle+b=0$ ($\mathbf{w}$ normal, §12.1); the [[Margin|margin]] $r=1/\|\mathbf{w}\|$ via [[OrthogonalProjection|orthogonal projection]] (§12.2.1, Eq. 12.14); the [[HardMarginSVM|hard-margin]] QP $\min\frac12\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$ s.t. $y_n(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_n\rangle+b)\ge1$ (Eqs. 12.18–19), the [[SoftMarginSVM|soft-margin]] relaxation with [[SlackVariable|slack]] $\xi_n$ + box $C$ (Eqs. 12.26a–c), and the equivalent [[HingeLoss|hinge-loss]] regularized-[[EmpiricalRiskMinimization|ERM]] form $\frac12\|\mathbf{w}\|^2+C\sum_n\max\{0,1-y_nf(\mathbf{x}_n)\}$ (Eq. 12.31, margin = $\ell_2$ regularizer); the [[DualSVM|dual SVM]] via [[LagrangianDuality|Lagrangian duality]] (Eq. 12.41, $N$-variable QP, box $0\le\alpha_n\le C$), the representer theorem $\mathbf{w}=\sum_n\alpha_ny_n\mathbf{x}_n$ (Eq. 12.38) and [[KKTConditions|complementary slackness]] → [[SupportVector|support vectors]] ($\alpha_n>0$), plus the [[ConvexHull|convex-hull]] reading (§12.3.2); the [[KernelTrick|kernel trick]] — replace $\langle\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j\rangle$ by a PSD [[KernelFunction|kernel]] ([[RBFKernel|RBF]]/polynomial, [[GramMatrix|Gram matrix]] $\succeq0$, Eq. 12.53) to lift to nonlinear classifiers without computing $\boldsymbol\phi$ (§12.4, Fig. 12.10); and the numerical solution by subgradient/[[StochasticGradientDescent|SGD]] on the hinge or standard-form [[QuadraticProgramming|QP]] solvers (§12.5). **This completes the per-chapter four-pillars arc** (regression Ch 9 / dimensionality-reduction Ch 10 / density-estimation Ch 11 / classification Ch 12).

## Key Claims

- **A practitioner who only uses ML libraries risks not knowing the *design decisions and limits* of the algorithms** (Foreword, p. 1). The book is written to surface those decisions through their mathematical content.
- **Three audience types** (Foreword, pp. 2–3) — *Astute Listener* (informed consumer; needs background for ethics/fairness/risk-management of ML), *Experienced Artist* (practitioner extending favorite methods), *Fledgling Composer* (researcher developing new methods). Same book, different reading modes (top-down vs bottom-up; §1.2).
- **Three core ML concepts** (§1, p. 11): *data*, *model*, *learning*. Data → vectors; models → simplified data-generating processes (probabilistic or as functions); learning → optimization of model parameters against a utility / loss function over training data, with the goal of generalizing to unseen data.
- **Four pillars × six foundations** (§1.2 / Fig. 1.1): regression / dim-reduction / density-estimation / classification sit on top of linear algebra, analytic geometry, matrix decomposition, vector calculus, probability & distributions, and continuous optimization.
- **Linear algebra is the substrate** (Ch 2): vectors are anything closed under addition and scalar multiplication — geometric vectors, polynomials, audio signals, $\mathbb{R}^n$ tuples. ML largely lives in $\mathbb{R}^n$. The chapter's mind map (Fig. 2.2): Vector → Vector Space (closure) → Linear Independence → Basis; Matrix → System of Linear Equations → Gaussian Elimination; Linear Mapping connects them.
- **Analytic geometry adds geometry to linear algebra** (Ch 3): [[InnerProduct]]s induce [[Norm]]s induce metrics. [[CauchySchwarzInequality]] defines [[Angle]]. [[OrthogonalProjection]] is *the* operation behind least-squares regression (Ch 9), PCA (Ch 10), and SVM margin (Ch 12). [[SymmetricPositiveDefiniteMatrix|Symmetric positive definite matrices]] characterize all valid inner products in $\mathbb{R}^n$ (Thm 3.5).
- **Matrix decomposition = matrix factoring** (Ch 4, p. 98): "An analogy for matrix decomposition is the factoring of numbers, such as the factoring of 21 into prime numbers 7·3." Determinant + trace summarize a matrix in numbers; Cholesky, Eigendecomposition, SVD factor it into interpretable factors. **The determinant is the signed volume of the parallelepiped spanned by columns** (§4.1, Example 4.2) — the geometric reading that justifies its later appearance in change-of-variables (§6.7) and Gaussian densities (§6.5).
- **Vector calculus = the gradient machinery for optimization** (Ch 5). Partial derivatives collected in a [[Jacobian]] (§5.2 — the row-vector convention is a deliberate choice). [[TaylorSeries]] extends to multivariate Taylor (§5.8) for [[Linearization]]; [[Backpropagation]] is automatic differentiation via the chain rule (§5.6) and is the mathematical content of neural-net training.
- **Probability is a generalization of Boolean logic** (Ch 6, §6.1.1 — citing Jaynes 2003). [[CoxJaynesTheorem]] proves that any internally consistent assignment of plausibilities must obey the rules of probability. The book is *agnostic* between Bayesian and frequentist interpretations (§6.1, Remark) but routinely uses both. A [[ProbabilitySpace]] = (sample space $\Omega$, event space $\mathcal{A}$, probability $P$); a [[RandomVariable]] is a function $X:\Omega\to\mathcal{T}$, not a variable. *"The name 'random variable' is a great source of misunderstanding as it is neither random nor it is a variable. It is a function."* (§6.1.2, marginal note, p. 175).
- **Optimization splits two ways** (Ch 7): unconstrained ([[GradientDescent]] + [[Momentum]] + [[StochasticGradientDescent|SGD]], §7.1) vs constrained ([[LagrangeMultipliers]], §7.2) plus the special case of [[ConvexOptimization]] (§7.3) where *every local minimum is global* and duality theory applies. The [[ConditionNumber]] $\kappa = \sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$ (§7.1.1) — the ratio of largest to smallest singular value — controls gradient-descent convergence speed.
- **Empirical Risk Minimization is the framing of supervised learning** (§8.2, p. 258). [[MaximumLikelihoodEstimation|MLE]] (§8.3) is its probabilistic counterpart; full Bayesian inference (§8.4) integrates over the parameter uncertainty rather than picking a point estimate. **Model selection happens at a higher level** (§8.6) via [[NestedCrossValidation|nested cross-validation]] — and philosophically is *[[Abduction|abduction]]*, not induction or deduction (§8.2 marginal).
- **Linear regression is "linear in the parameters, not in the inputs"** (Ch 9, §9.2 remark, p. 295). A polynomial regression is a *linear* model because $\boldsymbol\theta$ enters linearly through a [[FeatureMap]] $\phi(\mathbf{x})$. The MLE closed form $\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{ML}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$ is derived as both an [[OrthogonalProjection]] (§9.4) and the gradient-zero solution to squared-error NLL (§9.2.1). [[BayesianLinearRegression]] (§9.3) integrates parameters out instead.
- **PCA is the [[KarhunenLoeveTransform]]** (Ch 10, p. 318). Three equivalent derivations (§10.2 maximum variance, §10.3 minimum reconstruction error, §10.7 latent-variable / probabilistic PCA) all yield the same answer: project onto the $M$ eigenvectors of the [[DataCovarianceMatrix]] $\mathbf{S}=\frac{1}{N}\sum \mathbf{x}_n\mathbf{x}_n^\top$ with the largest eigenvalues. The variance retained by an $M$-dim projection equals $\sum_{m=1}^M \lambda_m$ (Eq. 10.23). PCA is "compression like JPEG or MP3" without losing the dominant signal.
- **The EM algorithm is the workaround for the no-closed-form GMM MLE** (Ch 11, §11.2–11.3). The log-likelihood $\sum_n \log \sum_k \pi_k \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n|\boldsymbol\mu_k, \boldsymbol\Sigma_k)$ cannot be optimized in closed form (the log can't enter the sum over $k$, p. 351). The fix is to introduce [[Responsibility|responsibilities]] $r_{nk}$ (soft cluster assignments, §11.2.1) and alternate: E-step recomputes $r_{nk}$ given parameters; M-step recomputes $(\boldsymbol\mu_k, \boldsymbol\Sigma_k, \pi_k)$ given $r_{nk}$. The mean update $\boldsymbol\mu_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{\sum r_{nk}\mathbf{x}_n}{\sum r_{nk}}$ is an importance-weighted Monte Carlo estimate.
- **The SVM is the geometric counterpart to MLE-driven linear regression** (Ch 12, p. 371). Where Ch 9 starts from a probabilistic model and derives an optimization problem, the SVM *starts from the loss function* (maximize the [[Margin]] $r$, geometrically) and arrives at a [[ConvexOptimization]] problem (quadratic programming) with no analytic solution. Hard-margin: $\min \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$ s.t. $y_n(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}_n\rangle+b)\geq 1$. The dual (Ch 12.3) exposes support vectors; the kernel trick (Ch 12.4) lifts the SVM to nonlinear classifiers without ever computing $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ explicitly.
- **The book intentionally provides only four representative ML algorithms** (Foreword, p. 2): linear regression, PCA, GMM, SVM. These are *worked examples* of the foundations, not a survey — for that, the book defers to Bishop / MacKay / Murphy / Shalev-Shwartz–Ben-David.

## Key Quotes

> "Machine learning is the latest in a long line of attempts to distill human knowledge and reasoning into a form that is suitable for constructing machines and engineering automated systems." — Foreword, p. 1
> 
> Framing for why mathematical foundations matter: ML libraries hide the math, but the *design decisions* sit in the math.

> "We have found many people who want to delve into the foundations of basic machine learning methods who struggle with the mathematical knowledge required to read a machine learning textbook. Having taught undergraduate and graduate courses at universities, we find that the gap between high school mathematics and the mathematics level required to read a standard machine learning textbook is too big for many people. This book brings the mathematical foundations of basic machine learning concepts to the fore..." — Foreword, p. 1
> 
> The mission statement. This book exists to close the gap that Murphy/Bishop/MacKay assume away.

> "Math is linked in the popular mind with phobia and anxiety. You'd think we're discussing spiders." — Strogatz 2014, quoted in Foreword margin, p. 2

> "We provide only four representative examples of machine learning algorithms. Instead, we focus on the mathematical concepts behind the models themselves." — Foreword, p. 2
> 
> Frames the book's scope: this is a *math* book applied to ML, not an ML survey.

> "The name 'random variable' is a great source of misunderstanding as it is neither random nor it is a variable. It is a function." — §6.1.2 marginal, p. 175
> 
> Cuts through the standard naming confusion at the foundation of probability.

> "Linear regression refers to models that are linear in the parameters, but the inputs can undergo any nonlinear transformation." — §9.2 marginal, p. 295
> 
> The distinction that lets [[PolynomialRegression]] / [[KernelRegression]] all sit under the "linear regression" framework.

> "Working directly with high-dimensional data, such as images, comes with some difficulties: It is hard to analyze, interpretation is difficult, visualization is nearly impossible, and (from a practical point of view) storage of the data vectors can be expensive." — §10, p. 317
> 
> The motivation for dimensionality reduction — and implicitly for the [[CurseOfDimensionality]] discussed in Corpus II / VI.

> "The SVM view of machine learning is subtly different from the maximum likelihood view of Chapter 9. The maximum likelihood view proposes a model based on a probabilistic view of the data distribution, from which an optimization problem is derived. In contrast, the SVM view starts by designing a particular function that is to be optimized during training, based on geometric intuitions." — §12, p. 371
> 
> The book's clearest statement that two equally valid framings of supervised ML exist: probabilistic (Ch 9, 11) and geometric (Ch 10, 12).

## Contradictions

- **Murphy's "self-supervised learning as a recently popular approach"** (Murphy 2022) reads as already dated against Corpus II — pretraining-then-finetune is the default in 2026. MML doesn't take a position on self-supervision at all (the book predates the BERT-era explosion as the conceptual core of ML), making it *consistent with* the assumption-free Murphy reading but *silently behind* Corpus II.
- **MML's Ch 5 row-vector gradient convention** (Eq. 5.40, p. 146; full treatment on [[mml-ch05-vector-calculus|the Ch 5 deep dive]]): "the row vector in (5.40) is called the gradient of $f$ or the Jacobian." This differs from Murphy / most deep learning texts where the gradient is a column vector. Same math, different transpose conventions — a known source of confusion when reading multiple references.
- **MML defines linear regression as $f(\mathbf{x}) = \boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf{x}$ (no bias)** (Eq. 9.4, p. 291) — adding the bias requires the $x^{(0)}=1$ augmentation trick (Example 8.1, p. 259). This contrasts with the [[madewithml-foundations-linear-regression]] convention of carrying $\theta_0$ separately. Both are correct, but cross-referencing the formulas requires noting the augmentation.
