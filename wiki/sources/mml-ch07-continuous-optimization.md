## Summary

Chapter 7 (book pp. 225–248) of [[mml-book|Mathematics for Machine Learning]] closes **Part I — Mathematical Foundations** and is the optimization engine that turns every probabilistic/loss-based model in Part II into something a computer can actually train. Because ML algorithms run on computers, the mathematical formulations become **numerical [[ContinuousOptimization|continuous optimization]]** problems — continuous (variables in $\mathbb{R}^D$) as opposed to *combinatorial* (discrete variables). The chapter assumes the objective $f:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}$ is **differentiable** (so a gradient exists everywhere) and, by ML convention, that we **minimize** (the gradient points uphill, so we walk downhill). It splits continuous optimization into two main branches plus a special, well-behaved class (mind map, Fig. 7.1, p. 226): **(1) unconstrained optimization via [[GradientDescent|gradient descent]]** (§7.1 — [[LearningRate|step-size]]/learning rate, [[Momentum|momentum]], [[StochasticGradientDescent|stochastic gradient descent]], and the [[ConditionNumber|condition-number]] convergence story); **(2) constrained optimization via [[LagrangeMultipliers|Lagrange multipliers]]** (§7.2 — the [[Lagrangian]], the primal/[[LagrangianDuality|dual]] problems, weak duality, and KKT/complementary slackness); and **(3) [[ConvexOptimization|convex optimization]]** (§7.3 — [[ConvexSet|convex sets]], [[ConvexFunction|convex functions]], [[JensensInequality|Jensen's inequality]], [[LinearProgramming|linear programming]], [[QuadraticProgramming|quadratic programming]], and the [[ConvexConjugate|Legendre–Fenchel transform]]) where, crucially, **every local minimum is a global minimum** and **strong duality** holds. The arrows in the mind map point forward: Lagrange multipliers feed Ch 10 (dimensionality reduction / PCA) and Ch 11 (density estimation / GMM); quadratic programming feeds Ch 12 (classification / [[SupportVectorMachine|SVM]]). The chapter explicitly defers more general (non-differentiable) settings to §7.4 and leans on Boyd & Vandenberghe (2004) for the convex-optimization machinery.

## Key Claims

### The split: unconstrained vs constrained vs convex (§7, pp. 225–227)

- **Continuous optimization splits two ways — unconstrained and constrained — with convex optimization as a special, tractable class** (§7, p. 225, mind map Fig. 7.1 p. 226). For unconstrained optimization "this [the gradient pointing downhill] is the only concept we need," but several **design choices** (step-size, momentum, stochasticity) arise; constrained optimization needs extra machinery (Lagrange multipliers, §7.2); convex problems (§7.3) are the special class where "we can make statements about reaching the global optimum."
- **Differentiability + minimization conventions** (§7, p. 225). The book assumes $f$ is differentiable so a gradient is available everywhere (it points "uphill," toward steepest ascent — recall [[mml-ch05-vector-calculus|Ch 5]] / [[Gradient]]); and by convention ML objectives are **minimized**, so we move in the negative-gradient direction. "Intuitively finding the best value is like finding the valleys of the objective function, and the gradients point us uphill."
- **Stationary points come from setting the derivative to zero, classified by the second derivative** (§7, pp. 225–226). For the running cubic-gradient example $\ell(x)=x^4+7x^3+5x^2-17x+3$ (Eq. 7.1) the gradient $\frac{d\ell}{dx}=4x^3+21x^2+10x-17$ (Eq. 7.2) is a cubic with three real roots; the second derivative $\frac{d^2\ell}{dx^2}=12x^2+42x+10$ (Eq. 7.3) classifies them ($<0$ ⇒ maximum at $x\approx-1.4$; $>0$ ⇒ the two minima at $x\approx-4.5,\,0.7$). Marginal note: the **global minimum** is near $x=-4.5$ ($\ell\approx-47$); a competing **local minimum** sits near $x=0.7$. By the **Abel–Ruffini theorem** (Abel 1826) there is in general no algebraic solution for polynomials of degree 5+ — so analytic root-finding fails in general and iterative gradient methods are needed (margin, p. 227).
- **Where you start matters in the non-convex case; convexity removes that dependence** (§7, p. 227). Starting at $x_0=-6$ the negative gradient walks toward the deep (global) minimum; starting at $x_0=0$ it walks to the shallow (local) one. *"For convex functions all local minimums are global minimum"* (margin, p. 227) — this is the entire payoff of §7.3, and "many machine learning objective functions are designed such that they are convex" (forward-ref to Ch 12 / [[SupportVectorMachine|SVM]]).

### 7.1 Optimization Using Gradient Descent (pp. 227–233)

- **Gradient descent is a first-order method that steps in the negative-gradient direction** (§7.1, p. 228). To solve $\min_{\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x})$ (Eq. 7.4) for differentiable $f$ with no closed form, iterate $\mathbf{x}_{i+1}=\mathbf{x}_i-\gamma_i\big((\nabla f)(\mathbf{x}_i)\big)^\top$ (Eq. 7.6; single-step form Eq. 7.5). The negative gradient $-((\nabla f)(\mathbf{x}_0))^\top$ is the direction of **steepest descent** and is **orthogonal to the contour lines** $f(\mathbf{x})=c$. For a suitable step-size $\gamma_i$, the sequence $f(\mathbf{x}_0)\geq f(\mathbf{x}_1)\geq\dots$ converges to a local minimum. Note the **transpose** on the gradient — the book uses the **row-vector convention** for gradients (margin, p. 228; carried over from [[mml-ch05-vector-calculus|Ch 5]] Eq. 5.40), so a transpose is needed to make the update dimensions work.
- **Worked 2-D quadratic** (Example 7.1, p. 228–229). $f(\mathbf{x})=\tfrac12\mathbf{x}^\top\!\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&20\end{bmatrix}\!\mathbf{x}-\begin{bmatrix}5\\3\end{bmatrix}^\top\!\mathbf{x}$ (Eq. 7.7) has gradient $\nabla f=\mathbf{x}^\top\!\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&20\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}5\\3\end{bmatrix}^\top$ (Eq. 7.8); from $\mathbf{x}_0=[-3,-1]^\top$ with $\gamma=0.085$ the iterates zig-zag toward the minimum (Fig. 7.3 heatmap; $\mathbf{x}_1=[-1.98,1.21]^\top$, $\mathbf{x}_2=[-1.32,-0.42]^\top$).
- **Gradient descent is slow near the minimum and zig-zags on poorly conditioned problems** (Remark, p. 229). Its asymptotic convergence rate is inferior to many other methods (Trefethen & Bau III 1997). On a long, thin valley the problem is poorly conditioned and "gradient descent increasingly 'zigzags' as the gradients point nearly orthogonally to the shortest direction to a minimum point" (Fig. 7.3).
- **7.1.1 Step-size (= learning rate)** (§7.1.1, pp. 229–230). The **step-size** $\gamma$ is "also called the learning rate" (margin, p. 229). Too small ⇒ slow; too large ⇒ overshoot, fail to converge, or even **diverge**. Two simple **adaptive heuristics** (Toussaint 2012): if the function value *increases* after a step, the step was too large — **undo the step and decrease** $\gamma$ (this guarantees monotonic convergence); if it *decreases*, the step could have been larger — **increase** $\gamma$. Example 7.2 (Solving a linear equation system, p. 230): solving $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ approximately by minimizing $\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^2=(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b})^\top(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b})$ (Eq. 7.9) has gradient $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}=2(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b})^\top\mathbf{A}$ (Eq. 7.10), usable directly in GD — though this special case has the analytic least-squares solution (forward-ref to Ch 9 / [[LinearRegression]]).
- **Convergence speed is governed by the condition number; preconditioning fixes it** (Remark, p. 230). For $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$, GD's convergence depends on the **[[ConditionNumber|condition number]]** $\kappa=\frac{\sigma(\mathbf{A})_{\max}}{\sigma(\mathbf{A})_{\min}}$ (ratio of max to min singular value, §4.5) — "the ratio of the most curved direction versus the least curved direction," i.e. long thin valleys. A **[[Preconditioner|preconditioner]]** $\mathbf{P}$ replaces $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ with $\mathbf{P}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b})=\mathbf{0}$, chosen so $\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{A}$ has a *better* condition number while $\mathbf{P}^{-1}$ stays cheap to compute (further reading: Boyd & Vandenberghe 2004, ch. 9).
- **7.1.2 Gradient descent with momentum gives the update a memory** (§7.1.2, pp. 230–231). Introduced by Rumelhart et al. (1986) and motivated as a fix for the zig-zag of Fig. 7.3 (poorly scaled curvature). The momentum term "emulates the phenomenon of a heavy ball that is reluctant to change directions," implementing a **moving average** that dampens oscillation and smooths erratic updates: $\mathbf{x}_{i+1}=\mathbf{x}_i-\gamma_i\big((\nabla f)(\mathbf{x}_i)\big)^\top+\alpha\,\Delta\mathbf{x}_i$ (Eq. 7.11) with $\Delta\mathbf{x}_i=\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_{i-1}=\alpha\,\Delta\mathbf{x}_{i-1}-\gamma_{i-1}\big((\nabla f)(\mathbf{x}_{i-1})\big)^\top$ (Eq. 7.12), $\alpha\in[0,1]$. Beyond acceleration, momentum is useful when the gradient is only known approximately — it **averages out noisy estimates** (a bridge to SGD). (Margin: Goh 2017 wrote an intuitive blog post.)
- **7.1.3 Stochastic gradient descent is a cheap, unbiased gradient estimate** (§7.1.3, pp. 231–233). When the objective is a sum of per-example losses $L(\boldsymbol\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^N L_n(\boldsymbol\theta)$ (Eq. 7.13) — e.g. the regression negative log-likelihood $L(\boldsymbol\theta)=-\sum_n\log p(y_n\mid\mathbf{x}_n,\boldsymbol\theta)$ (Eq. 7.14, forward-ref to Ch 9) — the **batch** update $\boldsymbol\theta_{i+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_i-\gamma_i\sum_{n=1}^N\big(\nabla L_n(\boldsymbol\theta_i)\big)^\top$ (Eq. 7.15) requires summing all $N$ gradients, which is expensive at scale. **SGD** ("stochastic" = "we acknowledge that we do not know the gradient precisely, but instead only know a noisy approximation") sums over a smaller **mini-batch** (extreme case: a single $L_n$). **The key insight: GD only requires the gradient to be an *unbiased estimate* of the true gradient** — the full sum is an empirical estimate of the expected value (§6.4.1), so *any* unbiased subsample suffices for convergence. With an appropriately decreasing learning rate and mild assumptions, SGD converges almost surely to a local minimum (Bottou 1998). Mini-batch size is a **variance/cost trade-off**: large batches → accurate, low-variance, hardware-friendly (vectorized matmuls), more stable but costlier; small batches → cheap, noisy, and the noise can **escape bad local optima** (helpful since ML cares about generalization, Ch 8, not exact minimization). SGD is "the current workhorse of large-scale machine learning" (deep nets on millions of images, topic models, RL, large-scale GPs; Bottou et al. 2018).

### 7.2 Constrained Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers (pp. 233–236)

- **The constrained problem** (§7.2, p. 233). Minimize $f(\mathbf{x})$ subject to $g_i(\mathbf{x})\leq 0$ for $i=1,\dots,m$ (Eq. 7.17), with $f,g_i:\mathbb{R}^D\to\mathbb{R}$ possibly non-convex in general (the convex case is §7.3). Illustration (Fig. 7.4, p. 233): box constraints $-1\le x\le 1,\,-1\le y\le 1$ push the unconstrained minimum (a circle outside the box) to a constrained optimum on the box boundary (a star).
- **The naive indicator-function reformulation is intractable** (Eqs. 7.18–7.19, pp. 233–234). One could convert to unconstrained form $J(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{i=1}^m \mathbf{1}(g_i(\mathbf{x}))$ (Eq. 7.18) with the infinite step penalty $\mathbf{1}(z)=0$ if $z\le 0$ else $\infty$ (Eq. 7.19) — same solution, but "equally difficult to optimize." The fix: **replace the step function with a linear function** via Lagrange multipliers.
- **The Lagrangian relaxes the indicator into a linear penalty** (Eq. 7.20, p. 234). Associate non-negative **Lagrange multipliers** $\lambda_i\geq 0$ with each inequality (Boyd & Vandenberghe 2004, ch. 4): $\mathfrak{L}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\lambda)=f(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{i=1}^m\lambda_i g_i(\mathbf{x})$ (Eq. 7.20a) $=f(\mathbf{x})+\boldsymbol\lambda^\top\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})$ (Eq. 7.20b), stacking constraints into $\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})$ and multipliers into $\boldsymbol\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^m$. See [[Lagrangian]], [[LagrangeMultipliers]].
- **Lagrangian duality: convert a constrained primal into an unconstrained dual** (Def. 7.1, p. 234). **Duality = converting a problem in the primal variables $\mathbf{x}$ into one in dual variables $\boldsymbol\lambda$.** The **primal problem** is Eq. 7.21 ($\min_{\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x})$ s.t. $g_i(\mathbf{x})\le 0$). The **Lagrangian dual problem** is $\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^m}\mathfrak{D}(\boldsymbol\lambda)$ subject to $\boldsymbol\lambda\geq\mathbf{0}$ (Eq. 7.22), where the **dual function** $\mathfrak{D}(\boldsymbol\lambda)=\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d}\mathfrak{L}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\lambda)$. (The book introduces *two* dualities: **Lagrangian duality** here, and **Legendre–Fenchel duality** in §7.3.3.) See [[LagrangianDuality]].
- **The minimax inequality and weak duality** (Eqs. 7.23–7.27, pp. 234–235). The **minimax inequality**: for any two-argument $\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})$, $\max_{\mathbf{y}}\min_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq\min_{\mathbf{x}}\max_{\mathbf{y}}\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})$ (Eq. 7.23) ("the maximin is less than the minimax"), proved from $\min_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi\le\max_{\mathbf{y}}\varphi$ (Eq. 7.24). Since the relaxed Lagrangian lower-bounds the indicator objective ($\lambda\ge0$), $J(\mathbf{x})=\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda\ge0}\mathfrak{L}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\lambda)$ (Eq. 7.25), and the primal is $\min_{\mathbf{x}}\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda\ge0}\mathfrak{L}$ (Eq. 7.26). Swapping the order gives **weak duality**: $\min_{\mathbf{x}}\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda\ge0}\mathfrak{L}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\lambda)\geq\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda\ge0}\min_{\mathbf{x}}\mathfrak{L}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\lambda)$ (Eq. 7.27) — **primal values are always ≥ dual values**; the inner $\min_{\mathbf{x}}\mathfrak{L}$ on the right is exactly $\mathfrak{D}(\boldsymbol\lambda)$.
- **The dual is always a concave maximization — even when the primal is non-convex** (p. 235). For a fixed $\boldsymbol\lambda$, $\min_{\mathbf{x}}\mathfrak{L}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\lambda)$ is **unconstrained**, and since $\mathfrak{L}$ is **affine** in $\boldsymbol\lambda$ (Eq. 7.20b), $\mathfrak{D}(\boldsymbol\lambda)$ is a pointwise minimum of affine functions of $\boldsymbol\lambda$ — hence **concave**, even when $f$ and $g_i$ are non-convex. So the outer problem (maximize a concave function) "can be efficiently computed." When $f,g_i$ are differentiable, find the dual by differentiating $\mathfrak{L}$ w.r.t. $\mathbf{x}$, setting to zero, and solving (used in §7.3.1 LP and §7.3.2 QP).
- **Equality constraints → unconstrained multipliers** (Remark, Eq. 7.28, pp. 235–236). Add equality constraints $h_j(\mathbf{x})=0$ (Eq. 7.28). Model each as two inequalities $h_j\le 0$ and $h_j\ge 0$; the resulting multipliers turn out to be **unconstrained**. Hence: constrain the inequality-constraint multipliers to be **non-negative**, leave the equality-constraint multipliers **free** in $\mathbb{R}$. (The four KKT conditions — stationarity, primal feasibility, dual feasibility $\lambda_i\ge0$, and **complementary slackness** $\lambda_i g_i(\mathbf{x}^*)=0$ — are the standard optimality conditions for Eqs. 7.17/7.28; see [[KKTConditions]].)

### 7.3 Convex Optimization (pp. 236–246)

- **Convex optimization = convex objective + convex feasible set ⇒ global optimality and strong duality** (§7.3, p. 236). When $f$ is a **convex function** and the constraints $g,h$ define **convex sets**, the problem is a **convex optimization problem**, and **strong duality** holds: *"the optimal solution of the dual problem is the same as the optimal solution of the primal problem."* (Contrast §7.2, where only weak duality is guaranteed.) See [[ConvexOptimization]], [[Duality|strong duality]].
- **Convex set** (Def. 7.2, Eq. 7.29, p. 236). A set $\mathcal{C}$ is **convex** if for any $x,y\in\mathcal{C}$ and $\theta\in[0,1]$, $\theta x+(1-\theta)y\in\mathcal{C}$ — "the straight line connecting any two elements of the set lies inside the set" (convex Fig. 7.5; nonconvex Fig. 7.6). See [[ConvexSet]].
- **Convex function** (Def. 7.3, Eq. 7.30, pp. 236–237). For $f:\mathbb{R}^D\to\mathbb{R}$ with a convex domain, $f$ is **convex** if for all $\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}$ in the domain and $\theta\in[0,1]$: $f(\theta\mathbf{x}+(1-\theta)\mathbf{y})\leq\theta f(\mathbf{x})+(1-\theta)f(\mathbf{y})$ — "a straight line between any two points of the function lie above the function" (the chord lies above the graph). A **concave function** is the negative of a convex function (Remark). The **epigraph** (the "filled-in" set above a convex function, made by "pouring water into" the bowl) is a convex set — the bridge between convex functions and convex sets. See [[ConvexFunction]].
- **First- and second-order convexity tests** (Eq. 7.31, p. 237). If $f$ is differentiable, $f$ is convex iff $f(\mathbf{y})\geq f(\mathbf{x})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x})^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})$ for all $\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}$ (the tangent lies below the graph). If twice differentiable, $f$ is convex iff its **[[Hessian]]** $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}^2 f(\mathbf{x})$ is **positive semidefinite** everywhere (Boyd & Vandenberghe 2004). Example 7.3 (p. 237) verifies both definitions on the **negative entropy** $f(x)=x\log_2 x$ (convex for $x>0$).
- **Operations preserve convexity (closure)** (Example 7.4, pp. 238). A **nonnegative weighted sum of convex functions is convex**: if $f$ convex and $\alpha\ge0$ then $\alpha f$ convex; if $f_1,f_2$ convex then $f_1+f_2$ convex (sum the two defining inequalities, Eqs. 7.34–7.37); hence $\alpha f_1+\beta f_2$ convex for $\alpha,\beta\ge0$ — extends to many terms. "This is again the idea of closure that we introduced in Chapter 2 for vector spaces."
- **Jensen's inequality** (Remark, p. 239). The convex-function inequality (Eq. 7.30) is "sometimes called **Jensen's inequality**"; more broadly the whole class of inequalities for nonnegative weighted sums of convex functions is called Jensen's inequality. See [[JensensInequality]].
- **Definition of a convex optimization problem** (Eq. 7.38, p. 239). $\min_{\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x})$ subject to $g_i(\mathbf{x})\le 0$ and $h_j(\mathbf{x})=0$, where all $f,g_i$ are convex functions and all $h_j(\mathbf{x})=0$ define convex sets. Two widely used, well-understood sub-classes follow: linear programs (§7.3.1) and quadratic programs (§7.3.2).
- **7.3.1 Linear programming** (§7.3.1, Eqs. 7.39–7.43, pp. 239–240). When objective and constraints are all linear: $\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d}\mathbf{c}^\top\mathbf{x}$ subject to $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\le\mathbf{b}$ (Eq. 7.39), $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}$, $\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^m$ — a **linear program** with $d$ variables and $m$ constraints. "Linear programs are one of the most widely used approaches in industry" (margin, p. 239). The Lagrangian is $\mathfrak{L}=\mathbf{c}^\top\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol\lambda^\top(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b})$ (Eq. 7.40) $=(\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{A}^\top\boldsymbol\lambda)^\top\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\lambda^\top\mathbf{b}$ (Eq. 7.41); $\partial_{\mathbf{x}}\mathfrak{L}=\mathbf{0}\Rightarrow\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{A}^\top\boldsymbol\lambda=\mathbf{0}$ (Eq. 7.42); dual function $\mathfrak{D}(\boldsymbol\lambda)=-\boldsymbol\lambda^\top\mathbf{b}$, giving the **dual LP** $\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^m}-\mathbf{b}^\top\boldsymbol\lambda$ s.t. $\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{A}^\top\boldsymbol\lambda=\mathbf{0}$, $\boldsymbol\lambda\ge\mathbf{0}$ (Eq. 7.43) — also a linear program, but with $m$ variables. "It is convention to minimize the primal and maximize the dual" (margin, p. 239). Choose primal or dual depending on whether $m$ or $d$ is larger (p. 240). Worked Example 7.5 (p. 240, Fig. 7.9): a 2-variable LP whose optimum lies at a vertex of the feasible polygon (star). See [[LinearProgramming]].
- **7.3.2 Quadratic programming** (§7.3.2, Eqs. 7.45–7.52, pp. 241–242). A convex **quadratic objective** with affine constraints: $\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d}\tfrac12\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}^\top\mathbf{x}$ subject to $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\le\mathbf{b}$ (Eq. 7.45), with $\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ symmetric **positive definite** (⇒ convex objective) — a **quadratic program** with $d$ variables and $m$ linear constraints. The Lagrangian $\mathfrak{L}=\tfrac12\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}^\top\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol\lambda^\top(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b})$ (Eq. 7.48a) gives, on $\partial_{\mathbf{x}}\mathfrak{L}=\mathbf{0}$, $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}+(\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{A}^\top\boldsymbol\lambda)=\mathbf{0}$ (Eq. 7.49) so $\mathbf{x}=-\mathbf{Q}^{-1}(\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{A}^\top\boldsymbol\lambda)$ (Eq. 7.50, using that $\mathbf{Q}$ is invertible). Back-substitution yields the **dual QP** $\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^m}-\tfrac12(\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{A}^\top\boldsymbol\lambda)^\top\mathbf{Q}^{-1}(\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{A}^\top\boldsymbol\lambda)-\boldsymbol\lambda^\top\mathbf{b}$ s.t. $\boldsymbol\lambda\ge\mathbf{0}$ (Eqs. 7.51–7.52). "We will see an application of quadratic programming in machine learning in Chapter 12" ([[SupportVectorMachine|SVM]]). Worked Example 7.6 (p. 241, Fig. 7.4). See [[QuadraticProgramming]].
- **7.3.3 Legendre–Fenchel transform / convex conjugate** (§7.3.3, Eqs. 7.53–7.68, pp. 242–246). Duality *without constraints*: a convex set can be equivalently described by its **supporting hyperplanes** (a hyperplane touching the set on one side); since a convex function's epigraph is a convex set, a **convex function can be equivalently described by a function of its gradient/tangents**. The **Legendre transform** formalizes this; in physics it relates the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian (margin). The general form is the **convex conjugate** (Def. 7.4, Eq. 7.53): $f^*(\mathbf{s})=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^D}\big(\langle\mathbf{s},\mathbf{x}\rangle-f(\mathbf{x})\big)$ — it transforms $f(\cdot)$ into a function of the **tangents/slopes** $\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x})$; it does **not** require $f$ to be convex or differentiable (Hiriart-Urruty & Lemaréchal 2001). For the smooth 1-D case $f(x)=x^2$: a supporting line $y=sx+c$ "just touches" $f$ at the smallest $c$; deriving $f^*(s)=sx_0-f(x_0)$ (Eq. 7.58) recovers Def. 7.4 without the supremum — for convex differentiable $f$ there is a **one-to-one correspondence between $f$ and its Legendre transform** (Remark, Eqs. 7.54–7.58, pp. 242–243). Applying the conjugate twice recovers the original ("the slope of $f(x)$ is $s$, the slope of $f^*(s)$ is $x$"). Worked Example 7.7 (Eqs. 7.59–7.62): the quadratic $f(\mathbf{y})=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{y}$ has conjugate $f^*(\boldsymbol\alpha)=\tfrac{1}{2\lambda}\boldsymbol\alpha^\top\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol\alpha$. Example 7.8 (Eqs. 7.63a–d): the conjugate of a **sum of per-example losses** $\mathfrak{L}(\mathbf{t})=\sum_i\ell_i(t_i)$ separates termwise into $\mathfrak{L}^*(\mathbf{z})=\sum_i\ell_i^*(z_i)$ — "for convex loss functions that apply independently to each example, the conjugate loss is a convenient way to derive a dual problem" (p. 246). See [[ConvexConjugate]] / [[LegendreFenchelTransform]].
- **Two routes to a dual coincide for convex problems** (pp. 245–246, Example 7.9). §7.2 derives a dual via Lagrange multipliers; §7.3.3 derives one via the Legendre–Fenchel transform. For convex problems with **strong duality**, primal and dual solutions match, and when $f$ is convex and differentiable the supremum is unique. Example 7.9 proves $\min_{\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})+g(\mathbf{x})=\max_{\mathbf{u}}-f^*(\mathbf{u})-g^*(-\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{u})$ (Eq. 7.68) by introducing a Lagrange multiplier $\mathbf{u}$ for $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}$ and recognizing the convex conjugates (Eqs. 7.64–7.67); for general inner products $\mathbf{A}^\top$ is replaced by the adjoint $\mathbf{A}^*$ (margin).

### 7.4 Further Reading (pp. 246–248)

- **Gradient descent's two weaknesses → two literatures** (§7.4, p. 246). (1) It is **first-order** (no curvature info), so it crawls down long valleys; remedies include **acceleration/momentum** generalized by Nesterov (2018), **conjugate gradient** (Shewchuk 1994), **second-order Newton** methods (use the Hessian), and **quasi-Newton L-BFGS** (Nocedal & Wright 2006) that approximate the Hessian cheaply; recent metric-based directions include **mirror descent** (Beck & Teboulle 2003) and **natural gradient** (Toussaint 2012). Many step-size and momentum ideas arise from curvature considerations (Goh 2017; Bottou et al. 2018). (2) **Non-differentiable functions** (kinks) break gradient methods; **subgradient methods** (Shor 1985) and the books by Bertsekas (1999), Luenberger (1969), Bonnans et al. (2006), and the survey Bubeck (2015) address this. Modern dataset sizes make batch GD infeasible, so **SGD is the workhorse** (surveys: Hazan 2015; Bottou et al. 2018). For duality/convex optimization: Boyd & Vandenberghe (2004), Bertsekas (2009), Nesterov (2018); convex analysis foundations Rockafellar (1970), Hiriart-Urruty & Lemaréchal (2001), Borwein & Lewis (2006); Legendre–Fenchel transforms beginner-friendly in Zia et al. (2009), surveyed in optimization by Polyak (2016). (Margin: Hugo Gonçalves' blog as an easier Legendre–Fenchel intro.)

## Key Quotes

> "For convex functions all local minimums are global minimum." — §7, margin note, p. 227
>
> The single most important statement in the chapter — the entire motivation for §7.3 and for designing ML objectives (e.g. the [[SupportVectorMachine|SVM]], Ch 12) to be convex.

> "Since we consider data and models in $\mathbb{R}^D$, the optimization problems we face are continuous optimization problems, as opposed to combinatorial optimization problems for discrete variables." — §7, margin, p. 225
>
> Defines the scope: continuous (this chapter) vs combinatorial (out of scope).

> "If the step-size is chosen too large, gradient descent can overshoot, fail to converge, or even diverge." — §7.1.1, p. 229. "The step-size is also called the learning rate." — margin, p. 229
>
> The step-size/learning-rate identity and the consequence of getting it wrong.

> "The key insight about why taking a subset of data is sensible is to realize that for gradient descent to converge, we only require that the gradient is an unbiased estimate of the true gradient." — §7.1.3, p. 232
>
> The theoretical license for SGD / mini-batching — any unbiased subsample of the gradient suffices.

> "Duality in optimization is the idea of converting an optimization problem in one set of variables $\mathbf{x}$ (called the primal variables), into another optimization problem in a different set of variables $\boldsymbol\lambda$ (called the dual variables)." — §7.2, p. 234
>
> The chapter's clean definition of duality; it then gives two concrete dualities — Lagrangian (§7.2) and Legendre–Fenchel (§7.3.3).

> "$\max_{\mathbf{y}}\min_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq\min_{\mathbf{x}}\max_{\mathbf{y}}\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})$ … This is also known as weak duality." — §7.2, Eqs. 7.23, 7.27, pp. 234–235
>
> The minimax inequality and its consequence: primal values are always ≥ dual values.

> "When $f(\cdot)$ is a convex function, and when the constraints involving $g(\cdot)$ and $h(\cdot)$ are convex sets, this is called a convex optimization problem. In this setting, we have strong duality: The optimal solution of the dual problem is the same as the optimal solution of the primal problem." — §7.3, p. 236
>
> The defining property of convex optimization and the strong-duality payoff that distinguishes it from the general (weak-duality-only) case of §7.2.

> "A set $\mathcal{C}$ is a convex set if for any $x,y\in\mathcal{C}$ and for any scalar $\theta$ with $0\le\theta\le1$, we have $\theta x+(1-\theta)y\in\mathcal{C}$." — Def. 7.2, Eq. 7.29, p. 236

> "The function $f$ is a convex function if for all $\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}$ in the domain of $f$ … $f(\theta\mathbf{x}+(1-\theta)\mathbf{y})\leq\theta f(\mathbf{x})+(1-\theta)f(\mathbf{y})$." — Def. 7.3, Eq. 7.30, p. 236. "The inequality in (7.30) is sometimes called Jensen's inequality." — Remark, p. 239

> "The convex conjugate of a function $f:\mathbb{R}^D\to\mathbb{R}$ is a function $f^*$ defined by $f^*(\mathbf{s})=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^D}(\langle\mathbf{s},\mathbf{x}\rangle-f(\mathbf{x}))$." — Def. 7.4, Eq. 7.53, p. 242
>
> The Legendre–Fenchel transform / convex conjugate — duality without constraints, expressing $f$ in terms of its tangent slopes.

> "For convex optimization problems we have strong duality, that is the solutions of the primal and dual problem match. The Legendre-Fenchel transform described here also can be used to derive a dual optimization problem." — §7.3.3, p. 245
>
> Ties the two duality routes (Lagrangian, Legendre–Fenchel) together under strong duality.

## Contradictions

- **Notational quirk — Lagrangian symbol.** This chapter writes the Lagrangian as a fraktur $\mathfrak{L}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\lambda)$ and the dual function as $\mathfrak{D}(\boldsymbol\lambda)$, with multiplier vector $\boldsymbol\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^m$ for inequalities only. The earlier [[LagrangeMultipliers]] wiki page (and Ch 12's SVM) use $\mathcal{L}$ and split multipliers into $\lambda_i\ge0$ (inequality) plus $\nu_j\in\mathbb{R}$ (equality). Same content; §7.2 derives the equality case via two inequalities (Remark, p. 235–236), reaching the same "non-negative for inequalities, free for equalities" rule. No mathematical contradiction.
- **Notational quirk — row-vector gradients.** Per the [[mml-ch05-vector-calculus|Ch 5]] convention, gradients are **row vectors**, so the GD update (Eqs. 7.5/7.6) and momentum (Eqs. 7.11/7.12) carry an explicit transpose $((\nabla f)(\mathbf{x}_i))^\top$. Deep-learning texts (and the [[GradientDescent]]/[[StochasticGradientDescent]] pages, which follow [[d2l-optimization|D2L]]) write the gradient as a column vector with no transpose. Same update, different convention — flagged on the umbrella [[mml-book]] page too.
- **Step-size = learning rate.** §7.1.1 uses "step-size" $\gamma$ as primary; the margin notes it "is also called the learning rate." The wiki's [[LearningRate]] page (from [[d2l-linear-regression]]) uses $\eta$. Same scalar, different name/symbol; cross-referenced on both pages.
- **Indexing of the running cubic minima.** The text says the global minimum is "around $x=-4.5$" with value "approximately $-47$" and a local minimum "around $x=0.7$" (p. 225–227); these are visual estimates, not the exact roots of the cubic gradient (which Abel–Ruffini guarantees have no general closed form for higher degrees, though this specific cubic does). No contradiction — the book is explicit they are eyeballed.
