## Summary

Chapter 21 of *Introduction to Information Retrieval* (Manning, Raghavan, and Schütze, 2008) develops the theory and practice of **link analysis** for web search, treating the hyperlink graph as a first-class ranking signal that complements traditional text-based scoring. The chapter establishes two foundational intuitions: (1) the **anchor text** pointing to a page is a good description of that page, and (2) a hyperlink from page A to page B generally represents an endorsement of B by A's author. From these intuitions it develops two algorithmic pillars that have shaped modern web search: **PageRank**, a query-independent global ranking computed as the stationary distribution of a Markov chain on the web graph, and **HITS** (Hyperlink-Induced Topic Search), a query-dependent algorithm that computes mutually reinforcing **hubs** and **authorities** on a per-query subgraph. The chapter formalizes both algorithms in linear-algebraic terms — PageRank as the principal left eigenvector of a teleportation-augmented transition matrix, HITS as principal eigenvectors of $A^T A$ and $A A^T$ — and discusses topic-specific (personalized) PageRank, ergodicity conditions, power-iteration convergence, and vulnerabilities such as link farms and anchor-text spam.

## Key Claims

- Hyperlinks encode useful ranking signal: most links represent implicit endorsements, although internal/template links (navigation, copyright footers) should be discounted.
- **Anchor text** terms should be indexed under the *target* page, not the source, because pages frequently fail to describe themselves (e.g., IBM's homepage historically didn't contain the word "computer"; Yahoo's didn't contain "portal").
- **PageRank** models a *random surfer* who at each step either follows a random outlink (probability $1-\alpha$) or **teleports** to a uniformly random page (probability $\alpha$, typically ~0.1).
- The PageRank vector $\pi$ is the **stationary distribution** of the resulting Markov chain, satisfying $\pi = \pi P$, i.e., the principal left eigenvector of $P$ with eigenvalue 1.
- Teleportation makes the chain **ergodic** (irreducible + aperiodic), guaranteeing a unique stationary distribution that is reached from any starting state — this also fixes the dangling-node problem.
- **Power iteration** $\pi_{t+1} = \pi_t P$ converges to $\pi$ regardless of initial distribution; iteration stops when successive distributions differ by less than a threshold.
- **Topic-specific PageRank** restricts teleportation to a topical seed set; personalized rankings can be obtained as a linear combination of a small number of precomputed topic vectors.
- **HITS** computes, for each page $v$, a hub score $h(v) = \sum_{u: v \to u} a(u)$ and an authority score $a(v) = \sum_{u: u \to v} h(u)$, normalized at each iteration. These are principal eigenvectors of $A A^T$ (hubs) and $A^T A$ (authorities).
- HITS operates on a **base set** built by expanding a text-retrieved **root set** with one-hop in- and out-neighbors, making it query-dependent in contrast to PageRank's query-independence.
- Both algorithms are vulnerable to manipulation: PageRank to **link farms**, HITS and anchor-text indexing to misleading anchor text and topic drift.

## Section Notes

### 21 — Link analysis (introduction)
Hyperlinks function as authority signals, with a clear analogy to **citation analysis** in scholarly literature (Garfield 1955). However, link spam — orchestrated pages that artificially inflate a target's in-link count — is a critical vulnerability. The chapter develops two ranking approaches: PageRank (global, Markov-chain based, with teleportation) and HITS (subset-based, hub/authority mutual reinforcement). Anchor text and topic-specific variants extend both. Search engines integrate these link-based signals with hundreds of other features (cosine similarity, term proximity, freshness, etc.) via machine-learned ranking.

### 21.1 — The web as a graph
The web is a **directed graph**: nodes are pages, edges are hyperlinks. Two intuitions drive link analysis: (a) anchor text describes the target, (b) a link A → B is an endorsement of B. Important caveat: many links are *internal*, generated by templates (site-wide nav bars, copyright footers) and don't carry endorsement value — practical implementations discount or filter them. The chapter situates this within the broader **bowtie** structure of the web graph (introduced earlier in §19.2.1): a strongly connected core (SCC), an IN component reaching the core, an OUT component reachable from the core, plus tendrils and disconnected components.

### 21.1.1 — Anchor text and the web graph
Anchor text from a link A → B is included in the index *under page B*, not page A. This compensates for the self-description gap: a page may be authoritative on a topic without containing the canonical terms users search for. Anchor terms are typically weighted analogously to in-page term-frequency, but flagged as anchor-derived so machine-learned scoring can treat them distinctly. **Manipulation**: a site can craft self-promoting anchor text to boost its own ranking on chosen queries, or orchestrate derogatory anchor campaigns (a "Google bomb") against third parties. Search engines must detect such patterns.

### 21.2 — PageRank
PageRank assigns each page a numerical score in $[0,1]$ derived purely from link structure (query-independent). The **random surfer** model: at each step the surfer either (i) chooses a uniformly random outlink from the current page, or (ii) **teleports** to a uniformly random page with probability $\alpha$. Teleportation models direct URL navigation and, crucially, eliminates pathologies — pages with no outlinks (**dangling nodes**) and disconnected components. The PageRank of page $v$ is the long-run fraction of time the surfer spends at $v$, i.e., the **stationary probability** $\pi(v)$.

### 21.2.1 — Markov chains
A **Markov chain** is a discrete-time stochastic process over $N$ states governed by an $N \times N$ **transition probability matrix** $P$, where $P_{ij}$ is the probability of moving to state $j$ from state $i$. Each row of $P$ sums to 1 (a **stochastic matrix**), and the **Markov property** holds: the next state depends only on the current state, not the history. By Perron–Frobenius, a stochastic matrix has a **principal left eigenvector** with largest eigenvalue equal to 1. If the chain is **ergodic** (irreducible — every state reachable from every other; aperiodic — gcd of return times is 1), this principal left eigenvector is unique up to scaling and equals the **stationary distribution** $\pi$ to which any initial distribution converges. PageRank's teleportation is the device that *guarantees* ergodicity on the web graph.

The transition matrix is constructed from the web's adjacency matrix $A$ by row-normalization, then mixed with a uniform teleport matrix:
$$P = (1-\alpha)\,\tilde{A} + \alpha \frac{E}{N}$$
where $\tilde{A}$ is the row-normalized adjacency, $E$ is the all-ones matrix, and $E/N$ is the uniform-teleport stochastic matrix.

### 21.2.2 — The PageRank computation
PageRank is computed by **power iteration**:
$$\pi_{t+1} = \pi_t P$$
starting from an arbitrary distribution $\pi_0$ (e.g., uniform $1/N$) and iterating until the change between successive vectors falls below a threshold. Because $P$ is ergodic, convergence is guaranteed and independent of $\pi_0$. The chapter works through a 7-node example showing the steady-state vector emerges after a small number of iterations. A telling observation: nodes with many in-links tend to score high, but a node that is **hard to reach** without teleportation may score lower than its in-degree suggests — PageRank measures *reachability under the random walk*, not raw in-degree. At web scale, the computation exploits the **sparsity** of $\tilde{A}$ (most pages have few outlinks) and reduces to repeated sparse matrix–vector multiplications, plus a uniform teleport term that can be applied in $O(N)$.

### 21.2.3 — Topic-specific PageRank
Instead of uniform teleportation, restrict teleportation to a curated **seed set** of pages within a topic (e.g., a sports portal). The resulting stationary distribution $\pi_s$ ranks pages by topic-conditioned importance. **Personalized PageRank** combines topic vectors linearly: a user with 60% sports / 40% politics interest gets ranking $0.6\,\pi_s + 0.4\,\pi_p$. This avoids recomputing PageRank per user — a small library of topic vectors supports arbitrary user-interest mixtures via cheap linear combination.

### 21.3 — Hubs and authorities (HITS)
Kleinberg's **HITS** algorithm assigns each page two scores reflecting two distinct roles:

- A **hub** points to many good authorities (e.g., a curated link list, a topical directory).
- An **authority** is pointed to by many good hubs (e.g., a definitive resource on a topic).

The scores reinforce each other mutually:
$$h(v) = \sum_{u : v \to u} a(u), \qquad a(v) = \sum_{u : u \to v} h(u).$$
Equivalently, with adjacency matrix $A$ (where $A_{uv}=1$ iff $u \to v$):
$$h = A\,a, \qquad a = A^T\,h \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; h = A A^T h, \quad a = A^T A\, a.$$
So $a$ is the principal eigenvector of $A^T A$ and $h$ is the principal eigenvector of $A A^T$. Vectors are **normalized** (typically $L_2$) after each iteration to prevent blow-up. Iteration converges to the principal eigenvectors of these symmetric positive semi-definite matrices.

### 21.3.1 — Choosing the subset of the web
HITS is query-dependent. Given a query (e.g., *leukemia*):

1. **Root set** — pull all pages containing the query term from a text index.
2. **Base set** — expand the root set with any page that links to or is linked from a root-set page (one hop in both directions, optionally capped).

The base set captures authoritative pages that may not literally contain the query term (a hardware authority page that never uses the word *hardware*), and hubs that index relevant authorities. HITS then runs the hub/authority iteration over only this base subgraph — orders of magnitude smaller than the full web — making per-query computation tractable.

### 21.4 — References and further reading
PageRank is due to **Brin and Page (1998)** and **Page et al. (1998)**; **HITS** is due to **Kleinberg (1999)**. The intellectual lineage runs back to **Garfield (1955)** on citation analysis and **Pinski and Narin (1976)**'s journal influence weight, which the chapter notes is "remarkably similar" to PageRank in formulation. Notable extensions and variants: **Chakrabarti et al. (1998)** and **Bharat and Henzinger (1998)** on improving HITS; **SALSA** (Lempel and Moran, 2000) merging random-walk and HITS ideas; **Haveliwala (2002, 2003)** and **Jeh and Widom (2003)** on topic-specific and personalized PageRank; **Ng et al. (2001b)** on robustness; **Berkhin (2005)** and **Langville and Meyer (2006)** on computational methods. Anchor-text-as-index goes back to **McBryan (1994)**. The chapter closes by warning that all link-analysis methods remain vulnerable to orchestrated **link farms**.

## Algorithms & Formulas

**PageRank — power iteration.** Given web adjacency $A$ (with $A_{ij}=1$ iff page $i$ links to page $j$), row-normalize to get stochastic $\tilde{A}$ (handling dangling rows by replacing zero rows with uniform $1/N$). Form the teleportation-augmented transition matrix
$$P = (1-\alpha)\,\tilde{A} + \alpha \frac{E}{N}, \qquad \alpha \approx 0.1$$
Initialize $\pi_0 = (1/N, \ldots, 1/N)$. Iterate
$$\pi_{t+1} = \pi_t P$$
until $\|\pi_{t+1} - \pi_t\|_1 < \varepsilon$. The fixed point $\pi = \pi P$ is the principal left eigenvector of $P$ with eigenvalue 1, and is the PageRank vector. Convergence and uniqueness follow from ergodicity of $P$, which is guaranteed by the positive teleport term ($\alpha > 0$).

**Topic-specific PageRank.** Replace uniform teleport vector $\mathbf{e}/N$ with a topic-specific stochastic vector $\mathbf{v}_s$ supported on a seed set $S$:
$$P_s = (1-\alpha)\,\tilde{A} + \alpha\,\mathbf{1}\mathbf{v}_s^T$$
giving stationary distribution $\pi_s$. Personalized PageRank for a user with topic mixture $\{w_k\}$ is the linear combination $\pi_{\text{user}} = \sum_k w_k\,\pi_k$.

**HITS — hub/authority mutual reinforcement.** On base-set adjacency $A$, initialize $h^{(0)} = a^{(0)} = \mathbf{1}$. Iterate
$$a^{(t+1)} \leftarrow A^T h^{(t)}, \qquad h^{(t+1)} \leftarrow A\,a^{(t+1)}$$
followed by $L_2$ normalization of each vector. This is the power method applied to $A^T A$ (for $a$) and $A A^T$ (for $h$), converging to their principal eigenvectors. The final $a$ ranks pages by authority; $h$ ranks them by hub quality.

**Eigenvector convergence.** Both PageRank and HITS reduce to power iteration on a structured matrix, recovering the principal eigenvector of $P$, $A^T A$, or $A A^T$ respectively. Rate of convergence depends on the spectral gap (ratio of second-largest to largest eigenvalue); for PageRank with $\alpha \approx 0.15$ the gap is favorable and ~50–100 iterations suffice on web-scale graphs.

## Key Quotes

> "A good hub page is one that points to many good authorities; a good authority page is one that is pointed to by many good hub pages."

> "The anchor text pointing to page B is a good description of page B."

> "Pages visited more often in this walk are more important."

> "A Markov chain is a discrete-time stochastic process: a process that occurs in a series of time-steps in which a random choice is made."

> "Given a query (say leukemia), use a text index to get all pages containing leukemia. Call this the root set of pages. Build the base set of pages, to include the root set as well as any page that either links to a page in the root set, or is linked to by a page in the root set."
