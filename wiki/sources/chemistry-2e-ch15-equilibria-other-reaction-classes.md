## Summary
OpenStax Chemistry 2e Chapter 15 extends the equilibrium framework of Chapters 13–14 to two further reaction classes. §15.1 treats the dissolution/precipitation of slightly soluble ionic compounds via the [[SolubilityProduct|solubility product constant Ksp]], deriving [[MolarSolubility|molar solubility]] from Ksp (and vice versa), using the [[ReactionQuotient|reaction quotient]] Q vs Ksp to predict [[PrecipitationReaction|precipitation]], and covering the [[CommonIonEffect|common-ion effect]] and [[SelectivePrecipitation|selective precipitation]]. §15.2 introduces [[LewisAcidsAndBases|Lewis acids and bases]] (electron-pair acceptor/donor forming a [[CoordinateCovalentBond|coordinate covalent bond]]), [[ComplexIon|complex ions]] with [[Ligand|ligands]], and the [[FormationConstant|formation constant Kf]]. §15.3 combines these into [[CoupledEquilibria|coupled equilibria]] — solubility coupled to acid–base or complex formation — where component K values multiply, dramatically altering solubility (ocean acidification of CaCO₃, dental enamel and fluoride, Al(OH)₃, photographic-film silver-halide chemistry).

## Key Claims
- For a slightly soluble compound MₚXq(s) ⇌ p M^(m+) + q X^(n−), the solubility product is Ksp = [M^(m+)]^p [X^(n−)]^q; pure solids do not appear in the expression.
- Ksp and molar solubility are interconvertible: e.g. CaF₂ molar solubility 2.15 × 10⁻⁴ M gives Ksp = (2.15 × 10⁻⁴)(4.30 × 10⁻⁴)² = 3.98 × 10⁻¹¹; for Ca(OH)₂, Ksp = 4x³ = 1.3 × 10⁻⁶ gives solubility x = 6.9 × 10⁻³ M.
- Precipitation is predicted by Q vs Ksp: Q < Ksp unsaturated (no precipitate), Q = Ksp saturated/equilibrium, Q > Ksp supersaturated (precipitates until Q = Ksp).
- The common-ion effect (Le Châtelier applied to dissolution) lowers solubility: adding a shared ion drives the equilibrium toward the solid because the product [ion₁][ion₂] = Ksp is fixed.
- Selective precipitation separates ions sharing a counter-ion by their differing Ksp; the least soluble compound precipitates first (AgCl before AgBr in a mixed-halide example, by comparing the [Ag⁺] each needs).
- A Lewis acid is an electron-pair acceptor and a Lewis base an electron-pair donor; their product is an acid–base adduct joined by a coordinate covalent bond. Brønsted-Lowry reactions are the special case where the Lewis acid is H⁺.
- A complex ion has a central metal cation bonded to ligands (Lewis bases such as H₂O, NH₃, CN⁻, OH⁻); its stability is the formation constant Kf, and Kd = 1/Kf for dissociation.
- For the Ag(NH₃)₂⁺ complex (Kf = 1.7 × 10⁷), a 0.10 M solution dissociates only ~1.1% ([Ag⁺] = 1.1 × 10⁻³ M), illustrating that large Kf means the complex strongly persists.
- Coupled equilibria share a species; adding the equations multiplies their constants (Koverall = K₁ × K₂; reversing inverts; Ksp/Ka2 = 180 for CaCO₃ in acid). Coupling dissolution to acid–base or complex formation can raise solubility by many orders of magnitude.
- CaCO₃ solubility rises sharply in acidic (CO₂-acidified) water; hydroxyapatite dissolves under bacterial acid (fluoride substitution into fluorapatite resists this); Al(OH)₃ solubility in a pH-4.74 acetate buffer rises ~10⁷× over pure water (5 × 10⁻⁹ M → 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ M); photographic fixing dissolves AgBr with thiosulfate (net K = Ksp × Kf = 24).

## Key Quotes
> "Only gases and solutes are represented in equilibrium constant expressions, so the Ksp does not include a term for the undissolved AgCl." — §15.1
> "acids and bases are identified by their ability to accept or to donate a pair of electrons and form a coordinate covalent bond." — §15.2 (G. N. Lewis, 1923)
> "Coupled equilibria involve two or more separate chemical reactions that share one or more reactants or products." — §15.3
> "The equilibrium constant for this net reaction is much greater than the Ksp for calcium carbonate, indicating its solubility is markedly increased in acidic solutions." — §15.3
> "Coupling complex formation with dissolution drastically increases the solubility of Al(OH)₃." — §15.3
