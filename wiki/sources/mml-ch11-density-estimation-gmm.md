## Summary

Chapter 11 (book pp. 348–369) of [[mml-book|Mathematics for Machine Learning]] is the **third of the four ML pillars** — **density estimation** — following regression ([[mml-ch09-linear-regression|Ch 9]]) and dimensionality reduction ([[mml-ch10-dimensionality-reduction-pca|Ch 10]]). It introduces the [[GaussianMixtureModel|Gaussian mixture model (GMM)]] as a density $p(\mathbf x)=\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)$ — a **convex combination of $K$ Gaussian components** that buys multimodality (and thus the ability to model "clustered" data like Fig. 11.1) at the cost of a log-likelihood with **no closed-form maximum**. The chapter's pedagogical spine is: write the GMM density (§11.1) → write the i.i.d. log-likelihood and show why the log cannot enter the sum over components (§11.2) → introduce **[[Responsibility|responsibilities]]** $r_{nk}$ as soft cluster assignments and derive the three responsibility-weighted update equations for means, covariances, and mixture weights (§11.2.1–11.2.4) → assemble these into the alternating **[[EMAlgorithm|EM algorithm]]** with its monotone log-likelihood increase (§11.3) → re-derive the whole apparatus from a discrete **[[LatentVariable|latent-variable]]** perspective with a one-hot indicator $\mathbf z$, giving responsibilities a principled reading as posterior probabilities (§11.4) → close with $K$-means as the hard-assignment special case, histograms, and [[KernelDensityEstimation|kernel density estimation]] (§11.5). It is the chapter that **introduces EM and the latent-variable view of density estimation**, the conceptual peer to the continuous-latent PPCA of [[mml-ch10-dimensionality-reduction-pca|Ch 10]].

## Key Claims

### §11.1 — The GMM density (book pp. 348–350)
- **Density estimation = representing data compactly via a density from a parametric family** (p. 348). A single [[GaussianDistribution|Gaussian]] (found by MLE/MAP, Ch 8.3) has "limited modeling capabilities" — Fig. 11.1's clearly bimodal 2-D sample would be poorly approximated by one Gaussian, motivating a more expressive family.
- **A [[MixtureModel|mixture model]] is a convex combination of $K$ base distributions** $p(\mathbf x)=\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k p_k(\mathbf x)$ (Eq. 11.1) with **[[MixtureWeight|mixture weights]]** constrained by $0\le\pi_k\le 1,\ \sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k=1$ (Eq. 11.2). Components $p_k$ come from a basic family (Gaussians, Bernoullis, Gammas, …). Mixtures "are more expressive than the corresponding base distributions because they allow for multimodal data representations" — they model datasets with multiple "clusters" (§11.1, p. 349).
- **The GMM** specializes the components to Gaussians (Eq. 11.3): $p(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)$, with **all parameters collected as $\boldsymbol\theta:=\{\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k,\pi_k:k=1,\dots,K\}$** ([[MixtureComponent|component]] means, covariances, and weights). Recovers a single Gaussian at $K=1$ (p. 349). Fig. 11.2's running example: $p(x\mid\boldsymbol\theta)=0.5\,\mathcal N(x\mid-2,\tfrac12)+0.2\,\mathcal N(x\mid1,2)+0.3\,\mathcal N(x\mid4,1)$ (Eq. 11.5).

### §11.2 — Parameter learning via maximum likelihood (book pp. 350–360)
- **The training objective is the i.i.d. log-likelihood** (Eqs. 11.9–11.10): $\log p(\mathcal X\mid\boldsymbol\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^N\log\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k\mathcal N(\mathbf x_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)=:\mathcal L$, where each per-point likelihood $p(\mathbf x_n\mid\boldsymbol\theta)$ is itself a Gaussian-mixture density.
- **MLE has no closed form** (§11.2, p. 351–352). Unlike linear regression (§9.2) or PCA (Ch 10), setting $\mathrm d\mathcal L/\mathrm d\boldsymbol\theta=\mathbf 0$ yields a **set of dependent, simultaneous equations** that can only be solved iteratively. The blocking reason: the $\log$ sits **outside** the sum over $k$ and **cannot move inside** it (p. 351–352). The contrast is sharp: for a single Gaussian the sum over $k$ vanishes, the log applies directly to the Gaussian (Eq. 11.11: $\log\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma)=-\tfrac D2\log(2\pi)-\tfrac12\log\det\boldsymbol\Sigma-\tfrac12(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)$), and closed-form MLE follows.
- **Necessary optimality conditions** (Eqs. 11.12–11.14): each of $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\boldsymbol\mu_k$, $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\boldsymbol\Sigma_k$, $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\pi_k$ must vanish. Via the chain rule (§5.2.2) all three need $\partial\log p(\mathbf x_n\mid\boldsymbol\theta)/\partial\boldsymbol\theta=\tfrac{1}{p(\mathbf x_n\mid\boldsymbol\theta)}\,\partial p(\mathbf x_n\mid\boldsymbol\theta)/\partial\boldsymbol\theta$ (Eq. 11.15), where $1/p(\mathbf x_n\mid\boldsymbol\theta)=1/\sum_{j=1}^K\pi_j\mathcal N(\mathbf x_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j,\boldsymbol\Sigma_j)$ (Eq. 11.16) — the recurring denominator that becomes the responsibility normalizer.
- **[[Responsibility|Responsibilities]]** (§11.2.1, Eq. 11.17): $r_{nk}:=\dfrac{\pi_k\mathcal N(\mathbf x_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^K\pi_j\mathcal N(\mathbf x_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j,\boldsymbol\Sigma_j)}$ — "the responsibility of the $k$th mixture component for the $n$th data point." $r_{nk}$ is **proportional to the likelihood $p(\mathbf x_n\mid\pi_k,\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)=\pi_k\mathcal N(\mathbf x_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)$** (Eq. 11.18) of the component given the data point: components with high likelihood for a point take high responsibility for it. The vector $\mathbf r_n:=[r_{n1},\dots,r_{nK}]^\top\in\mathbb R^K$ is a **normalized probability vector** ($\sum_k r_{nk}=1$, $r_{nk}\ge0$) — a **"soft assignment"** of $\mathbf x_n$ to the $K$ components (margin note: $\mathbf r_n$ follows a Boltzmann/Gibbs distribution). **$N_k:=\sum_{n=1}^N r_{nk}$** (Eq. 11.24) is the **total responsibility** / effective (fractional) count of points assigned to component $k$; $N=\sum_k N_k$.
- **Mean update** (Theorem 11.1, Eq. 11.20): $\boldsymbol\mu_k^{\text{new}}=\dfrac{\sum_{n=1}^N r_{nk}\mathbf x_n}{\sum_{n=1}^N r_{nk}}=\tfrac1{N_k}\sum_n r_{nk}\mathbf x_n$ — an **importance-weighted Monte Carlo estimate of the mean**, with the responsibilities $r_{nk}$ as importance weights (p. 354). Derived (Eqs. 11.21a–11.23) by differentiating, exploiting that only the $k$th component depends on $\boldsymbol\mu_k$, and solving $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\boldsymbol\mu_k=\mathbf 0$. Interpretation: $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ is pulled toward data points for which component $k$ has high responsibility (Fig. 11.4–11.5); also expressible as the expected value $\boldsymbol\mu_k\leftarrow\mathbb E_{\mathbf r_k}[\mathcal X]$ under the normalized weight vector $\mathbf r_k=[r_{1k},\dots,r_{Nk}]^\top/N_k$ (Eqs. 11.25–11.26).
- **Covariance update** (Theorem 11.2, Eq. 11.30): $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{\text{new}}=\tfrac1{N_k}\sum_{n=1}^N r_{nk}(\mathbf x_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf x_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top$ — an importance-weighted expected value of the squared centred data (p. 357). Derived (Eqs. 11.31–11.38) using the matrix-calculus identities $\partial\det(\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)^{-1/2}/\partial\boldsymbol\Sigma_k=-\tfrac12\det(\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)^{-1/2}\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}$ (Eq. 11.33, from §5 Eq. 5.101) and the inverse-quadratic-form derivative (Eq. 11.34, Eq. 5.103).
- **Mixture-weight update** (Theorem 11.3, Eq. 11.42): $\pi_k^{\text{new}}=\dfrac{N_k}{N}$ — the **ratio of the total responsibility of cluster $k$ to the number of data points**; i.e., $\pi_k$ is the relative importance of component $k$ for the dataset. Derived (Eqs. 11.43–11.49) by adding a **Lagrange multiplier** $\lambda(\sum_k\pi_k-1)$ (§7.2) to enforce $\sum_k\pi_k=1$; the optimality system gives $\pi_k=-N_k/\lambda$ and $\lambda=-N$, hence $\pi_k=N_k/N$.
- **All three updates depend on all parameters through the responsibilities** $r_{nk}=r_{nk}(\pi_j,\boldsymbol\mu_j,\boldsymbol\Sigma_j;\,j=1,\dots,K)$ — which is precisely why **no closed-form joint solution exists** (Remarks after Thms 11.1–11.3): the "closed-form" updates are only closed-form *given* the responsibilities, and the responsibilities are themselves functions of the parameters being updated.
- **Running example** (Examples 11.1–11.6): a 1-D dataset $\mathcal X=\{-3,-2.5,-1,0,2,4,5\}$ ($N=7$), $K=3$, initialized $p_1=\mathcal N(-4,1)$, $p_2=\mathcal N(0,0.2)$, $p_3=\mathcal N(8,3)$, $\pi_k=\tfrac13$. One full update cycle moves means $-4\to-2.7$, $0\to-0.4$, $8\to3.7$ (Eqs. 11.27–11.29); variances $1\to0.14$, $0.2\to0.44$, $3\to1.53$ (Eqs. 11.39–11.41); weights $\tfrac13\to0.29,0.29,0.42$ (Eqs. 11.50–11.52) — and raises the **log-likelihood from $-28.3$ (init) to $-14.4$ after one cycle** (Example 11.5, p. 360).

### §11.3 — The EM algorithm (book pp. 360–362)
- **The [[EMAlgorithm|expectation maximization (EM) algorithm]]** (proposed by **Dempster et al. 1977**) is "a general iterative scheme for learning parameters (maximum likelihood or MAP) in mixture models and, more generally, latent-variable models" (p. 360–361). Because the updates (11.20/11.30/11.42) are not a closed-form joint solution — responsibilities couple them — the updates *suggest* an alternating iterative scheme.
- **The two steps**, alternated until convergence (p. 361):
  - **E-step**: evaluate the responsibilities $r_{nk}$ (the posterior probability of point $n$ belonging to component $k$) using the current $\pi_k,\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k$ (Eq. 11.53, identical to Eq. 11.17).
  - **M-step**: use the updated responsibilities to re-estimate $\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k,\pi_k$ (Eqs. 11.54–11.56, identical to 11.20/11.30/11.42), and — importantly — the **newly updated means $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ (11.54) are used in the covariance update (11.55)** (margin note, p. 361).
- **Monotone increase**: "Every step in the EM algorithm increases the log-likelihood function (Neal and Hinton, 1999)" (p. 361); convergence is checked on the log-likelihood or the parameters. On the running example EM converges in **five iterations** to $p(x)=0.29\,\mathcal N(-2.75,0.06)+0.28\,\mathcal N(-0.50,0.25)+0.43\,\mathcal N(3.64,1.63)$ (Eq. 11.57); on the Fig. 11.1 2-D data with $K=3$ it converges in **62 iterations** (Figs. 11.8–11.10).

### §11.4 — The latent-variable perspective (book pp. 363–367)
- **A GMM is a discrete [[LatentVariable|latent-variable]] model**: the latent $\mathbf z$ attains a **finite** set of values — in contrast to PCA, where the latents were continuous-valued in $\mathbb R^M$ (p. 363). The probabilistic perspective (i) justifies the "ad hoc" decisions of §11.2, (ii) gives the responsibilities a concrete reading as posterior probabilities, and (iii) lets EM be derived in a principled way (p. 363).
- **Generative process / one-hot indicator** (§11.4.1): introduce a **binary indicator $z_k\in\{0,1\}$** flagging whether component $k$ generated $\mathbf x$, with $p(\mathbf x\mid z_k=1)=\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)$ (Eq. 11.58). Collect $\mathbf z:=[z_1,\dots,z_K]^\top\in\mathbb R^K$: a probability vector of $K-1$ zeros and exactly one 1 — a **one-hot encoding (1-of-$K$ representation)** with $\sum_{k=1}^K z_k=1$ (p. 364; this distribution is sometimes called "multinoulli", a multi-valued Bernoulli generalization, Murphy 2012).
- **Prior on the latent / mixture weights as a prior** (Eqs. 11.59–11.60): place $p(\mathbf z)=\boldsymbol\pi=[\pi_1,\dots,\pi_K]^\top$, $\sum_k\pi_k=1$; then **$\pi_k=p(z_k=1)$** — the mixture weight *is* the prior probability that component $k$ generated a data point.
- **Ancestral sampling** (Remark, p. 364): a GMM generates data by (1) sampling $z^{(i)}\sim p(\mathbf z)$ to pick a component, then (2) sampling $\mathbf x^{(i)}\sim p(\mathbf x\mid z^{(i)}=1)$; discarding the latents leaves valid GMM samples. Graphical model in Fig. 11.11.
- **Joint, then marginal** (§11.4.2): the joint $p(\mathbf x,z_k=1)=\pi_k\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)$ (Eq. 11.61). **Marginalizing out $\mathbf z$** (summing over its $K$ one-hot configurations) recovers exactly the GMM density: $p(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\theta)=\sum_{\mathbf z}p(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf z)p(\mathbf z\mid\boldsymbol\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)$ (Eqs. 11.63–11.66b). "The latent-variable model with latent indicators $z_k$ is an equivalent way of thinking about a Gaussian mixture model" (p. 366). This is the §8.4.3 *marginalize-out-the-latent* move (the same move as PPCA's continuous integral in [[mml-ch10-dimensionality-reduction-pca|Ch 10]], but here a finite sum).
- **Posterior = responsibility** (§11.4.3, Eqs. 11.68–11.69): by **Bayes' theorem**, $p(z_k=1\mid\mathbf x)=\dfrac{p(z_k=1)p(\mathbf x\mid z_k=1)}{p(\mathbf x)}=\dfrac{\pi_k\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^K\pi_j\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j,\boldsymbol\Sigma_j)}$, "which we identify as the responsibility of the $k$th mixture component for data point $\mathbf x$." This gives $r_{nk}$ a **mathematically justified interpretation as a posterior probability**, not merely an intuitive soft assignment.
- **Full dataset** (§11.4.4, Eqs. 11.70–11.72b): each $\mathbf x_n$ gets its own latent $\mathbf z_n=[z_{n1},\dots,z_{nK}]^\top$ sharing one prior $\boldsymbol\pi$ (plate notation, Fig. 11.12); the conditional factorizes $p(\mathbf x_1,\dots,\mathbf x_N\mid\mathbf z_1,\dots,\mathbf z_N)=\prod_n p(\mathbf x_n\mid\mathbf z_n)$; and $p(z_{nk}=1\mid\mathbf x_n)=r_{nk}$ exactly.
- **EM revisited** (§11.4.5): the E-step computes the **expected complete-data log-likelihood** $Q(\boldsymbol\theta\mid\boldsymbol\theta^{(t)})=\mathbb E_{\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\theta^{(t)}}[\log p(\mathbf x,\mathbf z\mid\boldsymbol\theta)]=\int\log p(\mathbf x,\mathbf z\mid\boldsymbol\theta)\,p(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\theta^{(t)})\,\mathrm d\mathbf z$ (Eqs. 11.73a–b), with the expectation taken under the **latent posterior at the current parameters**; the M-step picks $\boldsymbol\theta^{(t+1)}$ maximizing $Q$. **An EM iteration increases the log-likelihood, but there is no guarantee of reaching the global maximum** — EM converges to a **local optimum**, and **different initializations across multiple runs reduce the risk of a bad local optimum** (p. 367).

### §11.5 — Further reading (book pp. 368–369)
- **GMM as generative model**: ancestral sampling generates new data; Fig. 11.1 was itself generated this way (Bishop 2006).
- **Choosing $K$**: assumed known throughout; in practice select via **nested cross-validation** (§8.6.1).
- **Relationship to $K$-means** (p. 368): GMMs are "closely related to the $K$-means clustering algorithm," which also uses EM. **Treating the means as cluster centers and ignoring the covariances (or setting them to $\mathbf I$) gives $K$-means.** MacKay (2003): **$K$-means makes a "hard" assignment of points to cluster centers, whereas a GMM makes a "soft" assignment via the responsibilities** (the canonical hard-vs-soft framing).
- **EM is general**: applies to nonlinear state-space models (Ghahramani & Roweis 1999), reinforcement learning (Barber 2012), etc.; the latent-variable view is what lets EM be derived principledly (Bishop 2006; Barber 2012; Murphy 2012).
- **Standard MLE criticisms apply** (p. 368): (i) **overfitting / singularities** — a component's mean collapsing onto a single data point with covariance $\to\mathbf 0$ sends the likelihood to $+\infty$ (Bishop 2006; Barber 2012); (ii) MLE gives only a **point estimate** with no parameter uncertainty — a **Bayesian** treatment would place a prior on the parameters to get a posterior and the model evidence (for choosing $K$), but **no conjugate prior exists** for this model, so closed-form inference is impossible and one resorts to approximations such as **variational inference** (Bishop 2006).
- **Other density-estimation techniques** (§11.5, p. 369): **[[Histogram|histograms]]** (Pearson 1895) — a nonparametric, "binning"-based step-function estimate whose bin size is a critical hyperparameter (cross-validation, §8.2.4, picks it); **[[KernelDensityEstimation|kernel density estimation]]** (Rosenblatt 1956; Parzen 1962) — $p(\mathbf x)=\tfrac1{Nh}\sum_{n=1}^N k\!\big(\tfrac{\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n}{h}\big)$ (Eq. 11.74), placing a nonnegative kernel $k$ (integrating to 1; uniform or Gaussian) on every data point with bandwidth $h>0$ playing the histogram-bin-size role, yielding a *smooth* estimate (Fig. 11.13).

## Contradictions / notational quirks

- **No mathematical contradictions with existing wiki content** — Ch 11 *confirms and completes* the GMM/EM/Responsibility coverage that the [[GaussianMixtureModel]], [[EMAlgorithm]], and [[Responsibility]] pages had already attributed to "[[mml-book]] Ch 11" (those pages predate this per-chapter ingest; this ingest canonicalizes the citation to `mml-ch11-density-estimation-gmm` and adds verbatim, page-anchored detail). All additions are additive.
- **ELBO/free-energy not used by name.** The task's "ELBO/free-energy view if present" is *not* present as such: §11.4.5 frames EM via the **expected complete-data log-likelihood $Q(\boldsymbol\theta\mid\boldsymbol\theta^{(t)})$** (Eqs. 11.73a–b) and asserts (citing Neal & Hinton 1999) that each iteration increases the log-likelihood — but it does **not** derive the variational lower bound / evidence lower bound (ELBO) or the free-energy decomposition. The wiki's existing [[EMAlgorithm]] page describes EM as "optimizes a lower bound on the log-likelihood (the ELBO)"; that framing is *correct and standard* (Bishop 2006), but it is a **slight over-attribution to this chapter**, which stops at the $Q$-function and the monotone-increase claim. Noted, not corrected (the page's ELBO statement is true in general; this chapter just doesn't prove it).
- **"Soft assignment" $\ne$ probability of cluster membership in frequentist sense.** §11.2.1 introduces $r_{nk}$ first as a "soft assignment" (intuition) and only later (§11.4.3) justifies it as the genuine posterior $p(z_k=1\mid\mathbf x_n)$ — the chapter is explicit that the intuitive reading precedes the mathematical justification.
- **$\mathbf z$ as a real-valued vector.** §11.4.1 writes $\mathbf z\in\mathbb R^K$ (one-hot) even though its entries are binary $\{0,1\}$ — a notational convenience so that $\boldsymbol\pi=p(\mathbf z)$ and the joint can be written with linear algebra; the integral $\int\,\mathrm d\mathbf z$ in Eq. 11.73b is really a finite sum over the $K$ one-hot configurations (consistent with the $\sum_{\mathbf z}$ of Eq. 11.63).
