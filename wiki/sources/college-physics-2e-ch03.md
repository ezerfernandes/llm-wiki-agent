## Summary
Chapter 3 of OpenStax College Physics 2e extends one-dimensional kinematics to motion in a plane. Its unifying idea is the independence of perpendicular motions: any 2D motion can be split into independent horizontal and vertical components, analyzed separately, then recombined. The chapter develops graphical and analytical vector methods, applies them to projectile motion, and closes with the addition of velocities (relative motion across reference frames).

## Key Claims
- Two-dimensional motion decomposes into independent perpendicular (horizontal/vertical) components that do not affect one another.
- The straight-line resultant of perpendicular displacements follows the Pythagorean theorem: c = √(a² + b²); total displacement is generally shorter than the path walked.
- Vectors add commutatively (A + B = B + A); subtraction is addition of the negative (A − B = A + (−B)); scaling by a negative scalar reverses direction.
- The head-to-tail (graphical) method gives the resultant from the first tail to the last head; analytical (trigonometric) methods are more precise.
- A vector resolves into components Ax = A cos θ and Ay = A sin θ; the resultant is A = √(Ax² + Ay²) at angle θ = tan⁻¹(Ay/Ax).
- Components on the same axis add like scalars, but vector magnitudes do not add directly (3 + 4 → 5, not 7).
- Projectile motion assumes only gravity acts: ax = 0 (constant horizontal velocity) and ay = −g = −9.80 m/s².
- Maximum range on level ground occurs at a 45° launch angle; complementary angles give equal ranges but different heights.
- Range on level ground is R = v0² sin(2θ0) / g; maximum height is h = v0y² / (2g).
- Velocity is frame-dependent; relative velocities combine by vector addition, valid classically below ~1% of light speed.

## Key Quotes
> Perpendicular components of two-dimensional motion are independent: motion along one axis does not alter motion along the perpendicular axis. — independence-of-motion principle (3.1, 3.4)

> A projectile experiences zero horizontal acceleration and a constant downward gravitational acceleration, so its path is a parabola. — projectile motion (3.4)

> A velocity has no meaning until a reference frame is specified; observers in different frames record different velocities for the same event. — classical relativity (3.5)
