## Summary
Chapter 8 of OpenStax College Physics 2e introduces linear momentum as a vector quantity (mass times velocity) and develops its dynamics. It restates Newton's second law in momentum form, defines impulse via the impulse-momentum theorem, and establishes conservation of momentum for isolated systems. The chapter then classifies collisions as elastic (internal kinetic energy conserved) or inelastic (not conserved, with perfectly inelastic as the sticking-together extreme), extends analysis to two-dimensional point-mass scattering, and closes with variable-mass rocket propulsion.

## Key Claims
- Linear momentum is p = mv, a vector along the velocity, measured in kg·m/s.
- Newton's second law is most generally F_net = Δp/Δt; it reduces to F = ma only for constant mass.
- Impulse equals net force times time and equals the change in momentum; the same Δp can come from a large brief force or a small sustained one.
- Extending contact time reduces peak force — the basis for airbags, crumple zones, and rolling on landing.
- In an isolated system (zero net external force) total momentum is conserved; this follows from Newton's third law making internal momentum changes cancel.
- Momentum conservation can apply per-axis: a projectile conserves horizontal momentum but not vertical.
- Elastic collisions conserve both momentum and internal kinetic energy; perfectly elastic collisions are essentially only realized at the subatomic scale.
- Inelastic collisions conserve momentum but not kinetic energy; perfectly inelastic ones (objects stick) give the maximum kinetic-energy loss, with v' = m₁v₁/(m₁+m₂) for a stationary target.
- In 2D, momentum conserves independently along perpendicular axes; equal-mass elastic collisions scatter the bodies 90° apart.
- Scattering experiments use momentum conservation to deduce properties of unseen targets (basis of Rutherford-style and particle-physics discoveries).
- Rocket acceleration is a = (v_e/m)(Δm/Δt) − g; final single-stage speed is v = v_e·ln(m₀/m_r).
- Multi-stage rockets are needed because a single stage would require ~98.9% of its mass to be fuel to reach escape velocity.

## Key Quotes
> Momentum measures an object's tendency to keep moving, scaling with both how heavy it is and how fast it goes. — paraphrase, 8.1
> A given change in momentum can be produced either by a strong force acting briefly or a gentle force acting for a long time. — paraphrase, 8.2
> Because internal collision forces are equal and opposite, the momentum gained by one body is exactly lost by the other, leaving total momentum unchanged. — paraphrase, 8.3
> A perfectly inelastic collision, in which the bodies stick together, loses the most kinetic energy still permitted by momentum conservation. — paraphrase, 8.5
