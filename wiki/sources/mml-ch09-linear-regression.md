# MML Ch 9 — Linear Regression

> Deep dive into Chapter 9 (book pp. 289–316) of *Mathematics for Machine Learning* (Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, Cambridge UP 2020; draft rev. 2024-01-15). Part of the [[mml-book]] umbrella. This is the **first of the four ML pillars** the book builds on its six mathematical foundations — the regression pillar, and the worked example that fuses Ch 2 (linear algebra), Ch 3 (analytic geometry / [[OrthogonalProjection|projection]]), Ch 5 ([[VectorCalculus|gradients]]), Ch 6 ([[GaussianDistribution|Gaussians]] / [[BayesTheorem|Bayes]]), Ch 7 ([[ContinuousOptimization|optimization]]), and Ch 8 ([[EmpiricalRiskMinimization|ERM]] / [[MaximumLikelihoodEstimation|MLE]] / [[MAPEstimation|MAP]] / [[BayesianInference|Bayesian inference]]).

## Summary

Chapter 9 develops **linear regression** — curve fitting under a probabilistic noise model — as the canonical supervised-learning pillar of the book. It adopts a *likelihood* view from the outset: observations are noise-corrupted function values $y = f(\mathbf{x}) + \epsilon$ with i.i.d. zero-mean Gaussian noise $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$, giving the likelihood $p(y\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathcal{N}(y\mid\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\theta,\sigma^2)$. The crucial conceptual move is that "linear regression" means **linear *in the parameters* $\boldsymbol\theta$, not in the inputs $\mathbf{x}$** — so an arbitrary nonlinear [[FeatureMap|feature map]] $\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x})$ (e.g. polynomial monomials) keeps the model linear and closed-form-solvable. The chapter climbs the inferential ladder: (1) **[[MaximumLikelihoodEstimation|MLE]]** = least squares, solved by the **[[NormalEquations|normal equations]]** $\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{ML}}=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$; (2) the **[[Overfitting|overfitting]]** of high-degree polynomial MLE motivates (3) **[[MAPEstimation|MAP]]** estimation, which is exactly **[[RidgeRegression|ridge regression]] / regularized least squares**, with the regularizer revealed as a negative-log Gaussian prior; (4) **[[BayesianLinearRegression|Bayesian linear regression]]**, which integrates parameters out entirely — closed-form Gaussian parameter posterior, posterior predictive distribution with calibrated uncertainty, and the [[MarginalLikelihood|marginal likelihood]] for model selection; and finally (5) the **geometric reading** of §9.4 — MLE *is* the [[OrthogonalProjection|orthogonal projection]] of the target vector $\mathbf{y}$ onto the column space of the [[DesignMatrix|design matrix]]. §9.5 points forward to [[GeneralizedLinearModels|GLMs]] (and thence deep nets), [[GaussianProcess|Gaussian processes]], and sparsity-inducing ([[Lasso|LASSO]]) priors.

## Key Claims

### §9.1 Problem Formulation (pp. 289–292)

- **Regression = curve fitting under observation noise.** We seek $f:\mathbb{R}^D\to\mathbb{R}$ given training inputs $\mathbf{x}_n$ and noisy targets $y_n=f(\mathbf{x}_n)+\epsilon$; throughout the chapter $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$ is **fixed zero-mean Gaussian noise** (a margin note flags that the noise *type* could itself be a model choice, but it is held fixed here) (p. 289–290).
- **Five problems a regressor must solve** (p. 290): (i) model class & parametrization choice (degree of polynomial → [[ModelSelection|model selection]], §8.6); (ii) finding good parameters (loss + optimizer); (iii) [[Overfitting|overfitting]] & model selection; (iv) the relationship between **loss functions and parameter priors** (losses are induced by probabilistic models); (v) **uncertainty modeling** — the smaller the training set, the more important it is to equip predictions with confidence bounds.
- **The [[NoiseModel|likelihood / noise model]]** is $p(y\mid\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{N}(y\mid f(\mathbf{x}),\sigma^2)$ (Eq. 9.1), i.e. $y=f(\mathbf{x})+\epsilon$ (Eq. 9.2). The **linear** special case is $p(y\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathcal{N}(y\mid\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\theta,\sigma^2)\Longleftrightarrow y=\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\theta+\epsilon$ (Eqs. 9.3–9.4); this function class is the set of **straight lines through the origin** (the parametrization is $f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\theta$, no intercept) (p. 291).
- **The likelihood is the pdf of $y$ evaluated at $\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\theta$.** The *only* source of uncertainty is the observation noise; without it the $\mathbf{x}\!\to\!y$ relationship would be deterministic and the likelihood would collapse to a **Dirac delta** (a delta is a Gaussian in the limit $\sigma^2\to 0$) (margin note, p. 291).
- **"Linear in the parameters, not the inputs."** The model (9.3)–(9.4) is linear in *both* $\boldsymbol\theta$ and $\mathbf{x}$, but the chapter's defining claim — repeated in the margin (p. 291) and main text (p. 295) — is that $y=\boldsymbol\phi^\top(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol\theta$ for an arbitrary nonlinear transform $\boldsymbol\phi$ is *still* "linear regression" because **$\boldsymbol\theta$ enters linearly**. A "feature" is a representation $\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x})$ of the inputs.

### §9.2 Parameter Estimation — MLE & MAP (pp. 292–303)

- **The likelihood factorizes over the i.i.d. data** (Eq. 9.5): $p(\mathcal{Y}\mid\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol\theta)=\prod_{n=1}^N\mathcal{N}(y_n\mid\mathbf{x}_n^\top\boldsymbol\theta,\sigma^2)$, because $y_i,y_j$ are conditionally independent given their inputs (graphical model, Fig. 9.3, with $\boldsymbol\theta$ and $\sigma$ as the deterministic/known plate parents of the shaded $y_n$).
- **MLE maximizes the likelihood** (Eq. 9.7): $\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{ML}}\in\arg\max_{\boldsymbol\theta}p(\mathcal{Y}\mid\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol\theta)$. *Remark:* the likelihood is **not a probability distribution in $\boldsymbol\theta$** — it is unnormalized in $\boldsymbol\theta$ (and may not even be integrable), though it *is* a normalized distribution in $\mathbf{y}$ (p. 293).
- **MLE = least squares via the NLL.** Apply the log-transform (avoids numerical underflow over the product of $N$ probabilities such as $10^{-256}$; turns the product into a sum of per-example gradients) and minimize the **negative log-likelihood** (Eq. 9.8). The Gaussian log-likelihood per point is $\log p(y_n\mid\mathbf{x}_n,\boldsymbol\theta)=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(y_n-\mathbf{x}_n^\top\boldsymbol\theta)^2+\text{const}$ (Eq. 9.9), so the loss is
  $$\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol\theta)=\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{n=1}^N(y_n-\mathbf{x}_n^\top\boldsymbol\theta)^2=\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta\|^2\quad(\text{Eqs. 9.10a–b}).$$
  A margin note: **the NLL is also called the *error function***; the squared error is a (squared) *distance* $\|\mathbf{x}\|^2=\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x}$ (recall §3.1).
- **The [[DesignMatrix|design matrix]]** is $\mathbf{X}:=[\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_N]^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$ (each row a training input) and $\mathbf{y}:=[y_1,\dots,y_N]^\top$ (Eq. 9.10b explanation, p. 294).
- **The gradient is a row vector** (the book's deliberate Ch 5 numerator-layout / Jacobian convention): $\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d\boldsymbol\theta}=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}(-\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{X}+\boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D}$ (Eq. 9.11c).
- **The [[NormalEquations|normal equations]].** Setting $\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d\boldsymbol\theta}=\mathbf{0}^\top$ gives the closed form (Eq. 9.12c):
  $$\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{ML}}=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}.$$
  This is a **unique global minimum**: the loss is quadratic in $\boldsymbol\theta$ and the Hessian $\nabla^2_{\boldsymbol\theta}\mathcal{L}=\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}$ is positive definite (so the zero-gradient condition is necessary *and* sufficient) (Remark, p. 294). Equivalently it solves a linear system $\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\theta=\mathbf{b}$ with $\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$, $\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$ (Remark, p. 294). The right-multiply by $(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}$ is valid iff $\text{rk}(\mathbf{X})=D$, which holds (ignoring duplicate points) when $N\geq D$ — i.e. no more parameters than data points (margin note, p. 294).
- **[[MaximumLikelihoodEstimation|MLE]] with [[FeatureMap|features]]** (Eqs. 9.13–9.19). Under $\boldsymbol\phi:\mathbb{R}^D\to\mathbb{R}^K$, the model is $y=\boldsymbol\phi^\top(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol\theta+\epsilon=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\theta_k\phi_k(\mathbf{x})+\epsilon$ (Eq. 9.13). The **feature matrix / design matrix** $\boldsymbol\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}$ has rows $\boldsymbol\phi^\top(\mathbf{x}_n)$ and entries $\Phi_{ij}=\phi_j(\mathbf{x}_i)$ (Eq. 9.16). The NLL is $\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\Phi\boldsymbol\theta\|^2+\text{const}$ (Eq. 9.18), so the MLE is obtained by simply **replacing $\mathbf{X}$ with $\boldsymbol\Phi$** (Eq. 9.19): $\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{ML}}=(\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi)^{-1}\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\mathbf{y}$. Invertibility now requires $\text{rk}(\boldsymbol\Phi)=K$ (Remark, p. 296–297).
- **[[PolynomialRegression|Polynomial regression]]** (Example 9.3, Eqs. 9.14–9.15) is the canonical explicit feature map $\boldsymbol\phi(x)=[1,x,x^2,\dots,x^{K-1}]^\top$ — "lifting" a 1-D input into a $K$-dim monomial feature space lets a single matrix inversion fit a degree-$(K\!-\!1)$ polynomial. Example 9.4 (Eq. 9.17) shows the second-order feature matrix with rows $[1,x_n,x_n^2]$.
- **MLE for the noise variance** (Eqs. 9.20–9.22): differentiating the log-likelihood w.r.t. $\sigma^2$, setting to 0, gives $\sigma^2_{\text{ML}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N(y_n-\boldsymbol\phi^\top(\mathbf{x}_n)\boldsymbol\theta)^2$ — the **empirical mean of squared residuals** between noise-free predictions and noisy observations.
- **§9.2.2 Overfitting in Linear Regression** (pp. 298–300). The **[[RMSE|root mean square error]]** $\sqrt{\frac1N\|\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\Phi\boldsymbol\theta\|^2}$ (Eq. 9.23) is normalized and unit-matched to the targets (unlike the $\sigma^2$-scaled NLL, which is unitless). Sweeping the polynomial degree $M$ (Figs. 9.5–9.6) shows the classic [[Overfitting|overfitting]] curve: low $M$ underfits; $M=N-1=9$ interpolates every point but oscillates wildly; **training error never increases with $M$** but **test error is minimized at $M=4$** then explodes from $M=6$ onward. For $M\geq N$ the system is underdetermined ($\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi$ singular) with infinitely many MLEs.
- **§9.2.3 MAP Estimation** (pp. 300–302). To curb the large parameter magnitudes symptomatic of overfitting (Bishop 2006), place a prior $p(\boldsymbol\theta)$ and **maximize the posterior** $p(\boldsymbol\theta\mid\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\propto p(\mathcal{Y}\mid\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol\theta)p(\boldsymbol\theta)$ (Bayes, Eq. 9.24). The log-posterior $=$ log-likelihood $+$ log-prior $+$ const (Eq. 9.25), so MAP is a **"compromise" between prior and data** (p. 301). With a conjugate Gaussian prior $p(\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},b^2\mathbf{I})$ the negative log-posterior is $\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\Phi\boldsymbol\theta\|^2+\frac{1}{2b^2}\boldsymbol\theta^\top\boldsymbol\theta+\text{const}$ (Eq. 9.28); zeroing its gradient (Eqs. 9.29–9.30) gives
  $$\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\left(\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi+\tfrac{\sigma^2}{b^2}\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\mathbf{y}\quad(\text{Eq. 9.31}).$$
  The **only difference from MLE is the additional $\frac{\sigma^2}{b^2}\mathbf{I}$ term** in the inverse — which makes $\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi+\frac{\sigma^2}{b^2}\mathbf{I}$ strictly positive definite (so the inverse always exists, even when $\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi$ is only PSD), and reflects the regularizer (margin, p. 301). Example 9.6 (Fig. 9.7): MAP keeps degree-6/8 polynomials smoother than MLE but "is not a general solution" to overfitting.
- **§9.2.4 MAP ≡ Regularization (ridge / Tikhonov)** (pp. 302–303). **[[RidgeRegression|Regularized least squares]]** minimizes $\|\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\Phi\boldsymbol\theta\|^2+\lambda\|\boldsymbol\theta\|_2^2$ (Eq. 9.32): a **data-fit / misfit term** (∝ NLL) plus a **regularizer** with strength $\lambda\geq 0$. The regularizer $\lambda\|\boldsymbol\theta\|_2^2$ **is** a negative-log Gaussian prior: with $p(\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},b^2\mathbf{I})$, $-\log p(\boldsymbol\theta)=\frac{1}{2b^2}\|\boldsymbol\theta\|_2^2+\text{const}$ (Eq. 9.33), so $\lambda=\frac{1}{2b^2}$. The RLS solution $\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{RLS}}=(\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\mathbf{y}$ (Eq. 9.34) **equals $\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{MAP}}$ when $\lambda=\frac{\sigma^2}{b^2}$**. Using a general $p$-norm $\|\cdot\|_p$ with smaller $p$ yields sparser solutions; $p=1$ is **[[Lasso|LASSO]]** (Tibshirani 1996), useful for variable selection (Remark, pp. 302–303). This is the chapter's instantiation of MML's load-bearing analogy: **the prior is to MAP what the regularizer is to ERM**.

### §9.3 Bayesian Linear Regression (pp. 303–313)

- **The point-estimate problem.** Both MLE and MAP return a single $\boldsymbol\theta^*$ and can overfit (especially in the small-data regime). **[[BayesianLinearRegression|Bayesian linear regression]]** does not fit *any* parameters — it retains the **full posterior** over $\boldsymbol\theta$ and **averages predictions over all plausible parameter settings** (pp. 302–303).
- **§9.3.1 Model** (Eqs. 9.35–9.36): prior $p(\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_0,\mathbf{S}_0)$, likelihood $p(y\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathcal{N}(y\mid\boldsymbol\phi^\top(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol\theta,\sigma^2)$. The Gaussian prior turns $\boldsymbol\theta$ into a random variable; full joint $p(y,\boldsymbol\theta\mid\mathbf{x})=p(y\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\theta)p(\boldsymbol\theta)$ (graphical model, Fig. 9.8, with $\mathbf{m}_0,\mathbf{S}_0,\sigma$ as parents of $\boldsymbol\theta\!\to\!y$).
- **§9.3.2 Prior predictions** (Eqs. 9.37–9.40). Marginalizing $\boldsymbol\theta$ out *before seeing data* gives the **prior predictive** $p(y_*\mid\mathbf{x}_*)=\int p(y_*\mid\mathbf{x}_*,\boldsymbol\theta)p(\boldsymbol\theta)\,d\boldsymbol\theta=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol\theta}[p(y_*\mid\mathbf{x}_*,\boldsymbol\theta)]$ (Eq. 9.37) — needs only $\mathbf{x}_*$, no training data. By conjugacy it is Gaussian (Eq. 9.38): $p(y_*\mid\mathbf{x}_*)=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\phi^\top(\mathbf{x}_*)\mathbf{m}_0,\ \boldsymbol\phi^\top(\mathbf{x}_*)\mathbf{S}_0\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}_*)+\sigma^2)$, the variance splitting into **parameter uncertainty** $\boldsymbol\phi^\top\mathbf{S}_0\boldsymbol\phi$ + **measurement noise** $\sigma^2$ (Eq. 9.39). The noise-free function-value predictive (Eq. 9.40) just drops $\sigma^2$. *Remark:* the parameter distribution $p(\boldsymbol\theta)$ **induces a distribution over functions** (Example 9.7 / Fig. 9.9: degree-5 polynomials under $p(\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\frac14\mathbf{I})$).
- **§9.3.3 Posterior Distribution — Theorem 9.1** (Eqs. 9.41–9.50). Bayes gives $p(\boldsymbol\theta\mid\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})=\frac{p(\mathcal{Y}\mid\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol\theta)p(\boldsymbol\theta)}{p(\mathcal{Y}\mid\mathcal{X})}$ (Eq. 9.41), with the **[[MarginalLikelihood|marginal likelihood / evidence]]** $p(\mathcal{Y}\mid\mathcal{X})=\int p(\mathcal{Y}\mid\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol\theta)p(\boldsymbol\theta)\,d\boldsymbol\theta=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol\theta}[p(\mathcal{Y}\mid\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol\theta)]$ normalizing it (Eq. 9.42; margin: "the marginal likelihood is the expected likelihood under the parameter prior"). **Theorem 9.1**: the posterior is closed-form Gaussian
  $$p(\boldsymbol\theta\mid\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\theta\mid\mathbf{m}_N,\mathbf{S}_N),\quad \mathbf{S}_N=(\mathbf{S}_0^{-1}+\sigma^{-2}\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi)^{-1},\quad \mathbf{m}_N=\mathbf{S}_N(\mathbf{S}_0^{-1}\mathbf{m}_0+\sigma^{-2}\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\mathbf{y})$$
  (Eqs. 9.43a–c). The **proof** works in log-space and **completes the squares** (Eqs. 9.44–9.50): the log-prior + log-likelihood is a negative quadratic form in $\boldsymbol\theta$ (Eq. 9.45b), so the posterior is Gaussian; matching the quadratic term gives $\mathbf{S}_N^{-1}=\sigma^{-2}\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi+\mathbf{S}_0^{-1}$ and the linear term gives $\mathbf{m}_N$. A general **completing-the-squares recipe** is boxed (Eqs. 9.51–9.56): $\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-2\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{x}+\text{const}\to(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)$ with $\boldsymbol\Sigma=\mathbf{A}$, $\boldsymbol\mu=\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{a}$. (Margin: since the posterior is $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_N,\mathbf{S}_N)$, $\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\mathbf{m}_N$.)
- **§9.3.4 Posterior Predictions** (Eqs. 9.57–9.59). Replacing the prior with the posterior gives the **[[PosteriorPredictiveDistribution|posterior predictive]]**
  $$p(y_*\mid\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathbf{x}_*)=\int\mathcal{N}(y_*\mid\boldsymbol\phi^\top(\mathbf{x}_*)\boldsymbol\theta,\sigma^2)\,\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\theta\mid\mathbf{m}_N,\mathbf{S}_N)\,d\boldsymbol\theta=\mathcal{N}(y_*\mid\boldsymbol\phi^\top(\mathbf{x}_*)\mathbf{m}_N,\ \boldsymbol\phi^\top(\mathbf{x}_*)\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}_*)+\sigma^2)$$
  (Eq. 9.57c). The **predictive mean $\boldsymbol\phi^\top(\mathbf{x}_*)\mathbf{m}_N$ coincides with the MAP prediction** (margin, p. 308). The $\boldsymbol\phi^\top\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol\phi$ term is posterior parameter uncertainty (depends on training inputs via $\boldsymbol\Phi$), $\sigma^2$ is measurement noise; for noise-free function values (Eqs. 9.58–9.59) the $\sigma^2$ drops out. *Remark:* integrating $\boldsymbol\theta$ out induces a **distribution over functions** with mean function $\mathbf{m}_N^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\cdot)$ and variance $\boldsymbol\phi^\top(\cdot)\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol\phi(\cdot)$ (Figs. 9.10–9.11: as degree $M$ grows the posterior constrains parameters less, and predictive uncertainty balloons especially at the boundaries — "critical … in a decision-making system … e.g. reinforcement learning or robotics," p. 311).
- **§9.3.5 Computing the Marginal Likelihood** (Eqs. 9.60–9.64). For the generative process $\boldsymbol\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_0,\mathbf{S}_0)$, $y_n\mid\mathbf{x}_n,\boldsymbol\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n^\top\boldsymbol\theta,\sigma^2)$, the [[MarginalLikelihood|marginal likelihood]] is itself Gaussian in $\mathbf{y}$ (product of two Gaussians + linear transform are Gaussian, §6.5.2): mean $\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Y}\mid\mathcal{X}]=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{m}_0$ (Eq. 9.62), covariance $\text{Cov}[\mathcal{Y}\mid\mathcal{X}]=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{S}_0\mathbf{X}^\top+\sigma^2\mathbf{I}$ (Eq. 9.63), so $p(\mathcal{Y}\mid\mathcal{X})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}\mathbf{m}_0,\ \mathbf{X}\mathbf{S}_0\mathbf{X}^\top+\sigma^2\mathbf{I})$ (Eq. 9.64). *Remark:* the marginal likelihood ($\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol\theta}[p(\mathcal{Y}\mid\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol\theta)]$ under the **prior**) closely resembles the posterior predictive (the expectation under the **posterior**); the difference is the marginal likelihood predicts the **training** targets $\mathbf{y}$ and averages under the prior, whereas the predictive predicts test targets $y_*$ and averages under the posterior (p. 309).

### §9.4 Maximum Likelihood as Orthogonal Projection (pp. 313–315)

- **The geometric punchline: MLE *is* [[OrthogonalProjection|orthogonal projection]].** For the 1-D origin-line model $y=x\theta+\epsilon$ (Eq. 9.65), the MLE is the scalar $\theta_{\text{ML}}=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}=\frac{\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}$ (Eq. 9.66), and the fitted targets are $\mathbf{X}\theta_{\text{ML}}=\frac{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top}{\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{y}$ (Eq. 9.67) — exactly the **§3.8.1 projection of $\mathbf{y}$ onto the 1-D subspace spanned by $\mathbf{X}$**, with projection matrix $\frac{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top}{\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}}$, $\theta_{\text{ML}}$ the projection coordinate. "Closest" = minimum squared distance of $y_n$ to $x_n\theta$ (Fig. 9.12: the orange projections of the blue observations onto the regression line).
- **General feature case** (Eqs. 9.68–9.71). For $y=\boldsymbol\phi^\top(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol\theta+\epsilon$, $\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{ML}}=(\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi)^{-1}\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\mathbf{y}$ is the **projection of $\mathbf{y}$ onto the $K$-dim subspace of $\mathbb{R}^N$ spanned by the columns of the feature matrix $\boldsymbol\Phi$** (§3.8.2). When the feature functions $\phi_k$ are **orthonormal** ($\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi=\mathbf{I}$), the projection collapses to $\boldsymbol\Phi\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\mathbf{y}=(\sum_{k=1}^K\boldsymbol\phi_k\boldsymbol\phi_k^\top)\mathbf{y}$ (Eq. 9.71) — a sum of independent 1-D projections onto each basis vector, decoupling the features (wavelets, Fourier bases are orthonormal; Gram–Schmidt orthonormalizes a non-orthogonal basis, §3.8.3 / Strang 2003). This is the chapter's **explicit link back to Ch 3** (linear regression as solving $\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta$).

### §9.5 Further Reading (pp. 315–316)

- **[[GeneralizedLinearModels|Generalized linear models (GLMs)]]** generalize linear regression to non-Gaussian likelihoods (Bernoulli → [[LogisticRegression|logistic regression]] for classification; Binomial/Poisson for counts) via a smooth invertible **activation / transfer function** $\sigma(\cdot)$: $y=\sigma(f(\mathbf{x}))$ with $f(\mathbf{x})=\boldsymbol\theta^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x})$ (Eq.-context); $\sigma$'s inverse is the **canonical link function**. The logistic sigmoid $\sigma(f)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-f)}\in[0,1]$ is the Bernoulli-probability output. **GLMs are the building blocks of (deep) feedforward neural networks**: a GLM $\mathbf{y}=\sigma(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})$ *is* a single-layer net, and recursively composing $\mathbf{f}_k(\mathbf{x}_k)=\sigma_k(\mathbf{A}_k\mathbf{x}_k+\mathbf{b}_k)$ (Eq. 9.72) gives a $K$-layer deep network (Bishop 1995; Goodfellow et al. 2016).
- **[[GaussianProcess|Gaussian processes]]** (Rasmussen & Williams 2006) place a distribution **directly over functions** rather than parameters, via the [[KernelTrick|kernel trick]] (Schölkopf & Smola 2002); closely related to Bayesian linear regression and SVR, and equal to a single-hidden-layer Bayesian neural net with infinitely many units (Neal 1996; Williams 1997).
- **Non-Gaussian (sparsity) priors.** When $N\ll D$ (underdetermined), a **Laplace prior** enforces sparsity / **variable selection** — equivalent to L1-regularized regression / **[[Lasso|LASSO]]** (Tibshirani 1996); the Laplace density's sharp peak at zero (discontinuous first derivative) concentrates mass at zero, zeroing irrelevant parameters.

## Key Quotes

> "*Linear regression* refers to models that are 'linear in the parameters,' i.e., models that describe a function by a linear combination of input features. Here, a 'feature' is a representation $\phi(x)$ of the inputs $x$." — pp. 291–295 (margin note + main text)

> "The linear regression model in (9.3)–(9.4) is not only linear in the parameters, but also linear in the inputs $x$. … We will see later that $y=\phi^\top(x)\theta$ for nonlinear transformations $\phi$ is also a linear regression model because 'linear regression' refers to models that are 'linear in the parameters.'" — p. 291

> "The likelihood $p(y\mid x,\theta)$ … is not a probability distribution in $\theta$: It is simply a function of the parameters $\theta$ but does not integrate to 1 (i.e., it is unnormalized), and may not even be integrable with respect to $\theta$. However, the likelihood in (9.7) is a normalized probability distribution in $y$." — Remark, p. 293

> "We could right-multiply the first equation by $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ because $X^\top X$ is positive definite if $\text{rk}(X)=D$." — p. 294 (the existence condition for the normal-equations solution)

> "Setting the gradient to $0^\top$ is a necessary and sufficient condition, and we obtain a global minimum since the Hessian $\nabla^2_\theta\mathcal{L}(\theta)=X^\top X\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}$ is positive definite." — Remark, p. 294

> "Comparing the MAP estimate in (9.31) with the maximum likelihood estimate in (9.19), we see that the only difference between both solutions is the additional term $\frac{\sigma^2}{b^2}I$ in the inverse matrix. This term ensures that $\Phi^\top\Phi+\frac{\sigma^2}{b^2}I$ is symmetric and strictly positive definite (i.e., its inverse exists …). Moreover, it reflects the impact of the regularizer." — pp. 301–302

> "Bayesian linear regression pushes the idea of the parameter prior a step further and does not even attempt to compute a point estimate of the parameters, but instead the full posterior distribution over the parameters is taken into account when making predictions. This means we do not fit any parameters, but we compute a mean over all plausible parameters settings (according to the posterior)." — p. 303

> "Although for a seventh-order polynomial the MAP estimate yields a reasonable fit, the Bayesian linear regression model additionally tells us that the posterior uncertainty is huge. This information can be critical when we use these predictions in a decision-making system, where bad decisions can have significant consequences (e.g., in reinforcement learning or robotics)." — pp. 310–311

> "Looking carefully at (9.67) we see that the maximum likelihood estimator $\theta_{\text{ML}}$ in our example from (9.65) effectively does an orthogonal projection of $y$ onto the one-dimensional subspace spanned by $X$. … Maximum likelihood linear regression performs an orthogonal projection." — pp. 314 (main text + margin note)

> "The maximum likelihood solution provides also a geometrically optimal solution by finding the vectors in the subspace spanned by $X$ that are 'closest' to the corresponding observations $y$, where 'closest' means the smallest (squared) distance of the function values $y_n$ to $x_n\theta$. This is achieved by orthogonal projections." — p. 314

> "Generalized linear models are the building blocks of deep neural networks." — margin note, p. 315

## Contradictions / notational quirks

- **No contradictions** with existing wiki content; Ch 9 *confirms and operationalizes* the Ch 8 framing already recorded on [[MaximumLikelihoodEstimation]], [[MAPEstimation]], [[Regularization]], [[Overfitting]], [[MarginalLikelihood]].
- **Bias-free convention.** MML's §9.1 linear model $f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\theta$ (Eqs. 9.3–9.4) has **no explicit intercept** — the function class is straight lines *through the origin*. An intercept is recovered only by the §8.1 bias-augmentation trick (prepend a constant feature $x^{(0)}\equiv 1$, or use $\phi_0(x)=1$ as in the polynomial map Eq. 9.14). This differs from the [[d2l-linear-regression|D2L]]/[[islr-seventh-printing|ISLR]] convention of an explicit bias $b$ in $\hat y=\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}+b$. Reinforces the note already on [[DesignMatrix]].
- **Row-vector gradient.** $\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d\boldsymbol\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D}$ (Eq. 9.11c) and the optimality condition $\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d\boldsymbol\theta}=\mathbf{0}^\top$ follow MML's deliberate Ch 5 numerator-layout convention — gradients are *rows*, not columns. The transpose in $\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{ML}}^\top$ through Eqs. 9.12a–b (resolved to $\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{ML}}$ in 9.12c) is a consequence.
- **$\lambda$ vs $b^2$.** Two regularization parametrizations coexist: MAP uses prior variance $b^2$ giving inverse-term $\frac{\sigma^2}{b^2}\mathbf{I}$ (Eq. 9.31); regularized least squares uses $\lambda$ giving $\lambda\mathbf{I}$ (Eq. 9.34); they coincide at $\lambda=\frac{\sigma^2}{b^2}$ (and the RLS↔neg-log-prior identity holds at $\lambda=\frac{1}{2b^2}$ in Eq. 9.33 — the factor-of-2 / $\sigma^2$ bookkeeping differs between the loss-scaling conventions of (9.32) and (9.28)). Worth flagging for anyone comparing $\lambda$ definitions across the wiki's [[RidgeRegression]] (ISLR uses $\|\beta\|_2^2$ with no $\frac12$) and [[Regularization]] pages.
