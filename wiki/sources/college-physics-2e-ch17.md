## Summary
Chapter 17 of OpenStax *College Physics 2e* treats sound as a longitudinal pressure wave and develops its propagation, energy, perception, and applications. It covers the speed of sound and its dependence on medium and temperature, sound intensity and the logarithmic decibel scale, the Doppler effect and sonic booms, interference and resonance of standing waves in air columns, the biology of hearing (pitch, loudness, timbre), and ultrasound for medical imaging and therapy. The recurring theme is the distinction between sound as a physical disturbance and hearing as its biological interpretation.

## Key Claims
- Sound is a longitudinal wave of compressions and rarefactions propagating through matter; fluids carry primarily longitudinal sound because of negligible shear strength. ([[Sound]])
- The universal wave relation `v_w = f·λ` governs sound; in air, speed depends on absolute temperature as `v_w = 331·√(T/273)` m/s, and is essentially independent of frequency across the audible band. ([[SpeedOfSound]])
- Sound speed rises with medium rigidity and falls with density, so it is fastest in stiff solids and slowest in compressible gases like air. ([[SpeedOfSound]])
- Intensity is power per unit area (`I = P/A`) and relates to pressure amplitude by `I = (Δp)²/(2ρv_w)`. ([[SoundIntensity]])
- Loudness perception is logarithmic; sound level in decibels is `β = 10·log₁₀(I/I₀)` with `I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m²`, so each ×10 in intensity adds 10 dB and doubling intensity adds ~3 dB. ([[Decibel]])
- The Doppler effect shifts observed frequency up when source and observer approach and down when they recede, with distinct formulas for moving source vs. moving observer. ([[DopplerEffect]])
- When an object exceeds the speed of sound, waves pile up into a cone-shaped shock wave producing a sonic boom; aircraft generate two booms (nose and tail). ([[SonicBoom]])
- Air columns resonate as standing waves: closed-end tubes give odd harmonics `f_n = n·v_w/4L` (n = 1,3,5…); open tubes give all harmonics `f_n = n·v_w/2L`. ([[AcousticResonance]])
- Human hearing spans 20–20,000 Hz over a ~10¹² intensity range; perceived loudness (phons) depends jointly on intensity and frequency, with peak sensitivity at 2,000–5,000 Hz. ([[Loudness]], [[Pitch]])
- Ultrasound (>20 kHz) reflects at acoustic-impedance boundaries (`Z = ρv`, reflection coefficient `a = [(Z₂−Z₁)/(Z₁+Z₂)]²`); similar soft-tissue impedances allow deep penetration for imaging, while Doppler-shifted echoes measure blood-flow velocity. ([[Ultrasound]], [[AcousticImpedance]])

## Key Quotes (paraphrased)
> Intensity is the power per unit area carried by a wave. — definition of sound intensity
> Each tenfold rise in intensity corresponds to a 10-decibel rise in sound level. — logarithmic perception principle
> As a source's speed nears the speed of sound, the observed-frequency denominator approaches zero, so compressions superimpose and form a shock wave. — sonic-boom mechanism
> Sound energy transfers efficiently between media of similar acoustic impedance; large impedance mismatches reflect strongly. — impedance-matching principle in ultrasound
