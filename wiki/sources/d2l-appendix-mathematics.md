## Summary

Brent Werness, Rachel Hu and the [[d2l-preface|D2L]] authors' eleven-section **Mathematics for Deep Learning** appendix — D2L's dedicated math compendium, sitting outside Parts 1–3 and acting as the *math reference shelf* the rest of the book cites into. Covers, in order: geometry of vectors and linear-algebra operations, [[Eigendecomposition|eigendecomposition]], [[SingleVariableCalculus|single-variable]] / [[MultivariableCalculus|multivariable]] / [[IntegralCalculus|integral]] calculus, [[RandomVariable|random variables]] (discrete and continuous), [[MaximumLikelihoodEstimation|maximum likelihood]], common [[ProbabilityDistribution|distributions]] (Bernoulli, Discrete Uniform, Continuous Uniform, [[GaussianDistribution|Normal]], [[ExponentialDistribution|Exponential]], [[PoissonDistribution|Poisson]], [[GaussianMixtureModel|mixture models]] via [[CentralLimitTheorem|CLT]]), [[NaiveBayes|naive Bayes]] applied to [[MNIST]], [[Statistics|statistics]] (estimators / hypothesis testing / confidence intervals), and [[InformationTheory|information theory]] (entropy / KL / mutual information / cross-entropy). The framing thesis (§intro): *"most software developers no longer need to worry about the theory of computable functions, neither should deep learning practitioners need to worry about the theoretical foundations of maximum likelihood learning — but we are not quite there yet."* — the appendix is the mathematical "just-in-case" supplement to the rest of D2L.

## Key Claims

- **Geometry of linear algebra.** Vectors admit two interpretations — as *points* (location relative to origin) and as *directions* (arrows in $\mathbb{R}^n$). The [[DotProduct|dot product]] $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta$ gives the angle between vectors in *any* dimension via $\theta=\arccos(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w}/\|\mathbf{v}\|\|\mathbf{w}\|)$; [[CosineSimilarity|cosine similarity]] is the brightness-invariant similarity metric that exploits this — *"the angle between $\mathbf{v}$ and $0.1\mathbf{v}$ is zero."*
- **Hyperplanes and decision planes.** In $d$ dimensions, the set $\{\mathbf{v}:\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{v}=c\}$ is a $(d-1)$-dimensional [[Hyperplane|hyperplane]] perpendicular to $\mathbf{w}$, dividing the space into two half-spaces. *"The majority of deep learned classification models end with a linear layer fed into a softmax, so one can interpret the role of the deep neural network to be to find a non-linear embedding such that the target classes can be separated cleanly by hyperplanes."* Demonstrated by a hand-built FashionMNIST t-shirt/trousers classifier with decision plane $\mathbf{w}=\bar{\mathbf{x}}_1-\bar{\mathbf{x}}_0$.
- **Matrices as geometry.** A matrix $\mathbf{A}$ acts on a vector by sending each basis element to the corresponding column — it can *skew, rotate, scale* the grid but cannot distort it locally. [[LinearDependence|Linear dependence]] of columns (a non-trivial combination summing to zero) collapses the image to a lower-dimensional subspace; [[Rank|rank]] is the largest number of linearly independent columns; [[Determinant|determinant]] $ad-bc$ (for $2\times2$) is the (signed) volume-scaling factor; $\det(\mathbf{A})=0\iff$ non-invertible $\iff$ rank-deficient.
- **Tensor contractions + Einstein notation.** Generalizing matrix multiplication to arbitrary index patterns ($y_{il}=\sum_{jk}x_{ijkl}a_{jk}$); under [[EinsteinSummation|Einstein notation]] repeated indices are implicitly summed, giving compact expressions that read directly into `np.einsum("ijk,il,j->kl", B, A, v)`. Recovers $\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w}=v_iw_i$, $\|\mathbf{v}\|_2^2=v_iv_i$, $(\mathbf{Av})_i=a_{ij}v_j$, $\text{tr}(\mathbf{A})=a_{ii}$ as one-line tensor expressions.
- **Eigendecomposition.** Vectors satisfying $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}$ are [[Eigenvalues|eigenvectors]] with eigenvalue $\lambda$; found by solving the polynomial $\det(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})=0$. If $\mathbf{A}$ has a full set of linearly independent eigenvectors, $\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{W}^{-1}$ — matrix powers reduce to scalar powers ($\mathbf{A}^n=\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol\Sigma^n\mathbf{W}^{-1}$), $\det(\mathbf{A})=\prod_i\lambda_i$, and *"invertible is the same as having no zero eigenvalues."* [[GershgorinCircleTheorem|Gershgorin's theorem]] localizes eigenvalues to disks of radius $\sum_{j\neq i}|a_{ij}|$ centered at $a_{ii}$; on symmetric matrices the eigenvalues are real.
- **Derivative as local linearization.** *"The behavior of familiar functions can be modeled by a line in a small enough range"* — $f(x+\epsilon)\approx f(x)+\epsilon f'(x)$. The pre-1986 *method of finite differences* used this directly; [[Backpropagation|backpropagation]] (Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams 1986) gives the same gradient at single-prediction cost. Common derivatives ($\frac{d}{dx}x^n=nx^{n-1}$, $\frac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x$, $\frac{d}{dx}\log x=1/x$), sum / product / [[ChainRule|chain rule]] are derived from the linearization identity. [[TaylorSeries|Taylor expansion]] $f(x)=\sum_n\frac{f^{(n)}(x_0)}{n!}(x-x_0)^n$ generalizes to higher-order local approximation.
- **Gradient = steepest direction.** For $L:\mathbb{R}^N\to\mathbb{R}$, the [[Gradient|gradient]] $\nabla L=[\partial L/\partial x_1,\ldots,\partial L/\partial x_N]^\top$ satisfies $L(\mathbf{w}+\boldsymbol\epsilon)\approx L(\mathbf{w})+\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\nabla L(\mathbf{w})$. Decomposing the inner product as $\|\nabla L\|\cos\theta$ shows the most-negative direction is $-\nabla L$ — *"this brings us to one of the most important mathematical concepts in machine learning: the direction of steepest descent points in the direction of $-\nabla L$."* Critical points are zeros of $\nabla L$; the [[Hessian|Hessian]] $\nabla^2 L$ distinguishes minima / maxima / [[SaddlePoint|saddles]] via eigenvalue signs.
- **Multivariate chain rule + matrix calculus.** For nested compositions $L=f(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}))$, the multivariate chain rule is $\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_i}=\sum_j\frac{\partial L}{\partial u_j}\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}$ — recovering backpropagation as the algorithmic application of the chain rule on a [[ComputationalGraph]]. Reverse-mode automatic differentiation is structurally the chain rule applied right-to-left.
- **Integral as area.** $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ is the (signed) area under the curve, approximated by Riemann sums $\sum_i\epsilon\cdot f(x_i)$. [[FundamentalTheoremOfCalculus|Fundamental theorem of calculus]] $\int_a^b F'(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)$ links differentiation and integration; change of variables $\int_a^b f(u(x))u'(x)dx=\int_{u(a)}^{u(b)}f(u)du$ is "the chain rule for integrals"; multivariate integration extends to $\iint f(x,y)\,dxdy$ via Fubini / iterated single-variable integrals.
- **Probability densities.** Continuous random variables are described by a [[ProbabilityDensityFunction|probability density function]] $p(x)$ with $p(x)\geq 0$ and $\int_{-\infty}^\infty p(x)\,dx=1$; the [[CumulativeDistributionFunction|CDF]] $F(x)=P(X\leq x)=\int_{-\infty}^x p(t)\,dt$ satisfies $F'=p$. Multiple RVs need [[JointProbability|joint]] / marginal / conditional densities; [[Independence|independence]] $p(x,y)=p(x)p(y)$ factorizes the joint; [[Covariance|covariance]] $\text{Cov}(X,Y)=E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]$ and [[Correlation|correlation]] $\rho=\text{Cov}/\sigma_X\sigma_Y\in[-1,1]$ measure linear dependence.
- **Maximum likelihood = NLL minimization.** $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}=\arg\max_\theta P(X\mid\boldsymbol\theta)$ becomes $\arg\min_\theta-\log P(X\mid\boldsymbol\theta)$ — products of small probabilities ($10^{-9}$ to $10^{-100}$) underflow FP32, but their logs fit. Under independence, $-\log P=-\sum_i\log p(x_i\mid\boldsymbol\theta)$ — same as [[CrossEntropyLoss|cross-entropy]] / [[EmpiricalRiskMinimization|ERM]] with [[CrossEntropy|surprisal]] loss. **Bayesian interpretation:** with uninformative prior $P(\boldsymbol\theta)\propto 1$ and dropping $P(X)$, the MAP estimator coincides with the MLE. *"The maximum likelihood point of view is one of the most commonly encountered ways of thinking in machine learning."*
- **Asymptotic properties of MLE.** Three classical guarantees: (i) **consistency** — $\hat\theta_n\to\theta^*$ in probability; (ii) **asymptotic normality** — $\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n-\theta^*)\to\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{I}^{-1}(\theta^*))$ where $\mathcal{I}$ is the [[FisherInformation|Fisher information]]; (iii) **efficiency** — the MLE achieves the [[CramerRaoBound|Cramér-Rao lower bound]] asymptotically. Justifies why minimum-NLL is *the* default estimator across statistics, ML, and LLM pretraining.
- **Common distributions.** [[BernoulliDistribution|Bernoulli]] $(\mu=p,\sigma^2=p(1-p))$ — the coin flip; **Discrete Uniform** on $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ $(\mu=(n+1)/2)$; **Continuous Uniform** on $[a,b]$ $(\mu=(a+b)/2, \sigma^2=(b-a)^2/12)$; **Binomial** $(\mu=np,\sigma^2=np(1-p))$ — $n$ Bernoulli sums; **Poisson** $(\mu=\sigma^2=\lambda)$ — rare events with mass $p_k=\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}/k!$; **Gaussian** / Normal $(\mu,\sigma^2)$ — the dominant continuous distribution; **Exponential** $(\mu=1/\lambda,\sigma^2=1/\lambda^2)$ — memoryless waiting times. The [[CentralLimitTheorem|central limit theorem]] is operationalized by showing that $\text{Binomial}(n,p)$ approaches $\mathcal{N}(np,np(1-p))$ as $n\to\infty$ — *"the normal distribution is essentially the limit of any sum of a large number of equal small independent contributions."*
- **Naive Bayes classification.** Apply Bayes' rule $P(y\mid\mathbf{x})\propto P(\mathbf{x}\mid y)P(y)$ with the **naive conditional-independence assumption** $P(\mathbf{x}\mid y)=\prod_i P(x_i\mid y)$ — reduces $\mathcal{O}(2^d)$ joint estimation to $\mathcal{O}(d)$ per-feature counts. Applied to binarized MNIST: estimate $\hat P(y=c)=n_c/n$ and $\hat P(x_i=1\mid y=c)$ via [[LaplaceSmoothing|Laplace-smoothed]] counts; predict by maximizing $\log P(y)+\sum_i\log P(x_i\mid y)$ in [[LogSpace|log-space]] to avoid underflow. Achieves ~84% test accuracy — beating uniform-prior baselines despite the absurd "all 784 pixels independent given digit" assumption.
- **Statistics — estimators, hypothesis testing, confidence intervals.** Estimator $\hat\theta_n=\hat f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ is evaluated by [[MeanSquaredError|MSE]] $E[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2]$ = $\text{bias}^2+\text{variance}$ (the [[BiasVarianceDecomposition|bias-variance decomposition]]); **[[HypothesisTesting|hypothesis tests]]** weigh $H_0$ vs $H_A$ via a test statistic $T$, with type-I error $\alpha=P(\text{reject }H_0\mid H_0)$, type-II error $\beta=P(\text{fail to reject }H_0\mid H_A)$, and **statistical power** $1-\beta$; *p*-value $p=P(T\geq T_{\text{obs}}\mid H_0)$. **[[ConfidenceInterval|Confidence intervals]]** $(\bar X\pm 1.96\sigma/\sqrt{n})$ — random *intervals* containing the (fixed) true parameter $95\%$ of the time under repeated sampling, *not* "the parameter is in this interval with 95% probability."
- **Information theory — Shannon's framework.** Information of an event $X$ with probability $p$ is **[[SelfInformation|self-information]]** $I(X)=-\log_2 p$ bits; **[[Entropy|Shannon entropy]]** $H(X)=-E_{x\sim P}[\log p(x)]=-\sum_i p_i\log p_i$ is the expected self-information — *"the expected number of bits required to encode one realization of $X$ under the optimal code"* (Shannon 1948); maximum entropy on $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ is uniform with $H=\log n$. **Joint entropy** $H(X,Y)$; **conditional entropy** $H(Y\mid X)=H(X,Y)-H(X)$; **[[MutualInformation|mutual information]]** $I(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y)\geq 0$ — the information shared between two RVs.
- **KL divergence, cross-entropy, and the loss connection.** **[[KullbackLeiblerDivergence|KL divergence]]** $D_{KL}(P\|Q)=E_{x\sim P}[\log(p(x)/q(x))]\geq 0$ (Gibbs' inequality), zero iff $P=Q$, *asymmetric* — *"a distance-like measure, but not a metric."* **[[CrossEntropy|Cross-entropy]]** $H(P,Q)=-E_{x\sim P}[\log q(x)]=H(P)+D_{KL}(P\|Q)$ — minimizing cross-entropy w.r.t. $Q$ is equivalent to minimizing KL (since $H(P)$ doesn't depend on $Q$). This is *why* classification with [[CrossEntropyLoss|cross-entropy loss]] is the same as MLE: cross-entropy $=$ NLL $=$ KL up to a $P$-only additive constant. Closes with the **conditional cross-entropy** specialization to classification where $P$ is the one-hot label distribution.

## Key Quotes

> "One of the wonderful parts of modern deep learning is the fact that much of it can be understood and used without a full understanding of the mathematics below it. This is a sign that the field is maturing. Just as most software developers no longer need to worry about the theory of computable functions, neither should deep learning practitioners need to worry about the theoretical foundations of maximum likelihood learning. But, we are not quite there yet." — §intro, framing the appendix as a "just-in-case" mathematical foundation.

> "The most important mathematical concept in machine learning: the direction of steepest descent points in the direction of $-\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}L(\mathbf{w})$." — §multivariable-calculus, deriving gradient descent geometrically from $\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla L=\|\nabla L\|\cos\theta$ minimized at $\theta=\pi$.

> "Matrices are incapable of distorting some parts of space differently than others. All they can do is take the original coordinates on our space and skew, rotate, and scale them." — §geometry-linear-algebraic-ops, the geometric internalization of linear transformations that demystifies the matrix-multiplication operation underneath every deep-learning forward pass.

> "We can rearrange terms to obtain $\theta = \arccos(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w}/\|\mathbf{v}\|\|\mathbf{w}\|)$. This is a nice result since nothing in the computation references two-dimensions. Indeed, we can use this in three or three million dimensions without issue." — §geometry-linear-algebraic-ops, the dimension-agnostic angle formula that makes cosine similarity work on Transformer embeddings.

> "The normal distribution is essentially the limit of any sum of a large number of equal small independent contributions." — §distributions, the textbook gloss on the [[CentralLimitTheorem|central limit theorem]] — the empirical reason Gaussian noise models are everywhere.

> "Everything must follow the rules of information theory, no matter the format. With information theory, we can measure and compare how much information is present in different signals." — §information-theory, framing Shannon entropy / KL / mutual information / cross-entropy as the cross-modal common language under [[BERT]] / [[GPT]] / [[Diffusion]] training losses.
