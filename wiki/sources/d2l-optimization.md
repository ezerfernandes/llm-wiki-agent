## Summary

[[AstonZhang|Zhang]], [[ZacharyLipton|Lipton]], [[MuLi|Li]] & [[AlexanderSmola|Smola]]'s eleven-section *Optimization Algorithms* chapter (`raw/d2l-en/chapter_optimization/`: optimization-intro / convexity / gd / sgd / minibatch-sgd / momentum / adagrad / rmsprop / adadelta / adam / lr-scheduler). The textbook's most theoretical chapter, framed by a deliberate gap: **almost all DL optimization problems are nonconvex, yet the design and analysis of algorithms in the convex setting is profoundly instructive** — convex analysis supplies the only meaningful convergence proofs and the geometric intuitions (steepest descent, condition number, [[Preconditioning|preconditioning]], leaky averaging) that survive into the nonconvex DL regime. The chapter opens by distinguishing *optimization's goal* (minimize training loss) from *DL's goal* (minimize generalization error), then catalogs the three vexing pathologies of DL loss landscapes — local minima, [[SaddlePoint|saddle points]] (which dominate in high dimensions because eigenvalue signs of the Hessian flip independently), and [[VanishingGradient|vanishing gradients]] at activation tails. A primer on [[ConvexOptimization|convexity]] (convex sets, convex functions, [[JensensInequality|Jensen's inequality]], local-min-equals-global-min, Hessian PSD test, Lagrangian/penalty/projection constraint handling) is followed by a step-by-step algorithm tour: vanilla [[GradientDescent|gradient descent]] in 1D via Taylor expansion, the learning-rate dilemma (too small stalls, too large diverges), second-order [[NewtonsMethod|Newton's method]] (one-step convergence on quadratics but disastrous when the Hessian goes negative on nonconvex functions), [[Preconditioning|diagonal preconditioning]] as the cheap Newton proxy, [[LineSearch|line search]] (too expensive for DL); then [[StochasticGradientDescent|SGD]] as the $\mathcal{O}(1)$-per-step replacement for the $\mathcal{O}(n)$ batch gradient with unbiased noisy estimate and $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})$ convergence rate for convex objectives requiring a *vanishing* schedule (piecewise / exponential / polynomial); [[MinibatchSGD|minibatch SGD]] as the vectorization-friendly compromise (variance ↓ as $b^{-1/2}$, hardware-bandwidth ↑); [[Momentum|momentum]] / leaky averaging ($\mathbf{v}_t \leftarrow \beta\mathbf{v}_{t-1}+\mathbf{g}_t$) as both acceleration on ill-conditioned problems (eigenspace-decomposed analysis on convex quadratics) and noise smoothing, with effective sample weight $\frac{1}{1-\beta}$ (e.g. $\beta=0.9$ ⇒ ~10 gradients); [[Adagrad|Adagrad]] ([[JohnDuchi|Duchi]], Hazan & Singer 2011) as per-coordinate preconditioning using accumulated squared gradients $\mathbf{s}_t = \mathbf{s}_{t-1}+\mathbf{g}_t^2$ — particularly effective for sparse features, but with an unbounded $\mathcal{O}(t)$ accumulator that forces $\mathcal{O}(t^{-1/2})$ learning-rate decay (often too aggressive for DL); [[RMSProp|RMSProp]] ([[GeoffreyHinton|Tieleman & Hinton]] 2012) fixes this by replacing the linear accumulator with a leaky-average second-moment estimate $\mathbf{s}_t = \gamma\mathbf{s}_{t-1}+(1-\gamma)\mathbf{g}_t^2$, decoupling the rate schedule from coordinate adaptivity; [[Adadelta|Adadelta]] ([[MatthewZeiler|Zeiler]] 2012) further eliminates the global learning-rate hyperparameter by using a leaky average of *parameter changes* $\Delta\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ as a unit-matching factor; [[Adam|Adam]] ([[DiederikKingma|Kingma]] & [[JimmyBa|Ba]] 2014) combines first-moment momentum with RMSProp-style second-moment scaling and adds **bias correction** $\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t = \mathbf{v}_t/(1-\beta_1^t)$, $\hat{\mathbf{s}}_t = \mathbf{s}_t/(1-\beta_2^t)$ to correct zero-initialization bias, with [[Yogi|Yogi]] (Zaheer, Reddi et al. 2018) as a refinement for divergence under high-variance second moments. The closing [[LearningRateScheduler|learning rate scheduler]] section establishes the three orthogonal axes of rate management — *magnitude*, *decay rate*, *initialization* (warmup) — and walks the canonical policies: factor / multifactor (piecewise constant), polynomial $\eta_0(t+1)^{-\alpha}$ (square-root default), [[CosineLRSchedule|cosine annealing]] (Loshchilov & Hutter 2016) $\eta_T + \frac{\eta_0-\eta_T}{2}(1+\cos(\pi t/T))$, and [[Warmup|linear warmup]] (Gotmare et al. 2018: limits divergence of very deep networks during the early-training random-direction phase). The chapter is the wiki's canonical reference for *every* gradient-based optimizer beneath every neural net in [[d2l-preface|D2L]] and Corpus II.

## Key Claims

- **Optimization and deep learning have fundamentally different goals.** Optimization minimizes the training-set objective (empirical risk); deep learning seeks to minimize the *generalization* error (population risk on unseen data). "Minimizing the training error does *not* guarantee that we find the best set of parameters to minimize the generalization error." The two minima are at different locations — a fact that motivates [[Overfitting|overfitting]] mitigation (weight decay, dropout, early stopping) as orthogonal to optimization-algorithm choice.
- **DL loss landscapes have three structural pathologies: local minima, saddle points, and vanishing gradients.** Almost all DL objectives are *nonconvex*. Local minima trap gradient methods, but they are often "good enough" for prediction. **[[SaddlePoint|Saddle points]] dominate in high dimensions**: at any zero-gradient point in $\mathbb{R}^d$, the Hessian has $d$ eigenvalues; the probability that *all* of them are positive (a true local min) shrinks rapidly with $d$, so saddle points are *exponentially more common* than local minima. The example $f(x,y)=x^2-y^2$ has the saddle at the origin (a min in $x$, a max in $y$). Saddle points and [[VanishingGradient|vanishing gradients]] both create zero-gradient regions where optimization stalls.
- **Convexity is the only setting where local minima are global minima — and DL is not convex.** Proof by contradiction (chapter §convexity): if $f$ is convex on a convex set $\mathcal{X}$ and $x^*$ is a local minimum, then for any candidate $x'$ with $f(x') < f(x^*)$ the convex combination $\lambda x^* + (1-\lambda)x'$ lies arbitrarily close to $x^*$ but has $f < f(x^*)$, contradicting the local-min property. Standard tests for convexity: line-segment inequality on convex sets; Hessian PSD ($\nabla^2 f \succeq 0$); Jensen's inequality $E[f(X)] \geq f(E[X])$. Constraint handling: [[LagrangeMultipliers|Lagrangian]] $L(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}) = f(\mathbf{x})+\sum_i \alpha_i c_i(\mathbf{x})$, the **penalty** approximation (which is exactly what [[WeightDecay|weight decay]] does — $\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$ implements $\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 - r^2 \leq 0$), and **projection** onto the feasible set (which is exactly what [[GradientClipping|gradient clipping]] $\mathbf{g}\leftarrow\mathbf{g}\cdot\min(1, \theta/\|\mathbf{g}\|)$ does — a projection onto the $\ell_2$ ball of radius $\theta$).
- **Gradient descent is derived from a first-order Taylor expansion.** For continuously differentiable $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, $f(x+\epsilon)=f(x)+\epsilon f'(x)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)$; picking $\epsilon=-\eta f'(x)$ yields $f(x-\eta f'(x)) \approx f(x) - \eta (f'(x))^2 \leq f(x)$ as long as the higher-order terms are negligible. This makes precise why **too-large learning rate diverges** (the Taylor remainder dominates) and **too-small stalls** (per-step decrease is negligible). The multivariate generalization replaces $f'(x)$ with $\nabla f(\mathbf{x})$.
- **Newton's method achieves one-step convergence on quadratics — and disaster on nonconvex problems.** Stopping the Taylor expansion at second order yields $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} = -\mathbf{H}^{-1}\nabla f$. For $f(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^2$, the update is exact in one step. But on $f(x) = x\cos(cx)$ the Hessian goes negative, and the update *increases* $f$ — "a fatal flaw." Two fixes: take $|\mathbf{H}|$ instead of $\mathbf{H}$, or reintroduce a learning rate ($\eta < 1$) that "defeats the purpose" but recovers stability. Newton's method is impractical for DL anyway because $\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ for $d\sim 10^9$ is too expensive. **[[Preconditioning|Diagonal preconditioning]]** $\mathbf{x}\leftarrow\mathbf{x}-\eta\,\textrm{diag}(\mathbf{H})^{-1}\nabla f$ is the cheap proxy — and it foreshadows Adagrad / RMSProp / Adam, which use *gradient* magnitudes as a cheap proxy for the diagonal of $\mathbf{H}$.
- **SGD trades variance for cost: each step is $\mathcal{O}(1)$ vs batch GD's $\mathcal{O}(n)$.** For training objective $f(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i f_i(\mathbf{x})$, sample $i\sim\textrm{Unif}\{1,\ldots,n\}$ and update $\mathbf{x}\leftarrow\mathbf{x}-\eta\nabla f_i(\mathbf{x})$. The stochastic gradient is an **unbiased estimate**: $\mathbb{E}_i[\nabla f_i(\mathbf{x})] = \nabla f(\mathbf{x})$. Convergence analysis (Nesterov & Vial 2000) for convex objectives bounds $E[R(\bar{\mathbf{x}})]-R^* \leq (r^2+L^2\sum\eta_t^2)/(2\sum\eta_t)$ where $L$ bounds the gradient norm; with $\eta = r/(L\sqrt{T})$ this yields convergence rate $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})$. The proof requires the schedule $\eta_t\to 0$ but not too quickly. Without replacement (single pass per epoch in random order) is preferred over with replacement — it has lower variance (since with replacement, $P(\textrm{see~}i\textrm{~at~least~once}) \approx 0.63$, $P(\textrm{exactly~once}) \approx 0.37$).
- **Three families of dynamic learning rates: piecewise constant, exponential, polynomial.** Formally $\eta(t) = \eta_i$ on $[t_i, t_{i+1}]$ (piecewise); $\eta_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ (exponential, "leads to premature stopping"); $\eta_0(\beta t+1)^{-\alpha}$ (polynomial, with $\alpha = 0.5$ as a popular default that the SGD convergence proof shows is well-behaved on convex problems). For *nonconvex* problems no meaningful convergence guarantees are available — "minimizing nonlinear nonconvex problems is NP hard."
- **Minibatch SGD is the universal compromise.** Pure SGD is hardware-inefficient (single-observation matrix-vector ops don't saturate GPU bandwidth); full-batch GD is data-redundant. Minibatches of size $b = |\mathcal{B}|$ preserve the **unbiased gradient mean** but reduce variance by $b^{-1/2}$. Standard heuristic: $b\in[32, 256]$, power-of-2 preferred for memory alignment. The chapter benchmarks $b=1500/100/10/1$ on NASA Airfoil and shows $b=100$ outperforms full-batch GD in walltime *and* converges to a smaller risk. Increasing $b$ also reduces the implicit-regularization noise from [[BatchNormalization|batch normalization]].
- **Momentum replaces the instantaneous gradient with a leaky average over past gradients.** Update: $\mathbf{v}_t \leftarrow \beta\mathbf{v}_{t-1} + \mathbf{g}_t$, $\mathbf{x}_t \leftarrow \mathbf{x}_{t-1} - \eta\mathbf{v}_t$. Recursive expansion: $\mathbf{v}_t = \sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\beta^\tau \mathbf{g}_{t-\tau,t-\tau-1}$. The **effective sample weight** is $\sum_{\tau=0}^\infty \beta^\tau = \frac{1}{1-\beta}$ — so $\beta=0.9$ amounts to averaging over ~10 past gradients. On the ill-conditioned ellipsoid $f(\mathbf{x}) = 0.1x_1^2 + 2x_2^2$, plain GD oscillates in $x_2$ and crawls in $x_1$; momentum aligns well-aligned $x_1$ gradients and cancels oscillating $x_2$ gradients, restoring convergence. Proposed by [[BorisPolyak|Polyak]] 1964; comprehensive theoretical treatment in [[YuriiNesterov|Nesterov]] 2018. On convex quadratics, gradient descent with momentum decomposes into coordinate-wise optimization along the eigendirections of $\mathbf{Q}$ — the **larger range of stable parameters** ($0 < \eta\lambda < 2+2\beta$ vs $0 < \eta\lambda < 2$ for plain GD) explains why "large values of $\beta$ are desirable."
- **Adagrad delivers per-coordinate adaptive learning rates — but its accumulator decays the rate too aggressively for DL.** Update: $\mathbf{s}_t = \mathbf{s}_{t-1}+\mathbf{g}_t^2$, $\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{x}_{t-1} - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\mathbf{s}_t+\epsilon}}\mathbf{g}_t$. The squared-gradient accumulator $\mathbf{s}_t$ is a cheap proxy for the *diagonal of the Hessian*: coordinates with consistently large gradients get smaller effective learning rates; sparse / infrequently-activated coordinates retain larger ones. Particularly effective for sparse features (language models, computational advertising). Drawback: $\mathbf{s}_t$ grows linearly with $t$, yielding an $\mathcal{O}(t^{-1/2})$ rate decay — fine for convex problems but typically too aggressive for nonconvex DL. Proposed by [[JohnDuchi|Duchi]], Hazan & Singer 2011.
- **RMSProp = Adagrad with a leaky second-moment accumulator, decoupling rate scheduling from coordinate adaptivity.** Update: $\mathbf{s}_t = \gamma\mathbf{s}_{t-1} + (1-\gamma)\mathbf{g}_t^2$, $\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{x}_{t-1} - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\mathbf{s}_t+\epsilon}}\odot\mathbf{g}_t$. Replacing $\mathbf{s}_{t-1}+\mathbf{g}_t^2$ with $\gamma\mathbf{s}_{t-1}+(1-\gamma)\mathbf{g}_t^2$ gives the accumulator a finite half-life $\gamma^{-1}$ (e.g. $\gamma=0.9$ ⇒ ~10-step memory). The experimenter now controls the global rate $\eta$ independently of the per-coordinate scaling. Proposed by Tieleman & [[GeoffreyHinton|Hinton]] in 2012 lecture-notes form (never formally published as a paper).
- **Adadelta has no global learning rate: it uses a leaky average of past parameter changes as the unit-matching factor.** Two state variables: $\mathbf{s}_t = \rho\mathbf{s}_{t-1}+(1-\rho)\mathbf{g}_t^2$ (second moment of gradient) and $\Delta\mathbf{x}_t = \rho\Delta\mathbf{x}_{t-1}+(1-\rho)(\mathbf{g}'_t)^2$ (second moment of *parameter change*). The rescaled gradient is $\mathbf{g}'_t = \frac{\sqrt{\Delta\mathbf{x}_{t-1}+\epsilon}}{\sqrt{\mathbf{s}_t+\epsilon}}\mathbf{g}_t$; the update is $\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{x}_{t-1}-\mathbf{g}'_t$. With $\rho=0.9$ (half-life 10), Adadelta tends to work without further tuning. Proposed by [[MatthewZeiler|Zeiler]] 2012.
- **Adam unifies momentum + RMSProp + bias correction.** State: $\mathbf{v}_t = \beta_1\mathbf{v}_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)\mathbf{g}_t$ (first-moment leaky average), $\mathbf{s}_t = \beta_2\mathbf{s}_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)\mathbf{g}_t^2$ (second-moment leaky average). With $\mathbf{v}_0=\mathbf{s}_0=0$ both estimates are biased toward zero early in training; **bias correction** $\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t = \mathbf{v}_t/(1-\beta_1^t)$, $\hat{\mathbf{s}}_t = \mathbf{s}_t/(1-\beta_2^t)$ uses the geometric-series identity $\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\beta^i = (1-\beta^t)/(1-\beta)$ to renormalize. Update: $\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{x}_{t-1} - \frac{\eta\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{s}}_t}+\epsilon}$. Default hyperparameters: $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$, $\epsilon=10^{-6}$ — the variance estimate moves much more slowly than the momentum. Proposed by [[DiederikKingma|Kingma]] & [[JimmyBa|Ba]] 2014; one of the most popular DL optimizers. Known failure mode: Reddi, Kale & Kumar 2019 construct convex problems where Adam diverges due to poor variance control; **[[Yogi|Yogi]]** (Zaheer, Reddi et al. 2018) replaces $\mathbf{g}_t^2 - \mathbf{s}_{t-1}$ with $\mathbf{g}_t^2 \odot \textrm{sgn}(\mathbf{g}_t^2 - \mathbf{s}_{t-1})$ so the magnitude of the second-moment update no longer depends on the deviation magnitude.
- **The three axes of learning-rate scheduling are magnitude, decay rate, and initialization (warmup).** *Magnitude* — too large diverges, too small stalls; condition number sets the ratio of fastest-to-slowest direction. *Decay rate* — slower than $\mathcal{O}(t^{-1/2})$ for nonconvex DL. *Warmup* — large LRs early on with random-init parameters point in meaningless directions; linear warmup from 0 to $\eta_0$ over a few epochs prevents divergence, particularly in deep networks (Gotmare, Keskar, Xiong et al. 2018).
- **Canonical schedules: factor / multifactor (piecewise), polynomial, cosine.** **Factor**: $\eta_{t+1} = \max(\eta_\textrm{min}, \alpha\eta_t)$ — multiplicative decay with a floor. **MultiFactor / piecewise-constant**: drop the rate by $\alpha$ at fixed milestones (e.g. $t\in\{15,30\}$) — "essentially this ensures that we converge efficiently to a suitable solution and only then reduce the inherent variance of the parameters by reducing the learning rate." **[[CosineLRSchedule|Cosine annealing]]** (Loshchilov & Hutter 2016, SGDR): $\eta_t = \eta_T + \frac{\eta_0-\eta_T}{2}(1+\cos(\pi t/T))$ over $t\in[0,T]$, pinned to $\eta_T$ thereafter — empirically effective for computer vision and now ubiquitous in [[transformer|Transformer]] pretraining. **[[Warmup|Linear warmup]]** composes with any schedule (typically cosine): increase $\eta$ linearly from 0 to $\eta_0$ over the first $W$ steps, then apply the chosen decay.

## Key Quotes

> "Almost all optimization problems arising in deep learning are *nonconvex*. Nonetheless, the design and analysis of algorithms in the context of *convex* problems have proven to be very instructive." — §intro, the chapter's framing

> "For high-dimensional problems the likelihood that at least *some* of the eigenvalues are negative is quite high. This makes saddle points more likely than local minima." — §optimization-intro, the high-dimensional saddle-point lesson

> "Learning rates matter. Too large and we diverge, too small and we do not make progress. In high dimensions adjusting the learning rate is complicated. Preconditioning can help with scale adjustment." — §gd summary, the GD takeaways

> "Momentum replaces gradients with a leaky average over past gradients. This accelerates convergence significantly. The effective number of gradients is given by $\frac{1}{1-\beta}$ due to exponentiated downweighting of past data." — §momentum summary, the momentum lemma

> "Adam combines features of many optimization algorithms into a fairly robust update rule. Created on the basis of RMSProp, Adam also uses EWMA on the minibatch stochastic gradient. Adam uses bias correction to adjust for a slow startup when estimating momentum and a second moment." — §adam summary, the Adam recipe

> "Decreasing the learning rate during training can lead to improved accuracy and (most perplexingly) reduced overfitting of the model… A warmup period before optimization can prevent divergence." — §lr-scheduler summary, the schedule takeaways

## Contradictions

- None. Reinforces and theoretically grounds the prior wiki coverage: [[d2l-linear-regression]] §3.1.4's "minibatch SGD as the universal DL optimizer" is now backed by the convex-objective convergence proof in §sgd; [[d2l-multilayer-perceptrons]] §5.4's vanishing-gradient phenomenology is now framed as a saddle-point-like pathology in optimization terms; [[mml-book]] §7.1's GD + momentum + SGD trio is extended into the modern Adagrad → RMSProp → Adadelta → Adam progression that MML does not detail. Productive complement to [[Adam]]'s prior one-line stub — now substantively expanded with bias correction, $(\beta_1,\beta_2)$ defaults, and Yogi divergence-fix. Productive tension with the implicit MML / ISLR / D2L-classical view that "$\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt T)$ SGD convergence rates are sufficient" — D2L explicitly notes nonconvex DL has *no* meaningful convergence guarantees, and the chapter ends with the open question: "Optimization serves multiple purposes in deep learning. Besides minimizing the training objective, different choices of optimization algorithms and learning rate scheduling can lead to rather different amounts of generalization and overfitting on the test set."
